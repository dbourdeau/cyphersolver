“Quem novit” to Christian IV, 1627–28

An unsigned German letter to the King of Denmark, with about a hundred words in letter-like signs, was partly deciphered: a traveller kept waiting into a third week for a galley, and 3,000 thalers borrowed.

The letter
One page, 19 lines, to Christian IV of Denmark (“Gnädigster König”), dated “den 20 Iulii anno currentis” with no year and signed “Quem novit” (“whom you know”) in place of a name. About 100 cipher words stand among clear German.
Where
Copenhagen, Rigsarkivet, TKUA, Speciel del, Nederlandene B.41, Arkivalieronline unit 261377, image 443 (right page). It lies in the folder of Johann Schultetus’s reports from The Hague, 1628–29 (catalogue entry no. 2541, which stays in the catalogue), but is not in his hand. Not on DECODE.
The cipher
A simple substitution, word by word: 23 letter-like signs for 21 letters (s and k each have two signs; i stands for i, u and v); one sign, seen once, has no value. Three code numbers (117 twice, 118) stand for names or places.
How it was deciphered
By Malcolm McBratney with AI agents, from the ciphertext of the letter itself, with the crib “Gnädigster König” and word patterns. Three separate transcriptions, two of them blind; a unit counts as read when each sign takes the value that at least two of the three readers give.
What it says
H. has waited into a third week for a galley; the Signoria has assigned a large one and he leaves today. He has borrowed another 3,000 thalers and asks the King to pay them.
Still open
The g/q signs, which the readers dispute and which need a person at full size; two smaller sign classes; the codes 117 and 118; the writer and the year (1627 or 1628), not proved.
Credit
Malcolm McBratney, working with AI agents (issue #450, pull request #483): the key, the three transcriptions and the reading. The leaf was found on 12 November 2026 in a sweep of the Rigsarkivet folder by the AI sessions he directed.

01 The letter

The leaf is the right-hand page of image 443. It has 19 lines in one hand: clear German with the cipher words written in among it, then the date and the signature. The writer calls the addressee “Gnädigster König”; the leaf lies in Rigsarkivet’s volume of papers from the Netherlands, in the folder of Johann Schultetus’s reports from The Hague (catalogue 2541), between papers of September 1628 and Schultetus’s letter written after 24 July 1628. Three code numbers appear: 117 twice and 118 once, and the number 3000 and the date 20 are written in figures.

Who wrote it is proposed, not proved. One article (Bohemiae Occidentalis Historica 2/2019, read here only through a summariser) puts Christian Wilhelm of Brandenburg, Administrator of Magdeburg, at Venice in early July 1627 on his way to lead Danish forces in Silesia; a three-week wait for a galley on 20 July would fit, and the letter would then be of 1627 (old style). That was not tested against any dated source, and the filing order points to 1628 instead. The year stays open, and so does the writer. “Venice” for the code 118 rests on the same guess (the Signoria and the galleys point there).

The same folder holds a second cipher that this page does not treat. Image 447 (right page) is a chancery decipherment sheet headed “Scultetus 17 Novemb:” for the number code of Schultetus’s letter of 17 November 1628 on the left page, so that code has a chancery decipherment; it is a different cipher, not the one of this letter (catalogue 2541 was corrected on both counts).

02 The cipher and its key

Each cipher word is a run of signs for the letters of one German word, and the signs look like letters of the Latin alphabet or Greek-like shapes (a pi, a theta, an omega-like sign, a long s). In the contributor’s labelling, which this page follows, 24 sign labels occur in 557 sign tokens; 23 have a value and stand for 21 plain letters. The key is only partly alphabetical: the plain letters l m n o are the signs a b c d, and the rest shows no order. The full table with shapes and counts is key.tsv; some of it:

Sign (label)PcpqtkHfmL, lS, hi
Plain lettergnadiehrtsku, v

The key was found by hand, from the ciphertext of this letter and a crib. The clear German continues “… lasset E. unverhalten sein”, so the first two cipher words, which stand at the head of a letter to a king, were taken to be Gnädigster König; the rest followed from word patterns. No solver was used. The contributor discloses that the letter values took shape while his first reader transcribed, so “frozen before decoding” means before any scripted decode, not before any guess.

The German fit confirms the key; it is not a blind test of it. The key was fitted to this letter by hand, so the lines 11–19 “held-out” figure the contributor first gave (63 of 68 units) is not held out, and he says so. What the fit does show is that three independent transcriptions of the signs, made by separate sessions, all decode to German under the fixed key.

03 The reading

A reader decoded the letter into German sentences and translated them; the full German and English are in the Full texts below. H. tells the King that he has now waited into a third week, to his vexation and at considerable cost, because he could get no galley; small galleys were there, but nobody, “wie insonderheit [117]”, would advise him to risk them. The Signoria has therefore assigned a large one, which arrived yesterday, and he will set out on it today “im Nahmen Gottes”. Because of the delay and other necessary outlays he has borrowed another 3,000 thalers, hopes that E. (Your Majesty) will be content and have them paid, and will gladly let them be deducted later. [117] has so far resolved nothing on H.’s proposal, but he hopes W. will declare themselves better. Dated [118], 20 July of the current year; signed “Quem novit”. The letters H., E. and W. are written in clear as abbreviations; who they stand for is not established here.

The reading has holes in many sentences: 33 of the 103 cipher units are marked in place. The text reads as connected German whose sense can be followed, but it is not a clean reading, and the repository’s sentence check scores it marginal (20 of 43 sentences clean).

04 How it was counted

The unit is the cipher word, in reader 1’s division (103 units). A unit counts as read only if the decode, with every sign taking the value that at least two of the three readers give, is a German word as written. Letters supplied from the sense, a reader’s emendation, a sign on which the readers do not agree, and the code numbers all count as not read.

The 33 units not read are: 13 that involve the g/q class of signs (the contributor’s g sign, plain c, which the readers split between g and q; a unit with another doubtful sign as well is counted here), 7 emendations by the first reader, 4 on the pi/Z/n class, 3 on c/e, the 3 code numbers, and 3 single other signs (heute, um[b], ann[o]).

Three readers made three transcriptions. By the contributor’s own labels they score, under the fixed key: reader 1, who built the key while transcribing, 93 of 103 (90.29%); reader 2, blind, 62 of 104 (59.62%); reader 3, blind, 49 of 110 (44.55%). That first figure was the one filed on the issue; the contributor has withdrawn it as the headline because it was one reader’s count and the two blind readers did not reproduce it. The rule of the majority count was written down and frozen before it was decoded, and was decoded once. The order of events (the two blind transcriptions frozen at 09:49:43 and 11:22:40 UTC, the adjudicated text frozen at 13:17:09 and decoded at 13:17:19 UTC, all on 12 November 2026) is by the contributor’s record: only reader 1’s folder carries an external time stamp (RFC 3161, 02:25:10 GMT), and that was checked at integration for what it covers (the certificate chain was not verified).

Sign agreement between the readers: reader 2 with reader 1 on 511 of 565 signs (90.44%), reader 3 with reader 1 on 497 of 564 (88.12%), reader 3 with reader 2 on 484 of 564 (85.82%); in reader 1’s frame, at least two of three agree on 544 of 557 signs (97.67%).

Checks: the contributor’s German 4-gram control finds none of 5,000 shuffled keys (two seeds) as good for any of the four readings. At integration an own control with a word list built from the repository’s German corpora found 60 of the 100 cipher words in the list for the true key against 6 for the best of 3,000 shuffled keys, and an independent decoder from the key file and the first transcription gave the same German. With only 557 sign tokens and 23 values there is little room to overfit. The repository’s own validation (docs/_validate.py) was not run on this letter.

At integration the right page of image 443 was fetched from the archive (4473 × 3436 px) and looked at in the project’s integration check: it is the letter signed “Quem novit”, with 117, 118, 3000 and 20 as transcribed, and lines 9–17 were compared at crop level only (layout and sign repertoire agree). No person has compared the transcriptions sign by sign with the image.

05 What remains open

  • The g and q signs. At the resolution available, the tail of the contributor’s g (plain c) and q (plain d) cannot be told apart by eye: g before theta (in sich, noch, nichts) and q (in das, den, damit) look alike. The distribution supports two signs (g stands before theta 14 times and q never; the two word-initial c, commendiret and currentis, are g), but this needs a person looking at the image at full size. 13 units depend on it (two of them with another doubtful sign as well).
  • The pi/Z/n and c/e sign classes (4 and 3 units), and the one n-form sign with no value.
  • The codes 117 (twice) and 118: no value, three tokens, no other leaf in this code known. 118 is the place of writing.
  • The writer and the year (1627 or 1628); who H., E. (the King) and W. are.
  • The clear German was transcribed by one reader only.
  • Prior art is thin: no reading was found in the printed sources and catalogues the contributor checked (Danske Magazin, Kong Christian IV’s egenhændige Breve, Danske Samlinger, Historisk Tidsskrift, the Swedish Handlingar, DECODE, Cryptiana, archive.org full text), but an anonymous letter cannot be searched by name, so “not printed” cannot be proved. Christian Wilhelm’s 1627 correspondence and Venetian state papers were not checked.

# "Quem novit" to Christian IV, 20 July [1627 or 1628], Rigsarkivet TKUA Nederlandene B.41, unit 261377, image 443 (right page): reading

Conventions: text between asterisks is from cipher, the rest is in clear (clear text read by reader 1 only). [117] = a code
number with no value. A letter in brackets inside a word = a sign on which fewer than two of the three readers agree, or
whose agreed value does not make the word; the letter is supplied from the sense and the unit is counted NOT read.
[?word] = reader 1's emendation of a one-sign slip (counted not read). Every hole is listed in `holes.md`.
Basis: `decryptions/adjudicated.txt` (majority of three readers per sign, decoded once with `key_B.txt`), 70 of 103
cipher units read as written. Word division, capitals, umlauts and punctuation are editorial.

## Reading

ziemlichen *[?Unkosten]*, nunmehr in die *drei [w]ochen*, will H. *keine Gallera bekommen* können, *aufhalten* müssen,
undt obwohl *e[t]li[c]he kleine Galleren* vorhanden gewesen, hatt H. doch Niemandt, wie insonderheit [117], darauf
*[z]u wagen* nit *raten [w]ollen*. Dahero nunmehr ein *[?Signoria] [?eine] grosse [c]ommendiret*, welche gestern alhier
angelangt, auf welcher H. *no[c]h heu[t]e im Nahmen Gottes si[c]h [?auf] den Wegk ma[c]hen wil.*

*Sonsten hat* H. *wegen dessen, das er solang aufhalten [wo]rden, au[c]h um[b] anderer nothwendigen Ausgaben halber
alhier noch ein* 3000 *Thaler aufnehmen müssen, verhoffet* E. *werde damit [z]u friden sein undt es aus[z]ahlen lassen,

# "Quem novit" to Christian IV, 20 July [1627 or 1628]: English

Same holes as the reading, in the same places. Text between asterisks is from cipher. A bracketed word or letter is
supplied from the sense, not read; [117], [118] are code numbers with no value. Spelling normalised.

## Translation

*Most gracious King*, H. lets Y(our Majesty) know how H., to his *great vexation* and *displeasure*, and at considerable
*[?cost]*, has now had to *wait* into the *third [week]*, because H. could get *no galley*; and although *[some] small
galleys* were on hand, nobody, [117] in particular, would *advise* H. *[to] risk* himself on them. So the *[?Signoria]*
has now assigned *[?a] large one*, which arrived here yesterday, and on it H. *will [make] his way [this very day] in God's name.*

*Further, because he was held up so long, [and] [for] other necessary outlays, H. has had to borrow another* 3,000
*thalers here; he hopes* Y(our Majesty) *will be [content] with that and have it paid, and will gladly let [it] be
[deducted] in [?future].*

*For the rest, [117] has so far resolved [nothing] on* H.'s *proposal, but I have good [?hope] that* W. *[may] yet declare
themselves somewhat better. Dated at* [118], *20 July of the current [year].*

"Whom you know." [in place of a signature]

## Notes

- "H.", "E." and "W." are written in clear as abbreviations. E. is the addressee, addressed as king (Christian IV). H. is
  the traveller; 118 is the place of writing; "Signoria" and the galleys point to Venice. None of this is proved.
- Writer: PROPOSED, NOT PROVED. One article (Bohemiae Occidentalis Historica 2/2019, read through a summariser only)
  has Christian Wilhelm of Brandenburg, Administrator of Magdeburg, reaching Venice in early July 1627 on his way to lead
  Danish forces in Silesia. A three-week wait for a galley on 20 July fits that journey, so the letter may be of 20 July
  1627 (Old Style) and "H." may be the Administrator. Not tested against any dated source.
- Year: the leaf lies between papers of September 1628 (image 442) and Schultetus's letter after 24 July 1628 (image 444);
  the filing order suggests 1628, the identity lead 1627. Not settled.
