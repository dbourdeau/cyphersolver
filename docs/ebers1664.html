Adolf Ebers in Moscow, 1662–1666: the key of his code

Go to the full text ↓

The Swedish commissary’s German letters from Moscow were deciphered at the time between the lines; the key, rebuilt from those glosses and tested once on a page not used to build it, is now a reusable table.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 11 November 2026

Method: key recovered based on adjacent plaintext · Extent: complete · On the map

With Brice Dudout (transcription, verification, solution) · Also credited · Contemporary key or decipherment (earlier solution)

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · complete

The letters
Letters of Adolf Ebers (ennobled Eberschildt), Swedish commissary in Moscow, to Charles XI (the Regency), in German, 1662–1666, with the sensitive passages in number code.
Where
Riksarkivet, Stockholm, Diplomatica Muscovitica, SE/RA/2113/2113.1/3/b/602 (172 images, series R0003225). Not on DECODE.
The cipher
A German letter substitution with homophones: two-digit groups 15–95 separated by dots, mixed with clear German.
How the key was found
Learned from the glossed words of one letter (images 106 and 108), frozen by SHA-256, then measured once on image 138: 0.909 of the letters against 0.047 for the best of 200 shuffled keys.
What it says
On the measured page: envoys to go to Poland for the boundary commission, the voivodes at Smolensk, treating with the Poles; Marselis and Nashchokin are named.
Credit
Key, transcription and test by Brice Dudout (pull request #383). The decipherment itself is the clerk’s of the 1660s. The lead came from the project’s Riksarkivet sweep.
Still open
Two values added after the test (53 = s, 55 = t) are untested; 46 is unresolved. Every run carries its gloss, so no unglossed page is left to test on.

01 The letters

The volume holds the letters of the Swedish commissary in Moscow to the Regency that governed for the young Charles XI. They are in German, and the passages to be kept secret are written in numbers. A clerk wrote the German decipherment over every run of numbers: the project’s leads review of 6 November 2026 found it on every page it zoomed and noted that “only the key is of further use”. This target supplies that key.

Brice Dudout used three images. Images 106 (Moscow, 17 July 1664) and 108, a second copy of the same letter, were used to learn the key. On image 138, the first 160 cipher groups of the left page were held out and measured once. Code runs occur on nearly every image from about 98 to 145 and at 81 and 160; beyond these three images they were not transcribed, since the gloss over them is already their decipherment.

02 The key

The order is roughly alphabetical: 15 a, 16 b, 18 c, 20 d, 22 e, 24 f, 36 g, 38 h, 39 i, 40 k, 41 l, 43 m, 45 n, 47 o, 48 p, 51 r, 94 s, 95 t, 67 u, 68 v, 69 w, 72 z. Frequent letters have a second number (77 a, 81 e, 25 f, 89 n, 90 o), and doubled letters their own (42 ll, 44 mm, 54 ss). 74 = ä rests on one word. Arabic numerals (3, 12, 2000, 2500) are written in clear.

The frozen key has 31 codes. After the measurement, glosses on images 101, 103 and 138 gave 53 = s (nichts, etwas, smolinsko, naschokin) and 55 = t (tractiren). These two rows are marked “post” in key.tsv and were not tested. Group 46 (image 106, “51.43.15.46”) is unresolved. The key table is released under CC BY 4.0.

03 The held-out test

The protocol was written down and hashed before any of the held-out part was transcribed. The key was learned from 53 glossed words of images 106 and 108 only, then frozen. On image 138 the first 160 cipher groups were transcribed and frozen first; only then was the gloss above them read and frozen. One measurement compared the decode with the gloss letter by letter (a local alignment, after normalising spelling such as u/v/w and umlauts):

  • 0.909 of the decoded characters agree with the gloss;
  • the best of 200 keys made by shuffling the same values gives 0.047 (mean 0.021), a margin of 0.862;
  • the thresholds fixed in advance were 0.85 and a margin of 0.30, so the key passes.

The 9 groups outside the frozen key (five of 53 and 55, four cut in the binding) counted as wrong. One bias is declared: the gloss is on the same image and was visible while the numbers were transcribed. On integration the measurement script was run again on the frozen files and gave the same figures, the SHA-256 hashes check out, and the decode of the transcription with key.tsv matches the decryption file line for line.

04 The measured page

Of the 160 groups, 151 are in the frozen key, 5 are 53 and 55, and 4 are cut in the binding. The decode with the full key, with the gloss’s word division:

alles in considerati[o]n nimt eben kluch von hier gehn gekommen ist hier beschlossen gesanten nach [p]olen commission [g]renzen zu
woiwoden smolinsko die polen zu tracti[r]en marselius naschokin in gnadge

The gloss of the time reads:

alles ?? consideration nimt eben klug von hier gehen gekommen ist hier beschlossen gesandten nach polen commission grentzen zu
woiwoden smolinsko die polen zu tractiren marselius naskokin in gnade

They differ only in spellings (kluch/klug, gesanten/gesandten, naschokin/naskokin) and in the letters cut in the binding, shown in brackets. These are phrases, not a running text: the clear German between the runs was not transcribed. In English: “takes everything into consideration … just as prudent … to go from here … has come … decided here … envoys to Poland … commission … boundaries … the voivodes, Smolensk … to treat with the Poles … Marselis … Nashchokin … in favour”.

05 What remains open

  • 53 = s and 55 = t, added after the measurement, are untested; 46 occurs once and is unresolved; 74 = ä rests on one word. The one held-out page is spent, so no blind test is left for them in the parts read.
  • A test with the gloss out of view cannot be made: every code run in the volume carries its gloss on the same leaf.
  • One reader (an agent) on 3000 px images, with no human palaeographic check. The few four-digit groups were not seen in the parts read. The date of the letter on image 138 is in its clear text, which was not transcribed.

The code of Johan von Lilienthal, the Swedish resident in Moscow from 1665, is a different cipher, with its own page: Lilienthal in Moscow, 1665–1667.

Image 138, left page: the 160 held-out cipher groups with the contemporary gloss not prose

Ebers reports that something has been taken into consideration and decided here: envoys are to go to Poland for the boundary commission, with the voivodes at Smolensk, to treat with the Poles; Marselis and Nashchokin are named, and someone is in favour ('in gnade').
