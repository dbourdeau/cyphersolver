Francis Needham before Sluys, 28 July 1587 — the pigpen letter, read

British Library, Harley MS 287 ff. 39–40 (DECODE R8479): Walsingham’s servant with Leicester’s relief fleet writes of the failure to relieve Sluys. The long cipher passages of f. 39v had no decipherment; the key was rebuilt from a handful of glossed words and all of them were read.

Daniel Bourdeau · solved 21 September 2026 · updated 22 September 2026

solved

Summary. DECODE lists R8479 as a three-page, partially decrypted letter of “Needham” to an unknown recipient, 28 July 1587, with an unknown cipher of graphic signs. The writer is Francis Needham, Walsingham’s servant, who sailed with the Earl of Leicester to relieve Sluys, and the letter is to Walsingham; its clear text follows the despatch of the same day calendared in CSP Foreign 1587 (pp. 206–8), which the Harley leaves are not. The cipher is a pigpen of nine box shapes in three forms (plain, dot below, dot inside) carrying the alphabet a–i, k–s, t–z in order. A contemporary hand glossed a few cipher words and the runs of f. 39r; the six runs of f. 39v were left bare. From the glosses advise, enemyes, campe nor the towne and Burgrave half the alphabet came out, the first bare lines read “want of intelligence” and “whether there was any bridge”, and the order of the grids gave the rest. Every run of f. 39v now reads: the besiegers knew nothing of the enemy, did not know whether there was a bridge, and found the channel closed with hoys and flyboats fastened with chains and strengthened behind with flat boats. One word of about six letters is unsure.

01 The letter

Harley MS 287 is a volume of Walsingham’s correspondence (the Cobham letters of 1588 on ff. 70–72 are read in another write-up). Folios 39–40 are one letter: f. 39r opens “It may please your honour. The sudden departure of the bark … before Sluce, was the cause that I was forced to break off”, f. 39v continues, f. 40r ends it with the date 1587, and the address leaf f. 40v is endorsed “28 July 1587. From Needham”.

CSP Foreign vol. 21 pt 3 (1929) calendars Needham’s letter to Walsingham of the same day from Flushing (SP 84/16 f. 216): the council aboard Leicester’s ship before the haven of Sluys, the firework ship that could hardly be brought up to the bridge, the States’ captains who would not lend boats because “this commission must come from the States”, and the fall of the town. The Harley letter tells the same story in more words; the calendar does not list it. A note in the same volume (p. 237) says Needham wrote to Walsingham in the cipher of Thomas Wilkes, whose key is at the front of BL Add MS 5935 (p. 35 n. 4). That manuscript is not online and was not used here.

02 The cipher

Nine box shapes, each written plain, with a dot beneath, or with a dot inside its angle. Read in order the shapes carry the alphabet straight through three grids:

Words are divided and spelled as in the clear text (wyth, chaynes, bootes). No homophones, nulls or code names occur on f. 39v. The dots are the whole difficulty: a dot below sits under the lower left of its sign, the script slants to the right, and a dot inside ⌟ lies in its upper left, so the first transcription pass read several t’s as k’s until the grid order showed the slip.

03 The reading of f. 39v

Cipher in italics; clear words around it paraphrased where the clear text was not transcribed in full.

  • “… we had want of intelligence these five days last past … [no] advise out of the enemyes campe nor the towne” (the last four words glossed at the time).
  • “and we were so far from knowing what was meet to be done at that time [that] we knewe not whether there was any bridge or not, or whether there were any at the chanel.”
  • “… being above 100 of their files into the town walls, and that the chanel was b[…]ed with hoyes and flyboates fastened wyth chaynes, strengthned behind wyth flatt bootes.”

This is the “bridge” of the calendared despatch: Parma had closed the haven of Sluys with a boom of boats chained together, and the English and Dutch could neither see it nor learn what it was. The rest of f. 39v, in clear, covers Sir Roger Williams’s sallies, the storm, the state of the town, Count Maurice, Colonel Morgan and Sir William Russell; the three words glossed near its foot (“Mr Burley”, “the Spaniard”, “were removed hence”) were read at the time.

04 Method

The four images were fetched from DECODE. The glossed words gave letters by alignment: advise = ⌟ ⊏ ⊔: ⊐ ⊐̣ □, enemyes = □ ⊏̣ □ ⌞̣ □: □ ⊐̣, the towne = ⌟: ⌝ □ ⌟: □̣ ⌞: ⊏̣ □, and Burgrave on f. 40r began ⊔ ⊔̣ ⌝̣ ⊓. The first bare line then read want of intelligence, and once a, b, c, d, e, h, i and k–s, t–z were in hand the order was plainly alphabetical, which fixed f, g, p and q and corrected the misread dots. A quadgram annealer was written for the case that the key would not close, and was not needed. The transcription is in needham1587/cipher.txt and the key in decode.py.

05 What remains uncertain

  • Line G, the verb after “the chanel was”: ⊔ then four cramped signs (one blotted), then □ ⊏, so b ···· ed. Sense: closed or blocked. Grade M.
  • All other cipher words: grade H (every sign read with the key; they spell English in the letter’s own spelling).
  • The key has not been checked against the Wilkes key in Add MS 5935.
  • The clear text of ff. 39r–40r is summarised, not transcribed.
