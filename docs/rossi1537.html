At a glanceCiphertext-only break · partial (93% of the cipher)

The letters
Pier Maria Rossi, count of San Secondo, writing from San Secondo to Ferrante Gonzaga, whom the address calls viceroy of Sicily, on 4 November 1537 (cc. 31–32) and 8 November 1537 (cc. 33–34). Both are in cipher except a few clear lines and the closes: 53 lines and 2,368 cipher units in all.
Where
Biblioteca Estense Universitaria, Modena, Autografoteca Campori, Lettera R, Rossi (conte di San Secondo), cc. 31–34. Catalogue entry 1564, one of five letters; the Estense records say “Parzialmente cifrata” and mention no decipherment.
The cipher
Simple substitution on a symbol alphabet of twenty letters, with homophones for a, i, c, g and u, no nulls and no code groups seen. One sign, P, stands for both d and t.
How it was read
An annealer with Italian quadgrams, run on a transcription frozen before decoding, gave continuous Italian with no crib; the rare letters were completed by reading. No decipherment or key on any leaf.
What it says
A jocular, wine-fuelled account of the retreat of “Astolfo”, an Imperial commander, before the French in Piedmont, with news of Pinerolo and Turin relieved, a raid from Mirandola on the Cremonese and a little gossip.
Still open
d versus t is chosen from context, 73 units are unread, three signs have no value and the persons are not identified. Letter A reads 0.937 and letter B 0.928 of the words, both under the 95% bar.
Credit
Transcriptions, key, reading and checks contributed by Malcolm McBratney (Brisbane), working with AI agents, as issue #428 and pull request #451 of 11–12 November 2026. Daniel Bourdeau asked for the verso of letter A, the d/t ledger and a strict recount, now in the folder.

01 The letters

Letter A (4 November 1537) has 22 cipher lines on c. 31r (910 units) and 14 on c. 31v (703 units), with four clear lines and the date at the foot of c. 31v. Letter B (8 November 1537) has 17 cipher lines on c. 33r (755 units), with two clear lines, the date and the signature at the foot. A few tokens inside the cipher are in clear (roman numerals such as xx, xxxx and xij) and the Latin word fuit heads one line of letter A; none is counted as a unit. The address leaf names Ferrante Gonzaga, viceroy of Sicily, “in Messina o dove sera”.

The clear close of letter A says why it is in cipher: “havendo pensato che a questa V. Ex.tia haverà havuta la zifra, ho scritto questa”. Rossi had sent Ferrante the cipher and assumed it had arrived. The leaves carry no decipherment, and none was found elsewhere.

02 The cipher

Twenty letters are written with their own symbols; a, i, c, g and u have two or three each (a three, i three, c two, g two, u two). No nulls and no code groups are seen. The sign names in key.tsv are the transcriber’s. Two details matter.

P stands for both d and t. The first transcription gave one code, P, to a tall sign with a hat. On reading, it covered d (89 times) and t (152 times): for d the hook of the hat closes onto the stem, for t the hat stays open over a separate tick. A third tall sign, with a closed arch and a bowl at the foot, is l. To see how well the shapes can be told apart without the word, 256 P signs were cut out under random ids and classed by a reader who saw only the crop. Where that reader and the contributor’s second look (not blind) both gave d or t they agree on 178 of 215 signs, 82.8% (172 of 208 on firm verdicts only). Against the reading, the blind classes A and B give its letter on 187 of the 241 P signs read d or t (77.6%), the second look on 214 (88.8%); the contributor’s figures of 195 and 231 also count as agreeing 8 signs the blind reader called unclear and 17 the second look never saw. So the shape rule is real but not safe sign by sign, and in practice d or t is chosen from context. The test is blind for P only: the l shape has no blind confirmation.

Other weak points. The sign # is a in its small form and q in its tall form before the u sign; the sign S (p) stands for 8 (u) ten times; four times q is followed by S where the word needs u. The reading corrects these.

03 How the key was found

  1. 11 November 2026: both letters were transcribed from the Estense images by one reader and the transcription frozen, with hashes recorded, before any decoding.
  2. An annealer with Italian quadgrams, first tested on a synthetic text of 904 characters with d and t merged and homophones (86.5% of the letters recovered), was run on the frozen transcription: six restarts of 50,000 steps, -4.227 per character, continuous Italian from the first run (“che Pinarolo et Turino sono socorsi et dodici pezi de artiglieria”). No crib and no gloss was used.
  3. The key was completed by reading (the rare letters b, f, g, m, p, z, which the solver had merged into frequent ones), and the reading was aligned unit by unit to the frozen transcription.

The method is a key recovered from the ciphertext alone. That the transcription was frozen before decoding rests on the contributor’s record (one pull request, his own manifests): the RFC 3161 token imprints and the stamped files he sent agree with it, and nothing outside his files fixes the order. A second transcription by another session that opened only the images agrees with the first on 2,247 of 2,363 aligned units (95.09%); it did not split d from t either.

04 The reading

The letters are jocular. Rossi writes after supper and says so; he mocks “Astolfo”, who had written all over Italy that he would fight the French and has instead withdrawn, and promises to report his cowardices from day to day. Letter B opens:

Vi scrissi pur che la seconda retirata di questo poltrone di Astolfo serebe di quaranta miglia, et così fu, con perdita de xij pezi de artiglieria. Io mi rido ta[n]to che dubito scopiar como Marguto, ta[n]to più che siamo sul cominciare. […] basta che Pinarolo et Turino sono socorsi, et dodici pezi de artiglieria perduti. Non vi è uno quatrino …
I did write to you that the second retreat of this coward Astolfo would be of forty miles, and so it was, with the loss of 12 pieces of artillery. I laugh [?so] much that I fear I shall burst like Margutte, [?the] more as we are only at the beginning. […] enough that Pinerolo and Turin are relieved, and twelve pieces of artillery lost. There is not a farthing …

Letter A adds Cesare Pallavicino riding post to the Marquis “to be present at this glorious retreat”, a recollection of the departure from Genoa in December (“it was not a flight, or we would not have gone to the mole to embark”) and an apology for the wine and for “not having to do business with Covos”. Letter B ends with men from Mirandola raiding the Cremonese and sacking Piadena and a second place that is not read, and Rossi keeping company with “the gran Meo, your prince of Luzara”, who has to be put to bed after supper. Both readings and translations are complete, with every hole marked in place; the Full texts below give them in full.

The contributor takes Astolfo for the Imperial commander in Piedmont, Alfonso d’Avalos, marchese del Vasto, from the retreat of forty miles and the relief of Pinerolo and Turin. That identification is unverified: the letters do not say so, and letter A also speaks of “il marchese” by title. Zopo, Vicencio the Venetian, Monfalconeto and the gran Meo are not identified, and the historical background he gives (the French under Montmorency relieving Turin in October–November 1537) was taken from general reference works and not checked against a history of the campaign. The writer is, by his notes, Pier Maria III de’ Rossi (1504–1547); this was not checked here.

06 What remains uncertain

  • d and t. 256 units carry the one sign P; the blind shape test agrees with the second look on 82.8%, so the reading chooses d or t from context. A person reading the heads on the original, or a blind re-transcription that splits the shapes, would settle it.
  • Unread places. 73 units on sixteen lines, chiefly L05, L08, L17 (a name), L21, V02-V03, B12 (the place after “Castel”, near Piadena) and B16–B17; the places are listed in holes.md. New doubtful words proposed on 12 November from crops (for instance “tal è [?voce]”, “[?vedeti]”) are not in the unit ledger.
  • Signs without a value. Three, two occurrences each: a 4 with a top bar, an apostrophe mark and a bare tick.
  • Persons and context. Astolfo, Zopo, the marchese, Vicencio, Monfalconeto and the gran Meo are not identified.
  • Prior art. No edition or decipherment was found and priority is not established; the Archivio Gonzaga in Mantua, the Archivio di Stato di Parma (Famiglia Rossi) and the printed Gonzaga correspondence were not reached.
  • Catalogue. Entry 1564 listed five letters; the Muzio letters of 1550 (another cipher, with its own page) have left it, and the Gosellini letter of 1555 stays.

Rossi to Ferrante Gonzaga, San Secondo, 4 Nov 1537, cc. 31r-31v read in part

Written after supper and, the count says, a good deal of wine. He mocks an Imperial commander he calls Astolfo, who had announced all over Italy that he would fight the French and has instead retreated twenty miles, with forty to follow. Cesare Pallavicino has ridden to the Marquis to see the retreat. He recalls a departure from Genoa the December before, and excuses his free speech by the wine and by not having to deal with Covos.

Builds coherent sentences, but under the read bar of 95% of words read as sense (94% here); the holes are marked in the reading.

# Letter B: Pier Maria Rossi, count of San Secondo, to Ferrante Gonzaga. San Secondo, 8 Nov 1537 (c. 33r)

## Reading

[c. 33r] Vi scrissi pur che la seconda retirata di questo poltrone di Astolfo serebe di quaranta miglia, et così fu, con perdita de xij pezi de artiglieria. Io mi rido ta[n]to che dubito scopiar como Marguto, ta[n]to più che siamo sul cominciare. Spero fra pochi giorni sentiremo di bello, et vi so dir che io canterò di questo Astolfo et suoi centurioni. Alargate pur le orechie, aciò [...] posiate bene capir ogni cosa. Per questa volta vi dirò poco: basta che Pinarolo et Turino sono socorsi, et dodici pezi de artiglieria perduti. Non vi è uno quatrino, et per le [?valli] [?meglio] la [?comodità]. Già quelli dela Mirandola sono corsi sul Cremonese et sachegiato dui lochi, Piadena et Castel[...]; [?vedeti] che principii, ché in efetto da Cremona si cavava la magior parte del baioco. > [clear, c. 33r] et a V. Ex.tia humilmente basando li mani mi li raco et prego Dio la conservi felicemente. Di San Secondo li viij di 9bre MDXXXVij. D. V. Ex.tia [?servitor] El conte de San Secondo

# Letter B, 8 Nov 1537: English translation

## Translation

[c. 33r] I did write to you that the second retreat of this coward Astolfo would be of forty miles, and so it was, with the loss of 12 pieces of artillery. I laugh [?so] much that I fear I shall burst like Margutte, [?the] more as we are only at the beginning. I hope within a few days we shall hear something fine, and I can tell you that I shall sing of this Astolfo and his centurions. So open your ears wide, so that [...] you can take everything in. For this time I will tell you little: enough that Pinerolo and Turin are relieved, and twelve pieces of artillery lost. There is not a farthing, and through the [?valleys] [?better] the [?convenience]. Already the men of Mirandola have raided the Cremonese and sacked two places, Piadena and Castel[...]; [?see] what beginnings, for in fact the greater part of the money was drawn from Cremona. I want to finish, so you will hold me excused, for I have to keep company with the gran Meo, your prince of Luzara, who does not let me [?live] [?for] [...]; and as soon as he has supped he has to be [?put] to bed.

> [clear, c. 33r] And humbly kissing Your Excellency's hands I commend myself to you and pray God keep you in happiness. From San Secondo, the 8th of November 1537. Your Excellency's [?servant], the count of San Secondo
