Edward Wotton from Scotland to Walsingham, August–September 1585

→

British Library, Add MS 32657, ff. 167–168, 173–174 and 175–176 (DECODE R4841–R4843). Three despatches catalogued as numerical ciphers with nothing decrypted. They are the ambassador’s drafts in clear English, with one- and two-digit code numbers standing for persons and places. The code numbers were identified from the cleartext around them, and the drafts were transcribed in full from the images and read in their last state (22 October 2026). The calendar of the sent despatches (CSP Scotland vol. 8) already glosses four of the numbers; it was consulted afterwards and corrects one identification.

The letters
Three drafts by Edward Wotton, ambassador to Scotland, to Sir Francis Walsingham, 25 August to 1 September 1585, in clear English.
Where
British Library, Add MS 32657, ff. 167–176; DECODE R4841–R4843, listed as numerical ciphers with no decryption (catalogue item 81).
The cipher
There is no letter cipher: a small nomenclator of one- and two-digit numbers stands in place of names (“39. came to me yesterday”).
How it was read
No cipher had to be broken. The drafts were transcribed and eleven code numbers identified from context; the Calendar of State Papers, Scotland vol. 8 later confirmed 19 and 39, gave 40 as the Justice Clerk, and 24 as Ferniehirst, not Huntly.
What it says
The plot that ended in the fall of Arran at Stirling in November 1585. Certain: 19 Arran, 39 the Master of Gray, 10 King James VI.
Still open
27, 32 and 36 are unidentified; 9, 20 and 3 are probable only.
Credit
Later glosses: Calendar of State Papers, Scotland, vol. 8 (1914).

01 The documents

Add MS 32657 is a volume of Walsingham’s Scottish correspondence (DECODE gives the span of the volume, 1547–1590, as the records’ date). DECODE imaged four pages each of three items:

  • R4841, ff. 167–168: “To Mr Secretary the 25th August”. The top of f. 167 carries the end of an earlier draft and jotted notes (“Maxwel”, “1000”, “Sterling”).
  • R4842, ff. 173–174: “To Mr Secretary the 30th of August 1585”.
  • R4843, ff. 175–176: “To Mr Secretary the first of September”.

All three are working drafts in Wotton’s hand, with deletions and interlinear additions. The code numbers are written inline, usually followed by a point, where a name would stand. The drafts also write “the K.” and “her Mtie” in clear in places, so the code was applied unevenly.

03 What the despatches say

25 August 1585 (R4841)

The King is hunting in a land of the Duke of Lennox. Gray (39) came to Wotton “yesterday”: although he had written that he would forbear to deal further until he heard that the Queen was satisfied with his doings, he judged delay dangerous. His discourse: he and the rest have run a wrong course “in seeking to disgrace 19 with 10”, whose love toward Arran is so extreme that he will not suffer a hair of his head to fall; as long as Arran continues in favour it is impossible to bring home the banished lords, and the league “cannot be sure”. Arran will be able to divert the King from England and toward France (3). To obviate this, the Queen should furnish the lords to take Arran and seize the King’s person. There are rumours that Arran and 24 are agreed, and that 24 may carry the King to France.

30 August 1585 (R4842)

Wotton answers Walsingham’s letter of the 21st about Gray’s complaint to “his friend in England”. Gray, 40 and 27 cannot live safely at court while Arran may return; 27 “was greatly hers, and is a great favorer of the Hameltons”. Arran still remains at Kinneil under guard. Bothwell has come to court, “to the great grief of the Arranistes”. The Jesuits Hay and Dury are in the country, and the King has charged Gray to take them.

1 September 1585 (R4843)

The device of Gray and the Prior of Blantyre: the lords are to join with 20 under colour of his feud with Johnstone, and send 500 men to seize the King and Arran. Gray asks for money to keep a hundred men and hold the King’s house; the commissioners are to meet on the Borders. This is the plan that became the return of the banished lords and the fall of Arran at Stirling in November 1585.

04 Method

The twelve images were fetched from DECODE with a logged-in session and transcribed directly; there was no cipher to break, only a hand to read. Each code number was identified by collecting every sentence it occurs in across the three drafts and matching it to the persons and events of August 1585 (Arran’s warding after the Russell affair, Gray’s intrigue with England, the Maxwell–Johnstone feud, the Jesuit mission). A number counts as certain (H) where two or more independent contexts fit only one person; probable (C) where one context points to one person or to a small set.

05 What remains open

  • 27, 40: two of Gray’s associates at court. The calendar gives 40 as Bellenden (below); 27 is untested, with Maitland a candidate.
  • 32: a place of refuge for Arran. 36: a source of money.
  • 9, 20, 3: probable only (grade C); for 20 and 24 see the calendar below. 3 was first read as France; on the full transcription it is the Queen of England, on sense only.
  • 11: open; and a few interlineations and struck-over phrases in the drafts of 30 August (p. 2 ll. 5–9, on embarking the Scots) and 1 September (p. 2 ll. 10–17, the commissioners), read only in their legible last state.
  • Prior identification, checked later the same day. CSP Scotland vol. 8 (1914) was consulted afterwards through the HathiTrust text view for the companion page on Walsingham’s side of this correspondence. The calendar glosses the numbers in Wotton’s sent despatches: Arran [“19”], the Master of Gray [“39”], the Justice Clerk [“40”] and Farnyhurst [“24”]. So 40 is Sir Lewis Bellenden, and 24 is Sir Thomas Kerr of Ferniehirst, not Huntly. Its no. 106 calendars the despatch of 1 September with “Morton” for 20 (Lord Maxwell, styled Earl of Morton).

To the Secretary, 25 August. The King is out hunting on an island belonging to the Duke of Lennox.

Sir, {39 = the Master of Gray} told me yesterday that, although he had written to you that he would stop dealing in the course he had taken up until he heard from England that the Queen was fully satisfied with what he had done, he had concluded that in important matters delay is dangerous, and so decided to give me his view of affairs here, so that you, informed by me, could pass it on to the Queen.

The gist of what he said is this. He now sees that he and the rest of his party have all along been going the wrong way about it in trying to bring {19 = Arran} into disgrace with {10 = the King}, whose love for him is so extreme that he will not let a hair of his head fall to the ground if he can help it. As long as {19 = Arran} stays in favour with {10 = the King} it will be impossible to bring the Lords home; unless they are restored the league cannot be relied on, and {39 = the Master of Gray} and the rest will not be safe. For {19 = Arran}, once he has the King again, will use his credit to ruin them and to turn {10 = the King} away from {3 = the Queen of England}; or, if he finds the King's affection for {3 = the Queen of England} [?unshakeable], he will not fail to carry him off to France.

To head off all these dangers, his advice is that the Queen, given so good an occasion by the death of Lord Russell, should take the matter very seriously and hold the King back from concluding the league for now, and let the Lords loose. To the Secretary, 1 September.

Sir, to carry out the proposal of {39 = the Master of Gray}, of which I have already told you, he and the Prior of Blantyre think it right that the Lords should by all means come to an agreement, quickly and secretly, with {20 = Lord Maxwell}; that {20 = Lord Maxwell} should then make a raid on the Laird of Johnstone, with whom he has a feud; so that, under colour of helping the Laird against {20 = Lord Maxwell}, the L. B., the L. H., S. J. H. and S. can gather their forces and join {20 = Lord Maxwell}, while the Lords send five hundred of their best men as fast as possible to seize {10 = the King} and {19 = Arran}. These five hundred are to be led by the Lord of Arbroath, the Earl [?of Mar] and the Master of G[lamis].

{39 = the Master of Gray} says that if he can quickly have enough money out of [36] to keep a hundred men for a short while, he will guarantee that the Lords are so strong inside {10 = the King}'s house that if {10 = the King}, on hearing that the Lords are coming into the country, tries to cross the water into Fife or to take refuge in [32], he will stop him by force and hand him over to them.

You can well judge how necessary it is to have a friend inside the court who can (should the surprise attack of the five hundred fail) keep {10 = the King} from slipping away until the Lords' whole force arrives. So in my judgement it would be no bad thing to grant {39 = the Master of Gray}'s request. He is now so far committed that, unless this succeeds, I consider him utterly ruined; and so he can be trusted all the more.

{39 = the Master of Gray} has had long talks with the Prior of Blantyre about this, and asks you to tell the Lords from him that whatever the Prior says to them when they come to the Borders, they should believe it, for he will certainly carry it out.

I have held back from telling the King the part of your letter about postponing the commissioners' meeting to a later day, [..] [..] until such a day, having been told by the Secretary that his commissioners cannot be ready so soon. They want one of the commissioners to be an earl, one of the men of highest rank. I made a show of disliking his proposal, but told him I would report it, and asked that his commissioners should have full power and authority to do and conclude whatever the purpose of the meeting requires, which he says they will have.

If the violent course is decided on, it cannot be consistent with {3 = the Queen's} honour for me to be in this country at that time. I beg you therefore to ask the Queen to recall me quickly, for I am in great danger; though I am unfit to be her ambassador, I may still live to serve her some other way. My sword cuts as well as any man's, and I have some friends in the part of the country where I live.

Note. The matter proposed by {39 = the Master of Gray} is being kept from {40 = the Justice Clerk} and [27], because we here think {40 = the Justice Clerk} would reveal it to [27], whom we do not take for any great friend of the Lords. I beg that the matter be handled so that {39 = the Master of Gray} can be sure the Queen places great trust in him; otherwise he may out of spite reveal the whole thing and wreck it.

As long as I stay in this country, people [?will] imagine that the Queen wants the league to go ahead and does not mean to support the Lords; that belief may do much harm and make their friends here hesitate.

[Endorsed:] Copy. To the Secretary, 1 September.
