Charles XII to the Council of the Realm, Bender, 26 April 1710

Go to the full text ↓

From exile at Bender, Charles XII offered Denmark peace under English and Dutch mediation and let his Council agree an armistice; Brice Dudout rebuilt the number key and deciphered both surviving copies.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 27 October 2026

Method: key recovered based on adjacent plaintext · Extent: complete · On the map

Contributed by Brice Dudout (solution, transcription)

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · complete

The letter
Charles XII, King of Sweden, to the Council of the Realm (the Rådet) in Stockholm, Bender, 26 April 1710 (Swedish calendar; 7 May New Style), countersigned by H. G. von Müllern. A second copy went, with the necessary changes, to his envoys in London and The Hague.
Where
Niedersächsisches Landesarchiv, Abt. Stade, Rep. 5a Nr. 670 (old Fach 76 Nr. 38), fols. 83r–84r (copy for the Rådet) and 85r–v (copy for the envoys): the copies received by the Swedish government of Bremen-Verden. Images on Archivportal-D. Catalogue no. 599.
The cipher
A Swedish nomenclator of about 1,100 numbers in alphabetical order: letters in two series, syllables, numbers, endings, then words and names. Denmark, England and Holland are written in clear as sigla (B.B., LL, ddd).
How it was deciphered
Brice Dudout rebuilt the key from a ciphered troop list in the same file whose clear draft survives, froze it, and tested it on letters not used to build it (93% and 96% of words recognised, controls at most 80%). The two copies of the letter, which encipher partly different words, check each other.
What it says
The Danish army has been routed in Scania. Charles is ready for “en billig fred under Sjömakternas mediation” and gives the Council leave to negotiate and to agree an armistice, for as long as a whole year.
Still open
One group (212) in the Rådet copy; ten word values set from the table’s order and the sense; five clerk’s slips. The Council’s clear copy, probably in the Riksarkivet, has not been consulted.
Credit
Brice Dudout (Europe chiffrée), working with Claude Opus 5.5: transcription, key, decipherment, Swedish text and English translation; he checked the doubtful numbers blind on the image. His write-up.

01 The file and its two copies

Rep. 5a Nr. 670 of the Stade branch of the Lower Saxony state archives is a file of Diverse chiffrierte Schreiben von und an Generalgouverneur Baron Nils Gyllenstierna, 1700–1711: ciphered letters received and sent by the Swedish government of Bremen-Verden, then a Swedish possession on the lower Elbe. It is digitised in 135 images under the Public Domain Mark. Fols. 83–85 hold two ciphered copies of one letter of Charles XII, dated Bender d. 26 April 1710 in the Swedish calendar, signed Carolus and countersigned by H. G. von Müllern of the King’s field chancery.

The copy headed Copia till hela Rådet i Stockholm (fols. 83r–84r) is for the Council of the Realm, which governed Sweden while the King was abroad. The copy headed Copia till Envoyerne Leijoncrona och Palmqvist mut. mutand. (fol. 85r–v) is the version for the envoys Christoffer Leijoncrona in London and Johan Palmquist at The Hague. Both are written the same way: the narrative in clear Swedish, the sensitive passages in number groups separated by points. Gyllenstierna’s chancery at Stade received them; no key or decipherment is filed with them.

The two copies do not encipher the same words. The envoys’ copy has 287 cipher groups and the Rådet copy 353, 640 in all. A word left in clear in one is often in cipher in the other (sidan is 170 231 in the envoys’ copy and clear in the Rådet copy), and the Rådet copy adds a long passage the envoys did not need: the Council’s authority to negotiate and to agree an armistice. Where the copies overlap, each is a check on the other.

02 The cipher

A one-part nomenclator in Swedish: about 1,100 numbers in alphabetical order. No table of it survives in the file, and its layout was worked out from the recovered values:

31–64letters, first series: a ä å b c ch ck d e f g h i k l m n o p q r s sk sch sp st t u w x y z31 a, 47 n, 52 r, 59 t
65–98letters, second series: the first plus 3465 a, 81 n, 87 s, 93 t
99–182syllables, consonant and vowel, from ba to wu100 be, 149 ne, 163 ra, 180 wi
183–211numbers183 en (1), 210 hundra, 211 tusen
212–304endings245 en, 267 ing, 304 tion
310–1102words and names, from af to Wismar552 fred, 653 Konung, 956 stille, 1033 wapen

Each letter has two numbers, one in each series; each syllable, ending and word has one. A word that is not in the table is spelled out from syllables, letters and endings: Sjömakternas is 913 692 312 47 65 53, Sjö-makt-er-n-a-s, and kunne is 137 47 149, ku-n-ne. The three powers are not enciphered at all: they stand in clear inside the number runs as sigla, B.B. for Denmark, LL for England and ddd for Holland, the United Provinces. The recovered word values follow alphabetical order almost without exception (99.7% by the contributor’s count), and that order is what let values occurring only once be placed.

The two copies use 178 different numbers for 638 occurrences, about 3.6 occurrences per number. Most numbers stand for a syllable or a whole word and occur once or twice. That matters for the statistical check in section 04.

03 How the key was rebuilt

A crib from the same file. The file also holds a ciphered troop list (fols. 73 and 82) whose clear draft survives (fol. 74). Aligning the two, Brice Dudout fixed the number groups from the totals and the officers’ names, then the first letter and syllable values, and from them the alphabetical layout of the whole table.

Frozen, then tested. The key, K1710, was frozen with its SHA-256 fingerprint recorded, and applied to pages not opened before: a German letter to von Müllern (fol. 100), in which 93% of the words were recognised, and two pages of a letter to the King (fols. 78v–79r), with 96%. Control keys with permuted values or shuffled groups reached at most 77–80% on the same measure.

The letter of 26 April. Both copies were then deciphered and compared word by word. Values that occur only in this letter were set from their place between neighbours in the table and from the sense: 345 andra lies between 337 allt and 357 Arme, 1049 wilkor just before 1054 Winter. Brice Dudout then checked the doubtful numbers on the image without being told the expected value, sixteen points in all. The key file in the repository holds 334 values.

04 The statistical check

The project’s statistical check (docs/_validate.py) scores the deciphered letters with a Swedish letter model and compares the score with two kinds of rival key. Random keys score far below the reading: −3.54 per letter on average against −2.59, 7.6 standard deviations lower. A key fitted by the annealer to the same numbers shuffled into random order, though, scores better than the reading (−2.35 on average, −2.33 at best). The verdict is underdetermined: on this text the letter model cannot tell the reading from a fitted key.

That follows from the cipher. With 178 different numbers for 638 occurrences, a free key can give each number whatever syllable makes the letters look Swedish, so almost any sequence can be fitted. The model is also built on modern Swedish, and the letter is spelt as in 1710 (wij, hwilken, giöra). The evidence for this key is of another kind: the crib, the letters held out from building it, the alphabetical order of the table, the agreement of the two copies, and the checks on the image.

English

  1. The news. Charles has learnt that the King of Denmark’s army, shipped into Scania the previous autumn, has been beaten and scattered. He takes it as God’s punishment of the Danish King’s faithlessness and breach of his word and seal, and expects him to change his designs and seek terms.
  2. The offer. He has reason enough to press his advantage, and the States General have let themselves be drawn onto the Danish side “by others”; even so he is inclined to an equitable peace with Denmark under the mediation of the Maritime Powers, England and the United Provinces, provided he is asked and assured beforehand of satisfaction for the wrong and damage that he and his subjects have suffered from “so unjust a breach of the peace”.
  3. To the envoys. The longer the King of Denmark draws out the war, the harder he will find it to satisfy Sweden. The envoys are to declare, as of themselves and without any order, the King’s wish for peace and his trust in Queen Anne’s friendship and readiness to procure an honourable and secure peace with Denmark.
  4. To the Council. The Rådet may hear and accept equitable peace proposals under the mediation of the Maritime Powers, if the Danes ask for it willingly and without further delay. It has leave and a free hand to open a peace negotiation with Denmark under English and Dutch mediation on the conditions stated, and meanwhile to agree an armistice or suspension of arms for as long as both sides accept: the King is content with any term, even a whole year. His ministers in England and Holland are told to say the same as of themselves; the Council is to settle the rest as it thinks best.

06 The letter in its time

Charles XII had been at Bender, on Ottoman soil, since the destruction of his army at Poltava in July 1709. Denmark re-entered the war that autumn and landed an army in Scania; Magnus Stenbock beat it at Helsingborg on 28 February 1710 (Swedish calendar), and it re-embarked. That is the action of the first sentence, which the letter presents as divine punishment.

The sentence on the Staterherrorna, the States General, has no verb in either copy, left out by the clerk or already missing in the draft. It probably refers to the negotiations of winter 1709–10 that led to the Hague convention of neutrality of 31 March 1710 (New Style), which in effect shielded Denmark from the Swedish troops in Germany; news of its signature could hardly have reached Bender by 26 April.

The mediation of the Maritime Powers brought no Danish–Swedish peace. The war went on in northern Germany, where Stenbock beat the Danes again at Gadebusch in December 1712.

The same key deciphers other pieces of the file: the troop list used as the crib and a long German letter to the King of autumn 1710 (plague and famine in Sweden, the neutrality of northern Germany, Poniatowski, the Porte and the Tsar). Those are not published here.

07 What remains uncertain

  • Group 212, in the Rådet copy only (fol. 83v, bo‹s‑tag‑are‑i‑212›t after som wid ett slikt wärk): not deciphered. It is the first number of the endings; the clear text before it is itself poorly made out, and the envoys’ copy lacks the passage. The sense of the clause around it is not established.
  • Values set from the table’s order and the sense (grade I): 238 det (twice; between 236 der and 239 dig, taken as the conjunction “that”, a use found in clear in the same letter), 473 Dansk (three times; between 444 Condition and 477 dem), 345 andra, 337 allt, 1049 wilkor, 25 ak in nöjaktig (Brice Dudout confirmed the number on the image, but it lies below the known letter series), and the word values 512 emot, 548 fort, 572 gärna and 934 slut. In the key file 1049 is still listed as wis?.
  • Clerk’s slips, checked on the image by Brice Dudout (grade M for the corrections made from sense): Konung‹169›en, a superfluous se; förlika 108 med oss, 108 cu for 908 sig, which the other copy has; satisfa-31-tion, a for c; Stat-ä-58 for r, which the other copy enciphers as 52; and ‹slores›, garbled for a verb whose sense is clear (“were it concluded”), followed by a struck group and ra. Both copies spell ge-na for gärna, so that slip was probably in the draft.
  • The clear text is in a fast cursive, and its reading is a working one: the gaps marked (…) and [?] are in the clear, not in the numbers.
  • Prior work. No edition of the letter was found: it is not in Carlson’s Konung Karl XII:s egenhändiga bref (1893, autograph letters only) or in the Handlingar rörande Skandinaviens historia, and web searches in Swedish, English and German found nothing (27 October 2026). The Historiska handlingar volumes and the Riksarkivet registers are not searchable online. The Council’s clear copy is probably in the Riksarkivet, Stockholm, and its content may be known to historians from there; it has not been consulted.
  • The rest of Rep. 5a Nr. 670 and the other Stade files of catalogue no. 599 are not covered by this page.

Charles XII, Bender, 26 April 1710: copies for the envoys (fol. 85) and for the Rådet (fols. 83-84)

A refugee at Bender, on Ottoman soil, since Poltava, Charles XII learns of the rout of the Danish army at Helsingborg. In a chancery letter whose sensitive passages are in cipher, he declares himself ready to make peace with Denmark under the mediation of England and the United Provinces, and gives the Council a “free hand” to negotiate and to conclude an armistice, “even for a whole year”. Two ciphered copies, received by the Swedish government of Bremen-Verden, are kept at Stade.

# English translation

## Copy for the envoys (fol. 85r–v)

Charles. Since we have lately learnt that the King of Denmark’s army, which last autumn was shipped over into Scania, has a short while ago, in an action, been utterly overcome, beaten and scattered, we may rightly conclude that the said King — inasmuch as the just God has thus punished his faithlessness and his wrongful dealing against his given word and seal — will change his evil designs and willingly seek to come to terms with us.

Therefore, although we have reason enough to pursue the happy success of our arms against him and to bring him into such straits that he can no longer harm us, while we have our other enemies to deal with, nevertheless, as we find that the Lords States, out of hasty thoughtlessness, [have placed themselves] on the Danish side, to which they have let themselves be led by others, we are inclined to enter into an equitable peace with the King of Denmark under the mediation of the Maritime Powers, provided that we are requested to do so and assured beforehand of a sufficient satisfaction for all the wrong, damage and prejudice that we and our subjects have had to suffer from so unjust a breach of the peace.

[You shall give to understand] that the longer the King of Denmark draws out the war and continues hostilities against us, the harder it will be for him to satisfy us in future and to make the amends [we can demand of him]. You shall, as of yourselves and without any order from us, declare not only our peace-loving disposition but also the trust we place in the friendship of the Queen of England and in her readiness to procure us an honourable and secure peace with Denmark. Herewith etc. Bender, 26 April 1710. Carolus. H. G. von Müllern.

## What the copy for the Rådet adds

… we wish to show ourselves inclined to hear and accept equitable peace proposals under the mediation of the Maritime Powers, provided that the Danes request it willingly, without further delay, and assure us beforehand of an equitable amends for the damage and inconvenience that we and our subjects have suffered from this breach of the peace and from the hostilities committed in one way or another.

We therefore hereby leave you permission and a free hand to open, under the mediation of England and Holland, a peace negotiation with Denmark on the conditions named above […], and meanwhile to conclude with Denmark an armistice or suspension of arms for as long as can be agreed on both sides; and we are content with whatever term such an armistice is concluded for, even if it be for a whole year.

We are letting our ministers in England and Holland know that they should declare our peace-loving disposition there as of themselves, as may be seen more fully in the enclosure. Relying on your known dexterity and proven zeal for our service and interest, [we leave you] to settle all this as you think best.
