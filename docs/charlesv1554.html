Brussels → London · numbers for letters, marked letters for syllables · 24 January 1554 · already in print, published cipher checked on a new leaf

Charles V to his ambassadors in England, 24 January 1554: Besançon Ms 1582, f. 2

A cipher leaf catalogued under 1555 turns out to hold the passage that the Besançon copy of a known letter lacks; its text and its cipher were already published, and both are confirmed on it.

Daniel Bourdeau

At a glanceAlready in print · key from adjacent plaintext, partial · 215 of 358 words sure

The document
Two pages of cipher with a decipherment of the time written around them: the central passage of Charles V’s letter to his ambassadors in England (Lalaing, Egmont, Courrières and Simon Renard), Brussels, 24 January 1554.
Where
Besançon, Bibliothèque municipale, Ms 1582, f. 2. The library’s catalogue calls it a cipher letter to the lieutenant d’Amont with a translation in the margin; a later hand dated it 1555. No DECODE record was found. The library’s scans were sent for research and are not shown.
Already known
The leaf was deciphered at the time. The text is in print from the minute (Gachard, 1882) and calendared in English (Tyler, Calendar of State Papers, Spain XII, 1949). The cipher was published by Eva Pich-Ponce in 2024.
New here
The leaf is identified and its date corrected: it holds the passage that Weiss (1843) printed as a gap in the Besançon copy. A key re-derived from the leaf’s own decipherment agrees with Pich-Ponce’s cipher. The cipher and the old decipherment differ in about two dozen places, and the printed minute sides with the cipher in 21.
What it says
How to answer the French peace overtures: the Emperor has always wanted peace, his proposal of September 1553 was never answered, and he will negotiate through Queen Mary once French terms are known.
Still open
The decode made here gives 215 of 358 words sure and 131 probable; the doubts lie in the re-derived key, which was kept apart from the published table on purpose. One word does not come out of the cipher, and the last line ends in three signs that do not spell the minute’s words.
Credit
The decipherment written on the leaf at the time, from which the key here was re-derived; Gachard and Tyler for the text, Pich-Ponce for the cipher, Weiss for the Besançon copy, Tomokiyo for the list of Renard’s ciphers. The identification, the transcription and the check are Brice Dudout’s (L’Europe chiffrée), working with Claude Opus 5.5 agents. Neither a key nor a solution is claimed.

01 What was already known

Everything a reader needs to know what this letter says was on the leaf or in print before this work.

  • The decipherment on the leaf. The clear text was written around the cipher at the time, on the leaf itself (section 02). The key here was re-derived from it.
  • The text. Louis-Prosper Gachard printed the minute of L’Empereur à ses ambassadeurs en Angleterre, Brussels, 24 January 1554, in full: Collection des voyages des souverains des Pays-Bas, vol. IV (Brussels, 1882), appendix CLV, pp. 311–314. The passage of the leaf is on pp. 313–314.
  • An English calendar. Royall Tyler, Calendar of State Papers, Spain, vol. XII (1949), pp. 44–49, from the minute at Vienna; this passage is on pp. 46–47. His note on p. 49 records “a fragment, from a mutilated copy at Besançon, printed by Weiss”.
  • The Besançon copy. Charles Weiss, Papiers d’État du cardinal de Granvelle, vol. IV (1843), no. LXIII, pp. 188–192, from the Renard embassy volumes (Besançon, Ms Granvelle 73, ff. 119–122). His text stops on p. 190 at “que nous avons toujours eu et avons encores”, with the footnote “Lacune considérable dans le manuscrit”, and resumes with “Davantaige, pour observer ce qu’est de coustume”.
  • The cipher. Eva Pich-Ponce, “El lenguaje cifrado de Carlos V y el matrimonio de María Tudor”, Cuadernos de Historia Moderna 49 (1), 2024, pp. 27–45. She describes this cipher from 24 letters of 1549–1554 to Simon Renard, studies the letter of 31 January 1554 (Ms Granvelle 73, ff. 201–206) and checked her table against two original nomenclators at Simancas.
  • The list of Renard’s ciphers. Satoshi Tomokiyo’s Cryptiana post “Three ciphers used in correspondence” (March 2026) gives it as the third cipher of the Granvelle and Renard correspondence, 1549–1554.

Neither Pich-Ponce nor Tomokiyo mentions Ms 1582, f. 2, or the letter of 24 January 1554 in cipher. The contributor found the five printed works only after transcribing the leaf and re-deriving its key, at a search of the earlier work on 18 November 2026.

02 The leaf and what it is

The leaf has cipher on both pages, about 1,121 signs in 50 lines. The decipherment of the time is written around it: three or four full lines above and below the cipher on each page, and a column in the margin. It has no address, signature or date, and the text starts and ends in the middle of a sentence.

The text runs from “[de no]stre part, que nous avons toujours eu et avons encores la mesme volunté de desirer la paix” to “il sera bien que de temps à autre [nous donnez advertissement …]”. That is the middle of the letter of 24 January 1554 as Gachard prints it. The leaf begins, in cipher, with the very words at which Weiss’s text stops, and it ends where Gachard’s next paragraph, “Davantaige”, begins. It holds the passage missing from the Besançon copy that Weiss printed. The catalogue date of 1555 is therefore wrong, and the “lieutenant d’Amont” of the catalogue title is Simon Renard, one of the four ambassadors.

One step further is a hypothesis and was not checked: that f. 2 is the leaf detached from that copy, Ms Granvelle 73, ff. 119–122. Settling it needs the two volumes side by side.

No image is shown on this page. The library sent its scans for research, and its consent is needed to publish them. A stretch of line 9 of the recto is set out below as transcribed: the transcribers’ labels for the signs, the value of each, and the words.

The labels are names given to shapes by the two transcriptions, both made by an AI model with the margin masked. In a label, ^ stands for a bar above the letter, _ for a bar below, a colon for two dots and a final t for the mark Pich-Ponce prints as a plus sign; the other labels are the transcribers’ own.

03 The published cipher on a new leaf

Pich-Ponce’s description, as her text states it: the letters a to t are written by the numbers 20 down to 2, with a second series from 21 to 39; u to z by 51–54 and 62–65; et by 55 or 66. A syllable of two letters is written with the letter that comes before its consonant in the alphabet, carrying a mark for the vowel: a plus sign for a, a bar above for e, a bar below for i, two dots for o, a dot below for u. There are nulls, and signs for persons such as the Pope, the Emperor and the King of France. Her tables are not reproduced here.

The key in this folder was made without her article. The two transcriptions were aligned with the decipherment on the leaf by a statistical alignment, and a rule was then fitted to the result. It came out with the same structure: 8 is n, 7 is o, 9 is m, 6 is p, 2 is t and 4 is r; 66 is et; a bar above gives re, de, le, se, ne; a bar below gives ti, di, si; two dots give vo, ro, co; and the base letter is the one before the consonant (c for d, k for l, q for r, m for n, t for v).

When the article was found, the contributor compared the key with her printed tables and gave counts only:

  • the 8 entries that are plain digits (185 occurrences) have her values;
  • 10 two-figure entries (252 occurrences) have her values if their hooked first stroke is taken as a 1 or a 2, as her series requires; this was not checked shape by shape;
  • 48 of the 60 syllable entries (375 of 416 occurrences) follow her rule, and for 3 of the other 12 her rule fits the printed minute where the alignment did not;
  • a few letters, the rare signs and the signs for persons were not matched.

Two labels of the transcription each cover two of her numerals (one is 20 a and 10 l, the other 17 d and 12 i), which is why the transcribers could not separate them. Her values were not carried into the key file or the decode. The contributor kept the two apart on purpose, so that the key from the leaf stays a check on her table and is not a copy of it.

How strong is the check? The key agrees with the decipherment of the leaf on 79% of letters. A key learnt on one page and applied to the other scores 0.726 and 0.691, against 0.25 at most for keys with their values shuffled; the first alignment used both pages, so this is not a strict test. The project’s own statistical test of a key, run on 24 November 2026 over 1,120 signs, is underdetermined: with about eight occurrences a sign, a letter model cannot tell this key from one fitted to shuffled text. The support for the key is of another kind: it was learnt from the decipherment on the leaf, the minute in print agrees with what it decodes where that decipherment departs from the cipher, and it matches a table published from other letters and checked against the Simancas originals.

04 Cipher against decipherment

The clerk who deciphered the leaf kept the sense and not always the words. Decoded with the key, the cipher differs from his decipherment in about two dozen places, and the minute printed by Gachard settles them. Of 24 places listed, the minute has the wording of the cipher in 21; in two of those the decipherment is missing or doubtful and not different. In one place it has the decipherment’s word, one is a partial match, and one is garbled in the decipherment and decodes from the cipher once the minute is known.

05 The decode and its count

The decode in the folder was made from the cipher with the re-derived key, so that cipher, old decipherment and printed minute can be set side by side. It is not offered as a new text of the letter. By the contributor’s count it has 358 words: 215 sure (60.1%), where the decode spells the word; 131 probable, where the decode is off by a sign or a key value and the word agrees with the minute; and 12 restored from the minute. With the probable words that is 346 of 358 (96.6%). A recount here gives 214 sure of 357, one word apart, on how the half-word that opens the leaf and one word split over two lines are counted.

Most probable words are probable for one of three reasons, all in the re-derived key: a missing value for the syllables no and po, so that nous comes out as “nus” and pour as “pur”; the two labels that each cover two numerals; and a confusion of two signs of two and three minims, which turns cardinal into “cardipaa”. Pich-Ponce’s table accounts for them (for the first, as far as no goes). Her values were not applied, to keep the two keys independent.

… {nous} {avons} souvent {comporté} et {dissimulé} {beaucop} de {choses} {aux} françois et que ceste {dernière} rompture {ilz} l’ont faicte comme {tout} le monde scait {voluntairement} et par {leur} {libre} election sans cause {quelconque} …

Braces mark the probable words, as in the folder’s file.

In the passage Charles V tells his ambassadors how to answer the French overtures for peace. He has always wanted the peace and repose of Christendom and has often borne much from the French; they made the last breach of their own will; he has never refused to negotiate on reasonable terms. The proposal he put forward in September 1553 at the request of the Cardinal of Imola was never answered by the King. They may assure Queen Mary that he will come to it the more readily because it is a thing negotiated by her, but that he must first hear what the French will say, on a truce as well as on a peace. Renard is to find out from Paget whether the French ambassador has spoken further of the marriage matches, and to send word from time to time.

An AI agent reviewing the contribution for the maintainers graded the text B: coherent French, but raw. Its own decoder, written from the key file, reproduces 48 of the 50 lines of the decode exactly. No person has reviewed the text, and nobody outside the contributor’s session has compared the transcription with the scans.

06 What remains uncertain

  • Line 13 of the recto: the cipher gives “deit” where the decipherment and the minute have mit.
  • The last line of the verso ends with three signs that do not spell the last eight words of the sentence in the minute (“regarder à quoi nous vouldrons resouldre selonc ce”); they are restored from the minute. Whether the cipher went on on another leaf is not known without the manuscript.
  • The last line of each page was cut off in the first crops and was transcribed later from the full scans, in two more passes. One sign at the end of the recto is cut by the edge of the paper.
  • The key is partial by choice: the values for no and po, the two double labels and the rare signs are left as re-derived.
  • Whether f. 2 was detached from Ms Granvelle 73, ff. 119–122, is not checked.
  • The two transcriptions agree on 91% of the signs. They were made by an AI model; no person has checked them against the scans.
