Reading the Hebrew exercises

Read the dotted rows in the upper left of the verso from right to left. The sequence includes כך (kaf and final kaf), למם (lamed, mem and final mem), then נן (nun and final nun). The same pattern accounts for the two forms of pe and tsadi farther along. Comparing repetitions across the tears recovers all 27 forms in order.

The verso repeats אומר אני מעשי למלך, “I address my verses to the king.” The right column of the recto supplies the continuation לשוני עט סופר מהיר, “my tongue is the pen of a skilled scribe,” and the opening word of the next verse. Another Geniza exercise, ENA 3129.4–5, uses the same quotation.

Testing the repeated signs

In the lower right of the verso, a cup-shaped sign and a ring on a stem help locate successive copies of one line. The row underneath gives a separate check on proposed letter values. In the working transcription a wavy ink stroke in this second row can be counted as one sign or two. A tear crosses the corresponding spot in the other copy.

One proposed first row spells the Hebrew letter names lamed, mem, nun, samekh, ayin. It fits the repeated sign and two wide spaces if the row runs left to right and the cup is a marker. But the next five letter names conflict with the repeated signs in the following row. Searching both directions, with and without the cup, gave a first-row fit in 666 of 1,000 shuffled lists of the 22 letter names. The alphabet on the sheet explains why the idea was worth testing. The second row shows why the fit cannot establish a key.
