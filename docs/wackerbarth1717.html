The letters
Three letters of August Christoph von Wackerbarth in French with passages in number cipher: probably to his wife, from the camp before Stralsund, 20 November 1715; to Flemming from Vienna, 24 April 1717; and Relation XXII to King Augustus II, Vienna, 14–16 June 1717.
Where
Dresden, Sächsisches Hauptstaatsarchiv, 10026 Geheimes Kabinett, Loc. 00712/05 (1715) and Loc. 00713/01 (1717). Scans online at archiv.sachsen.de. Not in the project’s catalogue; no DECODE record was found in the repository’s copy (the live database was not queried).
The cipher
One key in all three: French letters in one- and two-digit numbers with several numbers for common letters, three-digit numbers for names and words, most four-digit numbers taken for nulls, and clear words in between.
How it was deciphered
The key was rebuilt twice from decipherments of the time written between the lines of other letters, once in each volume, then applied to two letters that have none. The text of the letter of 24 April is taken from its own decipherment sheet of the time.
The tests
The declared ground-truth test failed (0.339 where 0.60 was required). A held-out language test passed, the 1715 letter’s own test passed, and at verification each volume’s key was found to read the other volume’s letter.
What they say
In 1717 the imperial chancellor Sinzendorf proposes a concert of the Emperor, the King of Poland and the King of England for peace in the North. In 1715 Wackerbarth tells his correspondent in Vienna, probably his wife, what to answer: Augustus II wants “une paix générale aucunement séparée”.
Credit
Transcription, key, tests and reading by Brice Dudout (pull request #418), who ran AI agents for the work. Anne-Simone Rous works on the same correspondence; her paper led him to the volumes, and this is simultaneous, independent work alongside hers.
Still open
263 of 1,638 cipher words: numbers that no interlinear gloss gives (79, 96, 1000 and others; 72 rests only on the non-blind alignment of the 24 April cipher on its sheet), words where the reading gives a number other letters than its gloss value or supplies letters the cipher does not give, about twenty numbers with no value at all, and three numbers probably misread. An original key may survive in Dresden; it was not looked for.

01 The letters

The Saxon Hauptstaatsarchiv in Dresden keeps the correspondence of two ministers of Augustus II, King of Poland and Elector of Saxony: Field Marshal Jacob Heinrich von Flemming and the cabinet minister and general August Christoph von Wackerbarth. The archive titles the series “Des Generalfeldmarschalls Herrn Graf von Flemming mit dem Kabinettsminister und General Herrn Graf von Wackerbarth gehabte Korrespondenz”. Two of its volumes are used here: Loc. 00712/05 (volume 288, 1715) and Loc. 00713/01 (volume 291, 1717, 298 scanned views). The letters are in French. Sensitive passages are written in numbers, and in many letters a clerk of the time wrote the decipherment between the lines. Some letters have no decipherment. Three letters are covered:

  • Relation XXII, Wackerbarth to the King (a copy, addressed “Sire”), Vienna, 14–16 June 1717: Loc. 00713/01, ff. 71–80v (views 0089–0099). Clear French and cipher alternate; 2,939 cipher numbers. No decipherment of the time.
  • The letter of 20 November 1715 from the camp before Stralsund: Loc. 00712/05, ff. 307–314 (views 0380–0387); 3,415 cipher numbers. No decipherment of the time beyond a few words in the margin. It is a copy, and Brice Dudout argues that it is Wackerbarth writing, probably to his wife, in Vienna (section 05).
  • Letter no. 15 to Flemming, Vienna, 24 April 1717: Loc. 00713/01, ff. 29–32 (views 0037–0040); 1,274 cipher numbers. Its decipherment of the time is on a separate sheet at ff. 33–33v.

No image of the letters is shown on this page. Brice Dudout reports the scans marked “In Copyright” on archiv.sachsen.de, and the archive’s imprint reserves copyright on its images unless stated otherwise; the record pages state no licence. The scans can be opened from the links under Sources.

The same volume of 1717 holds letters of November and December in another key, the Flemming key of Loc. 00675/10. Brice Dudout checked five of them against their glosses of the time. They are a different cipher and are not part of this page.

02 The cipher and its key

The cipher is a homophonic substitution of French letters: one- and two-digit numbers stand for letters, and common letters have several numbers (e is 2 and 10, o is 16, 22 and 33, t is 28 and 52). Three-digit numbers stand mostly for double letters, short words, names and titles (100 and 120 are plain d): 154 Votre Excellence, 168 l’Empereur, 177 le Czar, 187 le roi de Suède, 217 le roi d’Angleterre, 292 la Pologne, 306 Vienne. Most numbers of four digits fit no word and are taken for nulls; 1000 is proposed as “et”, and 4527, 4008 and 10009 are unresolved codes. Words in clear stand between the cipher passages.

Brice Dudout rebuilt the key twice, each time only from decipherments written between the lines at the time:

  • key c, from the glossed letters of spring 1717 in Loc. 00713/01 (views 0017, 0057–0062, 0071–0072, 0080–0081 and 0086–0088). The cipher numbers were aligned on the glosses by a script, and the key was frozen before it was measured. The decipherment sheet of 24 April was not used to build it.
  • K1715, from the glosses on views 0388–0390 of Loc. 00712/05, the extract of the countess’s letter of 5 November 1715, which the letter of 20 November answers, and its sequel: 57 numbers from 934 aligned pairs, by majority vote.

The two are the same key. The independent check made before this page was written found 47 numbers common to both and 43 of them with the same value (Brice Dudout counted 47 of 50). The four that differ are small: 20 is b, not “ee”; 71 is y (the 1715 key file lists i by a slip; its glosses have y eight times out of eight); 77 is s or ss; 97 is m in one key and la in the other. Both keys share 150 (le roi), 168 and 700 (et). Key c lists 103 as “les” (view 0088, “V.E. les desire”); a gloss of view 0080 also gives “le”: “deloger les russes” stands over 103 29, so 103 = le.

The glosses of each volume fill holes in the other’s key. Key c has no value for 4, 76 or 306; the 1715 glosses give 4 = x (nine times), 76 = st (five times) and 306 = Vienne (“la cour de Vienne”, view 0389). The key files (key.tsv, s15_key.tsv) are released under CC BY 4.0.

03 The tests

Brice Dudout wrote each test down and hashed it before running it, and ran each once. All his files carry the date 11 November 2026, so the order of events is by the contributor’s record.

The declared ground-truth test failed. The second protocol held the glossed letter of 28 May 1717 out of the key and declared that the key must recover at least 0.60 of the gloss words exactly, with a margin of at least 0.30 over the best of 200 permuted keys. The result was 0.339 against 0.128, a margin of 0.21. The same protocol required a second, language test on a page not decoded until then (view 0091 of Relation XXII, 353 numbers); that one passed, at 11.5 standard deviations above the permuted keys. The protocol allowed a reading only if both passed. Brice Dudout then decided to read Relation XXII on the language test alone, with the 95% bar as the final check: a rule changed after the result, which he declared as such. An earlier protocol had also failed on both its tests; by his later diagnosis its ground-truth score was divided by a sheet four times longer than the portion tested.

Other evidence makes the key credible. It is separate from the failed test and does not replace it:

  • The 1715 letter had its own pre-registered test. The key K1715 was frozen, the letter was transcribed by agents that had not seen the glosses, and one page (view 0384 left, 307 numbers) was measured once: 0.945 of the numbers fall in French words, against 0.730 for the best of 400 control keys, with the threshold set at 0.77 beforehand. Two deviations were declared: the control page was displayed on screen before the protocol was written (the key had been hashed in the same command, and the verifier rebuilt it from the training files), and a reduced thumbnail of view 0380 was seen at the start, before the protocol, to locate the pages, with nothing decoded from it.
  • The cross-volume check, made at verification on 12 November 2026 and not claimed by the contributor: K1715, learnt only from the 1715 volume, turns Relation XXII of the 1717 volume into French (90% of the numbers covered, 8.8 standard deviations above permuted keys over all 2,939 numbers). Key c does the same for the 1715 letter (5.6). Neither key was built from the other volume.
  • The verifier rebuilt both keys from the transcribed glosses and obtained the same tables (but for 71 in the 1715 table), decoded Relation XXII with a second program and obtained the same text, and scored view 0091 with the project’s own French model: 7.3 standard deviations, above all 200 permuted keys.
  • Two sample pages of the transcription were compared with the archive scans (view 0091 left, about 190 numbers; view 0384 left, about 150): all agree, except that at view 0384 line 11 a doubtful 28/25 looks like 28 on the scan while the reading uses 25.

The failed test could not be run again, because its word list and corpora are not in the folder; its figures match the contributor’s result file. The recorded verdict for the key stays “weak”.

04 Relation XXII, June 1717

Wackerbarth reports to the King that the imperial court, which had been waiting for the outcome of the campaign in Hungary, has at last spoken. Count Sinzendorf, chancellor of the court, tells him by order of the Emperor that the Emperor wishes to enter with the King and the King of England into a concert, to restore the tranquillity of the Empire and an “entire peace” in the North on a “juste équilibre”, in keeping with the laws of the Empire. Sinzendorf made the same overture the day before to Saint-Saphorin. The stay of the Russians in the Empire is referred again to the Diet of Ratisbon; the Emperor will concert with the directors of the Circles to get them out. The King of England wants the same measures taken regarding Poland, and no inch of the lost German provinces returned (the number that follows is unread; the sense wants the King of Sweden). The secret is closely kept: the ministers would not admit even a referendary to the conference. Sinzendorf has leave for a journey to Innsbruck of twelve to fifteen days, as the reading has it (the place and both numbers rest on inferred values).

The “triple alliance” is Saint-Saphorin’s opinion, not Sinzendorf’s proposal. The passage, on f. 77, follows the clear words “Selon lui: la Cour Imperiale ayant” (words in italics are in clear in the letter; braces mark a doubtful number):

été portée jusqu'à [la] démarche de nous faire connoître sa disposition à l'egard des affaires du nord l'affaire
pourroit même {être} menée au point d'en venir à une triple alliance entre l'Empereur le Roy d'Angleterre et le Roy

“In his view, the Imperial Court having been brought as far as the step of letting us know its disposition regarding the affairs of the North, the affair could even be carried to the point of a triple alliance between the Emperor, the King of England and the King.” The bracketed “la” is number 79, which no gloss gives.

Of the 716 cipher words, 634 are counted as deciphered (88.5%; section 07). The clear passages of views 0098–0099 were not transcribed in full.

05 Stralsund, 20 November 1715

The heading reads “Reponse à la lettre de 306. au camp devant Stralsund le 20e 9bre 1715”. The volume is catalogued as Flemming–Wackerbarth correspondence. Brice Dudout argues that this piece is a copy of a letter from Wackerbarth, probably to his wife, the Countess of Wackerbarth, in Vienna:

  • 306 is Vienna, by a gloss of the time;
  • the letter answers “celle du 5me du courant”, and the next piece in the volume (f. 315) is an extract of a letter from Vienna of 5 November 1715 on the same “raisonnement”, over which a hand of the time wrote “La Comtesse de Wa…” and “au C. de Wa…”;
  • the cipher reads “Voilà, ma chère femme, de quoi répondre…” (“ma” and “femme” from the key; “chère” rests on 72 = ch, which no interlinear gloss gives; it rests on the non-blind alignment of the 24 April cipher on its sheet);
  • the piece before it (f. 306) is a jesting full power signed “Le Comte de Wackerbart” at the same camp;
  • the writer directs the siege batteries and writes to General Dücker and to Count Virmond.

Against it: a private letter is not expected in a cabinet volume. The copy is in a clean secretary’s hand, unsigned, with a decipherer’s words in the margin. The attribution is probable, not proved.

The month was first transcribed “8bre” (October) and corrected to “9bre” (November): the figure is a 9 with a long descender, like the 9s of the cipher. The verifier read the heading on the scan of view 0380 and agrees. The frozen transcription files keep “8bre”.

The letter tells its recipient, probably the countess, what to answer to a “reasoning” put to her at the imperial court. The King of Sweden will be driven out of Germany, where only Stralsund and Wismar are left to him; before Stralsund the attack is far advanced, and Wismar is blockaded since the island of Poel was taken. The harm is of his own making. Then, in words the reader marked certain:

le roi est fort disposé à la paix mais à une paix générale aucunement séparée

“The King is well disposed to peace, but to a general peace, by no means a separate one.” The King does not want a total ruin of the Crown of Sweden and has stipulated as much with his allies. The reasoning heard in Vienna resembles the letter the Count of Croissy wrote at the start of the campaign. The remnants that might carry the King of Sweden back to Poland, at the head of “nos rebelles”, are shut up with him in Stralsund and should be destroyed while that is possible. Wackerbarth will write to General Dücker once the guns are in battery, and ask Count Virmond, the Emperor’s minister, to come, so that peace can be treated under the Emperor’s mediation.

Of the 922 cipher words, 741 are counted as deciphered (80.4%; section 07). Brice Dudout himself answered a blind plate of nine doubtful digit shapes, with two controls; three digits were corrected. The plate checks how digits are written, not what they stand for.

06 To Flemming, 24 April 1717

This letter was deciphered at the time. The sheet at ff. 33–33v is headed “Le C.te de Wackerbarth du 24. d’avril 1717”. As transcribed (399 words, one doubtful) it starts where the cipher of f. 29 starts, “vues du Roy de Suede sur la Pologne”, and runs to where the cipher of f. 32 ends, “le traité qui est requis”. The text given here is that sheet, with a translation: the King of Sweden’s designs on Poland and a landing near Danzig; the fear that Sweden may detach the Czar from the League; and the exchange of the county of Hanau, for which the consents of the Electors must be won directly and not through the Emperor.

The rebuilt key was run on the cipher of views 0037–0039 as a check: 180 of its 207 cipher words are counted under the rule below, and the sense agrees with the sheet. The check was not blind, since the sheet had been transcribed first. That the sheet covers the whole cipher follows from the transcriptions; it was not compared again with the scans at verification.

07 How the words were counted

Brice Dudout marked every cipher word as certain, probable, restored or unresolved, but by different rules in the two letters. In Relation XXII a word containing a number absent from the key is “restored” and not counted. In the 1715 letter the same kind of word is “probable” and counted. His figures were 89.5% for Relation XXII and 96.75% for the 1715 letter.

For this page both letters were recounted from the files under one strict rule. A script (integration_recount.py) aligns each word of the reading on the cipher numbers under it. A word is counted only when the contributor read it and every letter of it, as read, is given by the gloss value of the number under it, from a gloss of the time in either volume. A word is not counted when a number under it has only a value deduced from the sense, when a number with a gloss value is read as another letter, when such a number inside the word is passed over, or when a letter of the word has no number under it. The only spelling differences allowed are u/v, i/j/y, a consonant written once or doubled, “est” of the time read “ét” (estat, estre), and “à” + “le roy” read “au Roy”.

In the 1715 letter it takes out 151 words the contributor counted. 53 rest on values deduced inside the letter or taken from the non-blind reading of 24 April, above all 96 = le (19 words), 72 = ch (8) and 1000 = et (6). 37 read a number with a gloss value as other letters or pass it over (“devant” on “desant”, “assaut” on “attaut”, “Poel” on “poeh”). 61 have letters with no number under them; in 36 of these the cipher stops after at least three letters of the word (“réellem”, “allem”, “Strals”). These may be the clerk’s abbreviations, but the transcription records no abbreviation mark, so they are not counted; counting them would give 84.3%. Both letters are under the 95% bar, so both count as partial. Brice Dudout’s own figures were 89.5% and 96.75%. The rule was made strict after a second reader checked this page; the first recount had dropped a word only when a number under it had no gloss value at all, and gave 92.7% and 91.2%.

08 What remains open

Values from a gloss of the time come from a known plaintext (grade C). Values deduced from the sense are inferred (grade I) and are not counted. Numbers with a doubtful digit are uncertain (grade M).

  • Relation XXII, 82 words not counted. 43 restored and 8 unresolved by the reader, and 31 of the words he counted (above). Numbers with an inferred value only: 79 = la (12 times), 1000 = et (5), 40 = b (4), 73 = z (4), 38 = h (4), 92 = t (3), 89 = z (3), and once each 93 = d, 98 = la, 11 = k, 48 = le, 118 = est. Codes with no value: 4527, 289, 172, 169, 184 and 1900205. Two spelled words do not form.
  • The 1715 letter, 181 words not counted. 53 rest on inferred values: 96 = le, 72 = ch, 1000 = et, 85 = st, 64 = c, 70 = x, 73, 284 = comte, 40 = b, 609, 11. 98 have a number read as other letters, a number passed over, or letters with no number under them; the scans would have to be read again, blind, to settle them. Twelve are restored by the reader. Eighteen are unresolved: the numbers 183, 251, 270, 400, 357, 358 (twice), 365, 4008, 10009, 680, 68 and 94, and five spelled words. In view 0386 right, line 3, the 96 may be a 46.
  • 1000 = et, 40 = b and 11 = k were inferred in both letters separately and agree. No gloss gives them.
  • Three numbers in Relation XXII are probably misread, each a 7 with a hooked head taken for a 4: 174 for 177 (le Czar), 4 for 7, and 184 for 187 (le roi de Suède). They were left as transcribed and not re-read on the scans.
  • No ground-truth pass for key c. The declared test failed, and no other glossed letter of 300 numbers or more is left in the 1717 volume to repeat it.
  • An original key may survive. DECODE holds key records from Dresden, Loc. 03233/02, among them a key of Flemming with Wackerbarth. They were not tried on this cipher. This was not checked here.
  • No person has read the readings against the scans. Beyond the two sample pages, the transcription rests on AI agents.

09 Simultaneous work: A.-S. Rous

Anne-Simone Rous works on the cipher correspondence of Wackerbarth and Flemming. Her paper “The Keys of Diplomacy. The encrypted correspondence of Saxon-Polish Ministers Wackerbarth and Flemming 1700–1720” (HistoCrypt 2024) is where Brice Dudout found both volumes. By his statement the readings were made without contact with her and without her transcriptions, and nothing else of hers was used. They should be cited as simultaneous, independent work alongside hers; she may have gone further.

The paper could not be opened while this page was prepared: the repository of the University of Tartu refused every route tried on 11 and 12 November 2026. Only its abstract could be consulted. It says the paper presents tentative results of the first phase of a project on the whole correspondence of 1698 to 1728, from examples of 1700, 1706, 1715, 1717 and the 1720s. What follows is the contributor’s report of the paper and was not checked here: that she describes the 1717 system as numbers of one to three digits, mostly two, with 355 and 1000 in the nomenclator; that she lists the 1717 letter as not deciphered, including one of “24th July 1717” at ff. 29–33, which he takes for the letter of 24 April; and that she reports a glossed simple substitution in 1715. He adds that he may have misread her account.

## Summary

Wackerbarth reports that the Imperial Court, which had been waiting for the outcome of the Hungarian campaign, has at last
spoken. The Chancellor Count Sinzendorf, by order of Charles VI, proposes a concert between the Emperor, the King of Poland
(Augustus II) and the King of England (George I). Its aim is to restore the tranquillity of the Empire and an entire peace in
the North on a "just balance", in keeping with the laws of the Empire. The same overture
was made the day before to Saint-Saphorin, George I's minister, who thinks (in Wackerbarth's report: "Selon lui") that it might
go as far as a triple alliance; the triple alliance is his opinion, not part of Sinzendorf's proposal. The stay of the Russian troops in the Empire goes to the Diet of
Ratisbon and to the directors of the Circles. The Emperor is determined to get the Russians out and perhaps to guarantee against
their return. George I wants the same measures "regarding Poland" and will give back to the King of Sweden no part of his lost
German provinces. Strict secrecy: the conference was held without even a referendary. Wackerbarth will take the waters at
Baden while Sinzendorf is in Innsbruck.

Wackerbarth to Flemming, Vienna 24 April 1717 (read from the decipherment of the time)

The King of Sweden's designs on Poland (a landing near Danzig); the fear that Sweden may detach the Czar from the Northern League, which Vienna should thwart; the Hanau exchange: the Electors' consents must be won directly, not through the Emperor (promise of Ferdinand II to John George I); request for full power and a draft treaty.

[Concerning the] views of the King of Sweden on Poland, this further detail is added: his intention is to attempt a landing
on the side of Danzig; but nothing is said of Stanislas. A person who is, it is true, of no great rank, but who frequents
some houses of the first ministers and has credit there, told me on this occasion that this design of the King of Sweden
could not embarrass us as long as we were sure of the Czar's good intentions. But if the King of Sweden succeeded in his
design of detaching the Czar from the League and of reaching some understanding with him, we could be put in a bad situation,
a thing to which, in his view, one could not pay too much attention. Thereupon I let him understand that, should that happen,
this court here would no doubt find itself in a worse situation than ours, so that it ought to have more interest than we in
thwarting this negotiation of Sweden and so preventing the consequences it had to fear.

Your Excellency will have seen from my relation of the 10th of this month, signed jointly with Zech, what I said about an
interview with the Count of Hanau. It is of indispensable necessity that we lose not a moment in obtaining the consents of
the Electors ourselves. It would be an endless task ("une mer à boire") to persist in the view of our Council of State that
the Emperor must obtain them for us, according to the promise of Ferdinand II to John George I. If the Emperor asks the
Electors for them, they will ask him in exchange for things he could not do. So not only should we miss the consents that we
need to reach our aim; we should also close against ourselves the door to obtaining them, should we afterwards wish to work
for them ourselves. I believe, moreover, that we should make this affair more difficult and more uncertain by working on it
jointly with the Emperor. I still see a way to succeed in the project of an exchange, as [I wrote] to Your Excellency,
regarding the county of Hanau, if we clear up what is spoken of in my relation mentioned above, in which I ask for full
power and a draft in proper form of the treaty that is required. This is what for the present …

**[view 0380, f. 307]** (Heading:) Answer to the letter from Vienna. At the camp before Stralsund, 20 November 1715 ("9bre"; first transcribed "8bre", see NOTES.md).
I have seen by yours of the 5th of this month … a line of reasoning that was put to you on the interest of the allies of the
North with regard to the King of Sweden. There is in truth much that is solid in it … on a closer look it appears quite plainly
that it is only in so far as the King of Sweden finds his account in it. But let that pass, provided that we too really find our
convenience in it. You answered him very well … or, should the occasion arise, you may naturally bring this talk up again, and I
will tell you that it is quite certain that

**[view 0381, f. 307v]** the King of Sweden will infallibly be dislodged altogether from Germany, where so far nothing is left
to him but Stralsund and Wismar. Before Stralsund the attack has been [pushed] so far that, unless the King of Sweden wishes to
await the last extremity, I mean a general assault, he will draw no other advantage from it than the satisfaction of having
remained master of it a few days longer <400>. Since the island of Poel was taken without any loss on the [side] of the allies
of the North, Wismar is so closely pressed by land and by sea that, if only those who keep that place blockaded do not slacken
their attention, it cannot

**[view 0381, f. 308]** avoid falling this very winter, should it be needed to besiege it in form. From this situation of the
King of Sweden's affairs you can well see that this <183> can no longer prevent his being, in a short time, entirely dislodged
from the Empire or from the duchy of Zweibrücken, so that [he] can hardly trouble the allies of the North. The harm that comes
of it can only be regarded as a fruit of His Swedish Majesty's own will, since neither the good disposition of the allies of the
North for the restoration of peace, nor the exhortations of the Emperor and of his <c?eill> friends for that same peace and for
the restoration of tranquillity and rest in the Empire, nor the sad

**[view 0382, f. 308v]** situation <251> of his affairs compared with those of his enemies, the deplorable state of his people,
the exhaustion of his own forces, in men as in money as in the other needs for continuing a war, with only <aiparene> … against so
many enemies, each of whom is now able by himself to sustain the war against him, above all now that he will soon be brought so
low that a foreign power will hardly wish to embark with him, unless it were the Turk, whose help is so distant that he cannot
set his affairs right; finally,

**[view 0382, f. 309]** experience itself has not been able to persuade this [prince] that by going on with the war he would
make his condition ever worse. Could one see his state otherwise than as a fruit of his own will? It is also quite certain that

[conjuncture] can reasonably demand, and such also as it must be to secure <270> <aue> [the] foundation, the rest that is its

the King has gone so far in this sentiment that he has even

**[view 0383, f. 309v]** stipulated it in the treaties with his allies. It seems to me that this shows well enough his good
dispositions for peace, provided it is not of a nature to lay the foundation of a new war, but that one finds in it all the
security one can reasonably desire. Besides, the reasoning put to you seems to me to resemble in some manner the letter that

powers making war on him, and giving us at the same time such an idea of this [prince]'s firmness in pursuing his designs as must
