Erving at Madrid and Adams at St Petersburg: Two Legation Codes

George W. Erving to Madison, Madrid, 24 March 1807, Founders Madison/02-13-02-0559 · John Quincy Adams to the Secretary of State, St Petersburg, No. 88, 25 June 1812 · NARA microfilm M31 reel 12 and M35 reel 3

The holes in Madison’s own decode of Erving’s letter closed from the two received copies and Pinckney’s despatches; Adams’s code shown not to be Armstrong’s and rebuilt to 962 numbers from the clerk’s decodes and Ford’s printed decipherments; No. 88, which Ford printed with nine lines and the remainder “not decyphered”, read 355 of 390 groups as sense

Summary. Two small results from the same kind of source as the Armstrong postscript: the State Department’s despatch books on National Archives microfilm, where a reader in Washington sometimes wrote the decode between the lines. Adams. The Madison editors say that the Armstrong code (the = 972) was also used by John Quincy Adams at St Petersburg. His despatches to Washington are not in it. Every coded despatch checked on NARA M35 reel 3 uses a different code in which the is 1385 and of is 1576; Adams held a copy of Armstrong’s cipher, but for corresponding with Armstrong at Paris. That code is rebuilt here to 962 numbers: 391 from the Department clerk’s interlinear decodes on ten frames, 241 fixed by aligning 39 coded blocks of 1811 with the Department’s decipherments that Ford prints, 311 uncertain and 19 inferred. With it, despatch No. 88 (25 June 1812, the day after Napoleon crossed the Niemen), which Ford’s Writings of John Quincy Adams prints with “[Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” and the remainder “undecyphered”, reads 355 of its 390 groups as sense (0.910, measured): the nine lines 127 of 133 (0.955), the remainder 228 of 257 (0.887). The nine lines are about Count Rumyantsev, the Chancellor: the journey to Wilna went against his advice, he had an apoplectic stroke soon after arriving there and a second, worse one since, the German papers wrongly reported him dead, and he cannot stay much longer at the foreign ministry. The remainder is on Kurakin’s note, the French ambassador’s complaint, and Napoleon’s plan to strike along the Baltic. Erving. Erving’s private letter of 24 March 1807 is printed from Madison’s own decode, with the note that the key has not been found. Madison had the key; the holes in his decode are his own slips. With both received copies and Pinckney’s decoded despatches every hole closes (Hanover, in the scale, see that he was, Gibraltar), and the one real loss is a syllable Erving himself left out. Status: Erving’s letter complete, a small completion of a text already in print. Adams: No. 88 read in part, below the 95% bar; in the nine lines one word (im-pas-ion-ed), one slip (492 for by) and one doubtful group stay open, and four values rest on inference; in the remainder about 20 groups do not read and 7 are cut off at the edge of the film.

01 Adams: the premise checked

The starting point was a note the Madison editors attach to Armstrong’s letters (Founders Madison/02-10-02-0369 and 02-12-02-0197): the code was “also used by John Quincy Adams at St. Petersburg and by Jacob Lewis at Saint-Domingue; key not found but substantially reconstructed by the editors”. On Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811 (03-03-02-0121), the editors speak of “a code provided by the State Department to John Armstrong in France and to both William Short and Adams”, citing Ford 3:328. If that held, the 580-group Armstrong table already rebuilt on this site would read Adams’s coded despatches. It does not.

  • What Ford’s own text says. Secretary Smith’s instructions of 1809 (Ford 3:327–28) say that “the cypher with which you are furnished” is “the same with that of our minister at London” (William Pinkney), and tell Adams to “obtain at Paris, a copy of General Armstrong’s cypher also, for the like purpose”, that is, to correspond with Armstrong. In 1810 Adams acknowledges “the copy of General Armstrong’s cypher” (Ford 3:370). He held both. The despatches to Washington use the other one.
  • What the microfilm shows. Every coded despatch checked on M35 reel 3 (1811 and 1812) uses a code in which 1385 = the, 1576 = of, 1384 = that, 668 = and, 1401 = I. In Armstrong’s code the is 972 and of is 1354. Run through the Armstrong table (592 entries as it now stands), these pages come out as noise. Reel 2 frame 0155, of 1810, is in the same Adams code.

So the table already held cannot read Adams’s despatches. The editors’ note on the 7 January 1811 letter concerns a private letter to Madison, not a despatch, and was not checked against its manuscript, which is in the Forbes collection and not online; it may be right for that letter.

02 Adams’s code rebuilt

The method is the one used for Armstrong on M34 roll 13, with a second source added. On M35 reel 3 the Department clerk wrote the decode above the groups of several despatches; every pair on frames 0048 (1811), 0182 (No. 80, 29 February 1812), 0183, 0184, 0185, 0189, 0190, 0194, 0198 and 0206 (No. 88) was recorded with frame, page side and line. For 1811 there is no interlinear decode on most pages, but Ford printed the Department’s decipherments; 39 coded blocks of 1811 were transcribed from the film and aligned with Ford’s plaintext, group against syllable, and checked by eye. All the pairs, about 2,200 from the clerk and 860 from Ford, were merged into one table of 962 numbers, each with a grade:

GradeMeaningNumbers
Hthe clerk’s interlinear decode over the group391
Cfixed by Ford’s printed decipherment between known neighbours241
Mthe split of the syllables or the digit is a judgement311
Iinferred from the alphabetical slot and the sense only19

The code is a numbered syllabary of about 1,600 numbers: syllables, word-parts and common words, with 1–100 mostly whole words (13 observation, 16 Emperor, 29 United States, 44 negotiation, 78 President). An earlier version of this page described it as alphabetical blocks of fifty; the larger table does not bear that out. Values climb alphabetically only inside short runs, such as 1384 that, 1385 the, 1387 them, 1388 then, 1391 there, 1392 these, 1393 they, 1394 think, 1399 this, or 1592 on, 1594 one, 1595 ong, 1597 only, and then the run breaks (1304 tain and 1312 form sit among 1305 Bal, 1310 be, 1317 been). The alphabetical slot therefore brackets a value only where the neighbours on both sides are in order, and inference from it is kept to grade I. A superscript o after a group adds -s; a mark under a group changes it (a doubled letter, -ed, or another word). A few groups carry two values in the sources (1570 of and occurr; 1173 when and would).

In this hand 5 and 6, 7 and 9, and 0, 6 and 8 are easily confused. Re-reading No. 88 from zoomed crops corrected three groups (168, not 158; 1426, not 1425; 302, not 502) and showed that the underlined 1102 at the foot of the left page of frame 0206 is a catchword repeating the first group of the next page, not a group of the text. The table, its sources and the conflicts are in the repository (jqa/code_table.tsv, jqa/consolidate.py, jqa/code_conflicts.txt, jqa/overrides.tsv).

03 No. 88, 25 June 1812: the nine lines

No. 88 was written at St Petersburg on 25 June 1812, the day after the Grande Armée crossed the Niemen, when that news had not yet reached the capital. Ford prints it at 4:355–58, addressed to James Monroe. After Adams’s account of Prince Kurakin’s passports and the French ambassador’s demand for his, the paragraph ends: “On the other side I have reported to you what Count Romanzoff said to me the day before his departure for Wilna. Certainly negotiation then was his wish and expectation. [Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” The microfilm has the triplicate (M35 reel 3, frame 0206), and on it the clerk’s pencil stops exactly where Ford’s gap begins.

The block runs from the last four decoded groups to the end of the paragraph: 133 groups, 129 of them in Ford’s gap, with the clear words scarcely (twice), undoubtedly and In the German Gazettes written in the line. The rebuilt table gives every group a value (python render.py gap88.txt), and 127 of the 133 read as sense in the running text (python measure.py): 101 on clerk-attested values, 7 on Ford-fixed, 11 on uncertain and 8 on inferred ones; without the inferred values, 119 (0.895). In the reading below, words in square brackets rest on grade-I values; groups in braces have a value that does not make sense in the sentence. The opening words in brackets are the clerk-decoded close of the sentence that Ford prints.

[Certainly negotiation then was his wish] and expectation. But the [journ-ey] it-self of the Emperor Alex-an-der to Wil-na was in [opposition] to the Chan-ce-{last}-or and adv-ice, and if his ac-tive in-flu-ence had not been {im-pas-ion-ed} before it, it can scarcely fa-il to have been af-fect-ed {use} his ill-ness im-medi-ate-ly after his ar-riv-al [thither], which was undoubtedly an a-po-ple-ct-ic str-oke. Since then he has had a second and a more sever-e one. In the German Gazettes even his de-a-th has been an-no-un-ced; but that was a mis-take. At all events it is scarcely pos-sible that he should rem-ain much long-er in the depart-ment of foreign affairs.

{use} is group 492, attested four times as use or se (clerk 0182 and 0184; Ford’s blocks on 0066 and 0126); the sentence wants by, which is 792: probably Adams’s slip of a digit. {last} is 1457, which may be 1452 with the doubling mark (ll).

Since the first reading of 18 September, most of what was then inferred is now attested: itself (1081 self, clerk 0184, 0185, 0190), advice (1575 adv, Ford 0132; 1405 ice, Ford 0036 and clerk 0190), apoplectic (1141 ple; 1060 ct, clerk 0182), stroke (1586 oke), second (1077), severe (1091 sever), remain much longer (743 rem, 162 much, 888 long), mistake (134 mis, clerk 0182), announced (479 un), At all events (283 event, clerk 0048), foreign affairs (68, 1517), and 1186 is attested as will and Wil- (clerk 0182, 0184). Four groups of the passage remain inferred: 1434 journ and 168 ey, 83 opposition, 1398 thither. The word opposition carries the sense of the first sentence and rests on its slot (81 objection < 83 < 87 South) and on the sentence alone.

3.1 What it says

“He” is Count Nikolai Rumyantsev (Adams’s Romanzoff), Chancellor and minister for foreign affairs, who had gone to Wilna with Alexander. Adams reports that the Emperor’s journey to Wilna itself went against the Chancellor’s advice (the key word inferred); that whatever influence he still had would in any case have been affected by his illness, an apoplectic stroke soon after his arrival there; that since then he has had a second and worse one; that the German gazettes had reported him dead, wrongly; and that he can hardly remain much longer at the head of foreign affairs.

This fits what is known: Rumyantsev had a stroke in 1812 that cost him his hearing, and he left office in 1814. It differs on the timing. Standard accounts (Britannica, Wikipedia) tie the stroke to the news of the crossing of the Niemen on 24 June. Adams, writing at St Petersburg on 25 June before that news could have reached him, places the first stroke soon after Rumyantsev reached Wilna and a second, worse one before the 25th. Ford printed none of this.

04 The rest of No. 88

Ford ends the despatch with “[remainder of letter with exception of few lines undecyphered.]”. That remainder is on frame 0207, left leaf: thirteen lines of code, then clear sentences (manifestos of both parties expected; peace with Turkey signed a third time; “It is not General Kutuzoff but Count Rostopchin who is appointed Governor, civil and Military, of Moscow”), then five more lines of code. It has 257 groups, 7 of them with digits lost at the edge of the film; 246 have a value and 228 read as sense (0.887; 217 without the inferred values):

If this proposition has in any form been disclosed to the Russian government, it was certainly neither unreasonable nor im-[mod](erate) nor inconsistent with a system rigorously and {…} [de-fen-sive], to meet it with a proposition for the e-va-{?}-ation of Prussia by the French troops as {…} preliminary to negotiation. But the very point upon which the ambassador manifested the most sensibility in speaking of Prince Kurakin’s last note was that it held out a proposition with which it would be dishonourable in France to [comply]. He {…} it as a demonstration that the Emperor Alexander had determined not to ne-{…} — {…} — it {…} he {…} such a proposal as France had never thought of making was Russia even after the {…} of {…}.
[clear text] … an army of reserve is forming there, and it is {…} {…} great success on the side of the French should mark the commencement of the war, the Emperor Napoleon will attempt to pe-[net]-[rate] directly to that centre of the em-{…} {…} the side{s} of the Baltic.

The ambassador is Lauriston, the French ambassador at St Petersburg; Kurakin’s note is the Russian ambassador’s demand at Paris in April 1812 for the evacuation of Prussia, which Napoleon treated as a declaration of war. The groups are in jqa/rem88.txt, the groups not read as sense, with the reason for each, in jqa/sense88.tsv.

05 Erving 1807: the holes in Madison’s decode

George W. Erving, chargé at Madrid, wrote privately to Madison on 24 March 1807 (No. 21) “In the Cypher of the Legation”, the code Charles Pinckney had used in Spain. He reports a secret conversation between the Prince of the Peace (Godoy) and the Prussian chargé Henry: Bonaparte has offered the Prince the Electorate of Hanover for himself, and the Prince hopes by holding Hanover to make a separate peace with England and exchange it for Gibraltar. The Madison editors (PJM-SS 13:529–31) print the letter from Madison’s interlinear decode on the first received copy and state that the key has not been found (Weber, pp. 152–53). The decode has holes: “Haweder”, “Ha——er”, “Giblartar [sic]”, “see the he was more so”, “sp[eak]”, and at note 8 “to the […]” where Madison wrote “se al e”.

Three sources were read: the first received copy with Madison’s decode (NARA RG 59, Despatches from Spain vol. 10, microfilm M31 reel 12, frames 0283–0286); the “Duplicate” in the Madison Papers at the Library of Congress (mjm014714), coded throughout and not decoded; and Pinckney’s own despatches in the same code (M31 reel 7 frames 0346–0352, 22 February 1803, one copy decoded by Jacob Wagner on frame 0348; reel 8 frames 0083–0084, January 1804). Pinckney’s pages confirm the common values: 1651 the, 133 of, 1343 and, 244 to, 1578 that, 69 in (Wagner: “the In-tend-ant” = 1651.69.1586.103), 1515 late.

So every hole in the printed text closes, and the only real loss is Erving’s own dropped -eak. Madison had the key: the holes are his slips, not missing entries. The scale of this should be clear. The letter was already in print and legible; two of the corrections (that, speak) the editors had themselves suggested in their notes, and Hanover they had nearly established. What is new is in the scale, the proof that the “Haweder” and “Giblartar” forms are Madison’s and not Erving’s, and the demonstration that the Pinckney code is recoverable from the microfilm by the same method as Armstrong’s.

06 What remains open

Grades as in section 02: H clerk’s decode, C Ford-fixed, M uncertain, I inferred.

  • No. 88, the nine lines: groups that do not read. im-pas-ion-ed (1424 1112 1433 520): every group has a value, 1112 pas clerk-attested (H), but “if his active influence had not been impassioned before it” is not sense; either Adams coded another word or a group is misread. 492 use/se (M, attested four times) where by is wanted: probably Adams’s slip for 792. 1457 last in Chan-ce-?-or: perhaps 1452 with the doubling mark.
  • No. 88, the nine lines: inferred values (I). 1434 journ and 168 ey (“the journey itself”; the slot does not support 1434, the sense does), 83 opposition (the slot fits; the key word of the first sentence), 1398 thither. No clerk decode or Ford passage read so far contains them.
  • No. 88, the remainder. The word before defensive (350 289 1403 897, few-ex-table-ly: Ford values that give no sense; exclusively?); e-va-{1081}-ation, where the digit is blurred, and 1508 bable (M) where a pre- is wanted; 1185 (“he [took?] it as a demonstration”); 699 1535 570 in “ne-… — al — it tol he”; 1309 888 996 520 1453 416 (“after the … of …”, perhaps Poland); 220 586 1416 (“proper le the”, perhaps “probable that the greatest”); 927 1540 748 (“the em-…”, perhaps empire). Inferred there (I): 143 mod, 815 def, 239 sive, 108 comply, 432 net, 726 rate. Seven groups (142x, 21x, 15xx, 11xx, 1xxx, 55x, 13xx) lost digits at the edge of frame 0207; the duplicate or original of No. 88 was not found on reel 3.
  • The route to the open groups. Interlinear pages on reel 3 not yet read (frames 0047, 0056–57, 0211, 0228–29, 0233, 0247) and reel 2, and the 1812 coded blocks whose decipherments Ford prints in vol. 4 after p. 300, aligned as the 1811 blocks were. Each open group needs one more occurrence.
  • Ford’s other gaps. No. 51 (26 May 1811, “[One line and a half of cypher not decyphered]”, frame 0085, transcribed and partly rendered) and No. 55 (22 June 1811, “[one-half line of cipher not deciphered]”), both on reel 3; and reel 2 frame 0155 (1810), a long coded passage with no decode on the NARA copy.
  • The Forbes-collection letter. Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811, for which the editors name the Armstrong code, has not been checked against its manuscript.
  • Erving to Madison, 10 August 1807 (M31 reel 12, frames 0362–0366): several pages in the same code with no decode on the NARA copy. Erving wrote on 22 June 1807 that the Department could not read some of his letters. This is the next target for a rebuilt Pinckney key; whether Founders prints a decode (Early Access, 99-01-02 series) has still to be checked.
  • A full Pinckney key. The pairs used here come from this letter and two Pinckney pages. Pinckney’s decoded despatches of 1802–04 (Founders 02-03-02-0170, 02-03-02-0313, 02-04-02-0419, 02-04-02-0611, 02-06-02-0346 with Brent’s three-page decipherment, 02-06-02-0560, 02-07-02-0022) are on M31 reels 7 and 8 and should give a few hundred more pairs.
