Erving at Madrid and Adams at St Petersburg: Two Legation Codes

George W. Erving to Madison, Madrid, 24 March 1807, Founders Madison/02-13-02-0559 · John Quincy Adams to the Secretary of State, St Petersburg, No. 88, 25 June 1812 · NARA microfilm M31 reel 12 and M35 reel 3

The holes in Madison’s own decode of Erving’s letter closed from the two received copies and Pinckney’s despatches; Adams’s code shown not to be Armstrong’s and rebuilt to 1,166 numbers from the clerk’s decodes and Ford’s printed decipherments; No. 88, which Ford printed with nine lines and the remainder “not decyphered”, read: 380 of 390 groups as sense (0.974, measured), the nine lines in full

Summary. Two results from the same kind of source as the Armstrong postscript: the State Department’s despatch books on National Archives microfilm, where a reader in Washington sometimes wrote the decode between the lines. Adams. The Madison editors say that the Armstrong code (the = 972) was also used by John Quincy Adams at St Petersburg. His despatches to Washington are not in it. Every coded despatch checked on NARA M35 reel 3 uses a different code in which the is 1385 and of is 1576; Adams held a copy of Armstrong’s cipher, but for corresponding with Armstrong at Paris. That code is rebuilt here to 1,166 numbers: 431 from the Department clerk’s interlinear decodes on fourteen frames, 312 fixed by aligning coded blocks of 1811 and 1812 with the Department’s decipherments that Ford prints, 307 uncertain and 20 inferred. With it, despatch No. 88 (25 June 1812, the day after Napoleon crossed the Niemen), which Ford’s Writings of John Quincy Adams prints with “[Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” and the remainder “undecyphered”, reads 380 of its 390 groups as sense (0.974, measured): the nine lines all 133, the remainder 247 of 257 (0.961). The nine lines are about Count Rumyantsev, the Chancellor: the journey to Wilna went against his advice, he had an apoplectic stroke soon after arriving there and a second, worse one since, the German papers wrongly reported him dead, and he cannot stay much longer at the foreign ministry. The remainder is on Kurakin’s note, which the French ambassador called a proposal France “had never thought of making to Russia even after the battle of Friedland”, and on Napoleon’s expected thrust at the centre of the empire rather than along the Baltic. Erving. Erving’s private letter of 24 March 1807 is printed from Madison’s own decode, with the note that the key has not been found. Madison had the key; the holes in his decode are his own slips. With both received copies and Pinckney’s decoded despatches every hole closes (Hanover, in the scale, see that he was, Gibraltar), and the one real loss is a syllable Erving himself left out. Status: Erving’s letter complete, a small completion of a text already in print. Adams: No. 88 read (above the 95% bar; 0.928 without the inferred values), key rebuilt here. Open: seven groups cut by the binding gutter on the film, which need the original, and three single groups (1508, 1185, 570).

01 Adams: the premise checked

The starting point was a note the Madison editors attach to Armstrong’s letters (Founders Madison/02-10-02-0369 and 02-12-02-0197): the code was “also used by John Quincy Adams at St. Petersburg and by Jacob Lewis at Saint-Domingue; key not found but substantially reconstructed by the editors”. On Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811 (03-03-02-0121), the editors speak of “a code provided by the State Department to John Armstrong in France and to both William Short and Adams”, citing Ford 3:328. If that held, the 580-group Armstrong table already rebuilt on this site would read Adams’s coded despatches. It does not.

  • What Ford’s own text says. Secretary Smith’s instructions of 1809 (Ford 3:327–28) say that “the cypher with which you are furnished” is “the same with that of our minister at London” (William Pinkney), and tell Adams to “obtain at Paris, a copy of General Armstrong’s cypher also, for the like purpose”, that is, to correspond with Armstrong. In 1810 Adams acknowledges “the copy of General Armstrong’s cypher” (Ford 3:370). He held both. The despatches to Washington use the other one.
  • What the microfilm shows. Every coded despatch checked on M35 reel 3 (1811 and 1812) uses a code in which 1385 = the, 1576 = of, 1384 = that, 668 = and, 1401 = I. In Armstrong’s code the is 972 and of is 1354. Run through the Armstrong table (592 entries as it now stands), these pages come out as noise. Reel 2 frame 0155, of 1810, is in the same Adams code.

So the table already held cannot read Adams’s despatches. The editors’ note on the 7 January 1811 letter concerns a private letter to Madison, not a despatch, and was not checked against its manuscript, which is in the Forbes collection and not online; it may be right for that letter.

02 Adams’s code rebuilt

The method is the one used for Armstrong on M34 roll 13, with a second source added. On M35 reel 3 the Department clerk wrote the decode above the groups of several despatches; every pair on frames 0047, 0048 (1811), 0182 (No. 80, 29 February 1812), 0183, 0184, 0185, 0189, 0190, 0194, 0198, 0206 (No. 88), 0210–0211 and 0247 was recorded with frame, page side and line. For most pages there is no interlinear decode, but Ford printed the Department’s decipherments. Coded blocks were transcribed from the film and aligned with Ford’s plaintext, group against syllable, and checked by eye: 39 blocks of 1811, and then eight despatches of October 1811 to September 1812, No. 95 against the Department’s own clear copy on frames 0230–0232. All the pairs, 3,127 from the clerk (with the earlier pair list) and 2,785 from Ford, were merged into one table of 1,166 numbers, each with a grade:

GradeMeaningNumbers

The code is a numbered syllabary of about 1,600 numbers: syllables, word-parts and common words, with 1–100 mostly whole words (13 observation, 16 Emperor, 29 United States, 44 negotiation, 78 President). An earlier version of this page described it as alphabetical blocks of fifty; the larger table does not bear that out. Values climb alphabetically only inside short runs, such as 1384 that, 1385 the, 1387 them, 1388 then, 1391 there, 1392 these, 1393 they, 1394 think, 1399 this, or 1592 on, 1594 one, 1595 ong, 1597 only, and then the run breaks (1304 tain and 1312 form sit among 1305 Bal, 1310 be, 1317 been). The alphabetical slot therefore brackets a value only where the neighbours on both sides are in order, and inference from it is kept to grade I. A superscript o after a group adds -s; a mark under a group changes it (a doubled letter, -ed, or another word: 1107 paper, 1107 with the mark well; 1457 last, with the mark ll; 1535 al, with the mark all). A few groups carry two values in the sources (508 rect and ear; 1570 of and occurr; 1173 when and would).

In this hand 5 and 6, 7 and 9, and 0, 6 and 8 are easily confused. Re-reading No. 88 from zoomed crops corrected seven groups over two passes (168, not 158; 1426, not 1425; 302, not 502; 792, not 492; 1061, not 1081; 588, not 888; 1384, not 1385), showed that two groups of the last line are digits Adams himself overwrote (1383 than and 1097 sho, each with its last digit written over another), and showed that the underlined 1102 at the foot of the left page of frame 0206 is a catchword repeating the first group of the next page, not a group of the text. The table, its sources and the conflicts are in the repository (jqa/code_table.tsv, jqa/consolidate.py, jqa/code_conflicts.txt, jqa/overrides.tsv, where every hand decision carries its reason).

03 No. 88, 25 June 1812: the nine lines

No. 88 was written at St Petersburg on 25 June 1812, the day after the Grande Armée crossed the Niemen, when that news had not yet reached the capital. Ford prints it at 4:355–58, addressed to James Monroe. After Adams’s account of Prince Kurakin’s passports and the French ambassador’s demand for his, the paragraph ends: “On the other side I have reported to you what Count Romanzoff said to me the day before his departure for Wilna. Certainly negotiation then was his wish and expectation. [Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” The microfilm has the triplicate (M35 reel 3, frame 0206), and on it the clerk’s pencil stops exactly where Ford’s gap begins.

The block runs from the last four decoded groups to the end of the paragraph: 133 groups, 129 of them in Ford’s gap, with the clear words scarcely (twice), undoubtedly and In the German Gazettes written in the line. The rebuilt table gives every group a value (python render.py gap88.txt), and all 133 read as sense in the running text (python measure.py): 106 on clerk-attested values, 10 on Ford-fixed, 13 on uncertain and 4 on inferred ones; without the inferred values, 129 (0.970). In the reading below, words in square brackets rest on grade-I values; groups in braces have a value that does not make sense in the sentence. The opening words in brackets are the clerk-decoded close of the sentence that Ford prints.

[Certainly negotiation then was his wish] and ex-pect-ation. But the [journ-ey] it-self of the Emperor Alex-an-der to Wil-na was in opposition to the Chan-ce-ll-or and adv-ice, and if his ac-tive in-flu-ence had not been im-pa-ir-ed before it, it can scarcely fa-il to have been af-fect-ed by his ill-ness im-medi-ate-ly after his ar-[riv]-al [thither], which was undoubtedly an a-po-ple-ct-ic str-oke. Since then he has had a second and a more sever-e one. In the German Gazettes even his de-a-[th] has been an-no-un-ced; but that was a mis-take. At all events it is scarcely pos-sible that he should rem-ain much long-er in the depart-ment of foreign affairs.

im-pa-ir-ed: the groups on the page are 1424, then the catchword 1102 at the foot of the left leaf, then 1112 1433 520 at the top of the right leaf. The catchword is the group Adams meant to begin the page with, 1102 pa (Ford-fixed nine times), and in No. 83 (frame 0190) he codes un-im-pa-ir-ed as 1477 1424 1102 1435 520 (1435 ir, Ford-fixed three times). The 1112 1433 on the new page are his own copying slips, one digit each, for 1102 1435: read as he intended, impaired. Chan-ce-ll-or is 1385 1025 1016 1457 908 with the doubling mark under 1457, the same five groups Adams wrote for “the Chancellor” in No. 73 (frame 0165, Ford 4:256). The group before his illness is 792 by: the earlier reading 492 use took an open hook for this hand’s looped 4.

Most of what the first reading of 18 September inferred is now attested: itself (1081 self, clerk 0184, 0185, 0190), advice (1575 adv, Ford 0132; 1405 ice, Ford 0036 and clerk 0190), apoplectic (1141 ple; 1060 ct, clerk 0182), stroke (1586 oke), second (1077), severe (1091 sever), remain much longer (743 rem, 162 much, 888 long), mistake (134 mis, clerk 0182), announced (479 un), At all events (283 event, clerk 0048), foreign affairs (68, 1517), and 1186 is attested as will and Wil- (clerk 0182, 0184). Opposition (83), the key word of the first sentence, is now grade C from No. 73. Five groups of the passage remain inferred: 1434 journ and 168 ey, 1479 riv, 1398 thither and 1382 th.

3.1 What it says

“He” is Count Nikolai Rumyantsev (Adams’s Romanzoff), Chancellor and minister for foreign affairs, who had gone to Wilna with Alexander. Adams reports that the Emperor’s journey to Wilna itself went against the Chancellor’s advice; that whatever influence he still had would in any case have been affected by his illness, an apoplectic stroke soon after his arrival there; that since then he has had a second and worse one; that the German gazettes had reported him dead, wrongly; and that he can hardly remain much longer at the head of foreign affairs.

This fits what is known: Rumyantsev had a stroke in 1812 that cost him his hearing, and he left office in 1814. Adams’s own diary for September 1812 records Romanzoff speaking of two strokes of apoplexy suffered with the Emperor at Wilna. It differs on the timing from standard accounts (Britannica, Wikipedia), which tie the stroke to the news of the crossing of the Niemen on 24 June. Adams, writing at St Petersburg on 25 June before that news could have reached him, places the first stroke soon after Rumyantsev reached Wilna and a second, worse one before the 25th. Ford printed none of this.

04 The rest of No. 88

Ford ends the despatch with “[remainder of letter with exception of few lines undecyphered.]”. That remainder is on frame 0207, left leaf: thirteen lines of code, then clear sentences (manifestos of both parties expected; peace with Turkey signed a third time; “It is not General Kutuzoff but Count Rostopchin who is appointed Governor, civil and Military, of Moscow”), then five more lines of code. It has 257 groups, 7 of them with digits lost in the binding gutter at the line ends; 249 have a value and 247 read as sense (0.961): 195 clerk-attested, 20 Ford-fixed, 17 uncertain, 15 inferred; 232 (0.903) without the inferred values. Square brackets are grade I, braces a value that does not read, and angle brackets a gutter-cut group restored from the context only, which is counted as unread:

But the very point upon which the am-bass-⟨ad⟩-or manifest-ed the most sen-si-bi-li-ty in sp-ea-king of Prince Kurakin’s last no-te was that it held out a pro-po-⟨si⟩(tion) with which it would be dis-hon-(our)able in France to [comply]. He {1185} it as a [dem]-on-str-ation that the Emperor Al-ex-an-der had de-ter-[min]-ed not to [negotiate] at all — it {tol} he ⟨sa⟩-id — such a pro-po-sal as France had never thought of making to Russia even after the [bat]-tle of Fri-ed-la-nd.
[clear text] … an army of re-serve is form-ing there, and it is ⟨…⟩ probab-le that if great suc-cess on the, on the side of the French should mark the commence-ment of the war, the Emperor Na-po-le-on will at-⟨tem⟩-p-t to pe-[net]-[rate] di-rect-ly to that cen-(t)re of the em-[pire] than [along] the sho-res of the Baltic.

The ambassador is Lauriston, the French ambassador at St Petersburg; Kurakin’s note is the Russian ambassador’s demand at Paris in April 1812 for the evacuation of Prussia, which Napoleon treated as a declaration of war. “Even after the battle of Friedland” (June 1807, before Tilsit): France had never proposed evacuating Prussia even when Russia was beaten. The last lines expect Napoleon, if the war opens well for him, to strike directly at the centre of the empire rather than along the Baltic shore. The groups are in jqa/rem88.txt, the groups not read as sense, with the reason for each, in jqa/sense88.tsv.

05 Erving 1807: the holes in Madison’s decode

George W. Erving, chargé at Madrid, wrote privately to Madison on 24 March 1807 (No. 21) “In the Cypher of the Legation”, the code Charles Pinckney had used in Spain. He reports a secret conversation between the Prince of the Peace (Godoy) and the Prussian chargé Henry: Bonaparte has offered the Prince the Electorate of Hanover for himself, and the Prince hopes by holding Hanover to make a separate peace with England and exchange it for Gibraltar. The Madison editors (PJM-SS 13:529–31) print the letter from Madison’s interlinear decode on the first received copy and state that the key has not been found (Weber, pp. 152–53). The decode has holes: “Haweder”, “Ha——er”, “Giblartar [sic]”, “see the he was more so”, “sp[eak]”, and at note 8 “to the […]” where Madison wrote “se al e”.

Three sources were read: the first received copy with Madison’s decode (NARA RG 59, Despatches from Spain vol. 10, microfilm M31 reel 12, frames 0283–0286); the “Duplicate” in the Madison Papers at the Library of Congress (mjm014714), coded throughout and not decoded; and Pinckney’s own despatches in the same code (M31 reel 7 frames 0346–0352, 22 February 1803, one copy decoded by Jacob Wagner on frame 0348; reel 8 frames 0083–0084, January 1804). Pinckney’s pages confirm the common values: 1651 the, 133 of, 1343 and, 244 to, 1578 that, 69 in (Wagner: “the In-tend-ant” = 1651.69.1586.103), 1515 late.

So every hole in the printed text closes, and the only real loss is Erving’s own dropped -eak. Madison had the key: the holes are his slips, not missing entries. The scale of this should be clear. The letter was already in print and legible; two of the corrections (that, speak) the editors had themselves suggested in their notes, and Hanover they had nearly established. What is new is in the scale, the proof that the “Haweder” and “Giblartar” forms are Madison’s and not Erving’s, and the demonstration that the Pinckney code is recoverable from the microfilm by the same method as Armstrong’s.

06 What remains open

Grades as in section 02: H clerk’s decode, C Ford-fixed, M uncertain, I inferred. No. 88 is read; the fourteen groups below are what keeps it from 100%.

  • Seven gutter-cut groups (needs the original). 142x, 21x, 15xx, 11xx, 10xx, 55x, 13xx: the last group of seven lines of the remainder lost digits in the binding gutter on the microfilm of frame 0207. Context restores most of them (in-, pre-, sa-id, at-tem-p-t), but they are counted unread. The original and the duplicate of No. 88 are not on reel 3, and Adams’s letterbook copy at the Massachusetts Historical Society is not online; with either the letter would be at about 98%.
  • 1508, remainder l. 5. le/bable (M) where “as a pre[liminary]” wants a; no other occurrence in the decoded pairs.
  • 1185, remainder l. 9. “He [took?] it as a demonstration”: in no decoded frame or Ford block read so far.
  • 570, remainder l. 11. Valued tol (C) but it does not read in “it tol he said”; the image shows 570, not 500 was.
  • Inferred values (I) that the reading rests on. Nine lines: 1434 journ, 168 ey, 1479 riv, 1398 thither, 1382 th. Remainder: 1864 tain, 143 mod, 350 fl, 1403 ib, 815 def, 239 sive, 108 comply, 821 dem, 549 min, 426 negotiate, 1309 bat, 432 net, 726 rate, 927 pire, 1540 along. Without them No. 88 reads 362 of 390 (0.928).
  • The route to the open groups. 1185 and 1508 each need one more occurrence: the 1812 blocks of Nos. 77, 78 and 81 and the remaining lines of No. 85 (frame 0196, left, lines 12–23) are not yet aligned with Ford. Frames 0056–57 turned out to be a clear decyphered copy, and 0228–0233 No. 95 in code only.
  • Ford’s other gaps. No. 51 (26 May 1811, “[One line and a half of cypher not decyphered]”, frame 0085, transcribed and partly rendered) and No. 55 (22 June 1811, “[one-half line of cipher not deciphered]”), both on reel 3; and reel 2 frame 0155 (1810), a long coded passage with no decode on the NARA copy.
  • The Forbes-collection letter. Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811, for which the editors name the Armstrong code, has not been checked against its manuscript.
  • Erving to Madison, 10 August 1807 (M31 reel 12, frames 0362–0366): several pages in the same code with no decode on the NARA copy. Erving wrote on 22 June 1807 that the Department could not read some of his letters. This is the next target for a rebuilt Pinckney key; whether Founders prints a decode (Early Access, 99-01-02 series) has still to be checked.
  • A full Pinckney key. The pairs used here come from this letter and two Pinckney pages. Pinckney’s decoded despatches of 1802–04 (Founders 02-03-02-0170, 02-03-02-0313, 02-04-02-0419, 02-04-02-0611, 02-06-02-0346 with Brent’s three-page decipherment, 02-06-02-0560, 02-07-02-0022) are on M31 reels 7 and 8 and should give a few hundred more pairs.
