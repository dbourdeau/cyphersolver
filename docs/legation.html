Erving at Madrid and Adams at St Petersburg: Two Legation Codes

George W. Erving to Madison, Madrid, 24 March 1807, Founders Madison/02-13-02-0559 · John Quincy Adams to the Secretary of State, St Petersburg, No. 88, 25 June 1812 · NARA microfilm M31 reel 12 and M35 reel 3

The holes in Madison’s own decode of Erving’s letter closed from the two received copies and Pinckney’s despatches; Adams’s code shown not to be Armstrong’s, rebuilt from the clerk’s decodes, and the nine lines Ford printed as “not decyphered” read

Summary. Two small results from the same kind of source as the Armstrong postscript: the State Department’s despatch books on National Archives microfilm, where a reader in Washington sometimes wrote the decode between the lines. Adams. The Madison editors say that the Armstrong code (the = 972) was also used by John Quincy Adams at St Petersburg. His despatches to Washington are not in it. Every coded despatch checked on NARA M35 reel 3 uses a different code in which the is 1385 and of is 1576; Adams held a copy of Armstrong’s cipher, but for corresponding with Armstrong at Paris. That code is rebuilt here to some 370 groups from the Department clerk’s interlinear decodes, and with it the passage of No. 88 (25 June 1812, the day after Napoleon crossed the Niemen) that Ford’s Writings of John Quincy Adams prints as “[Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” reads, 124 of 134 groups. It is about Count Rumyantsev, the Chancellor: an apoplectic stroke soon after he reached Wilna, a second and worse one since, a false report of his death in the German papers, and Adams’s judgment that he cannot stay much longer at the foreign ministry. Erving. Erving’s private letter of 24 March 1807 is printed from Madison’s own decode, with the note that the key has not been found. Madison had the key; the holes in his decode are his own slips. With both received copies and Pinckney’s decoded despatches every hole closes (Hanover, in the scale, see that he was, Gibraltar), and the one real loss is a syllable Erving himself left out. Status: Erving’s letter complete, a small completion of a text already in print. Adams: one passage read, not a whole despatch; ten groups in it unread and 27 filled by inference; the rest of the despatch renders only in threads.

01 Adams: the premise checked

The starting point was a note the Madison editors attach to Armstrong’s letters (Founders Madison/02-10-02-0369 and 02-12-02-0197): the code was “also used by John Quincy Adams at St. Petersburg and by Jacob Lewis at Saint-Domingue; key not found but substantially reconstructed by the editors”. On Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811 (03-03-02-0121), the editors speak of “a code provided by the State Department to John Armstrong in France and to both William Short and Adams”, citing Ford 3:328. If that held, the 580-group Armstrong table already rebuilt on this site would read Adams’s coded despatches. It does not.

  • What Ford’s own text says. Secretary Smith’s instructions of 1809 (Ford 3:327–28) say that “the cypher with which you are furnished” is “the same with that of our minister at London” (William Pinkney), and tell Adams to “obtain at Paris, a copy of General Armstrong’s cypher also, for the like purpose”, that is, to correspond with Armstrong. In 1810 Adams acknowledges “the copy of General Armstrong’s cypher” (Ford 3:370). He held both. The despatches to Washington use the other one.
  • What the microfilm shows. Every coded despatch checked on M35 reel 3 (1812: frames 0182, 0189–0190, 0206–0207, 0211) uses a code in which 1385 = the, 1576 = of, 1384 = that, 668 = and, 1401 = I. In Armstrong’s code the is 972 and of is 1354. Run through the Armstrong table (592 entries as it now stands), these pages come out as noise. Reel 2 frame 0155, of 1810, is in the same Adams code.

So the table already held cannot read Adams’s despatches. The editors’ note on the 7 January 1811 letter concerns a private letter to Madison, not a despatch, and was not checked against its manuscript, which is in the Forbes collection and not online; it may be right for that letter.

02 Adams’s code rebuilt

The method is the one used for Armstrong on M34 roll 13. On M35 reel 3 the Department clerk wrote the decode above the groups of several 1812 despatches; frames 0206 (No. 88) and 0189 were read pair by pair and every pair recorded with frame, page side and line, and a grade: H the clerk’s interlinear decode, M aligned but uncertain, I inferred from the code’s structure and the context. The table now has about 370 distinct groups, 252 of them with at least one clerk-attested reading.

The structure makes inference strong. The code is an alphabetical syllabary cut into blocks of about fifty numbers; the blocks are shuffled, and inside a block the order is alphabetical:

A group that has not been seen decoded can therefore be pinned between its neighbours, as for Armstrong: 1186 lies between 1184 wish and 1188 Wil, and in No. 88 it stands before na after “the Emperor Alexander to”, so it is read Wil (a homophone). A few recorded readings sit out of alphabetical order (1304 tain in the b- block, 1395 strong among the th- words); they are either misreadings of the pencil or irregularities of the code and are not yet explained. The pair list and a renderer are in the repository (jqa/pairs_jqa.txt, jqa/render.py).

03 No. 88, 25 June 1812: the nine lines

No. 88 was written at St Petersburg on 25 June 1812, the day after the Grande Armée crossed the Niemen, when that news had not yet reached the capital. Ford prints it at 4:355–58, addressed to James Monroe. After Adams’s account of Prince Kurakin’s passports and the French ambassador’s demand for his, the paragraph ends: “On the other side I have reported to you what Count Romanzoff said to me the day before his departure for Wilna. Certainly negotiation then was his wish and expectation. [Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” The microfilm has the triplicate (M35 reel 3, frame 0206), and on it the clerk’s pencil stops exactly where Ford’s gap begins.

The block runs from the last four decoded groups to the end of the paragraph: 134 groups, 130 of them in Ford’s gap, with the clear words scarcely (twice), undoubtedly and In the German Gazettes written in the line. With the rebuilt table 124 of the 134 render (python render.py gap88.txt). In the reading below, syllables in square brackets are slot inferences that fit the block and the context but are not attested by the clerk; groups still unread are given as {number}. The opening words in brackets are the clerk-decoded close of the sentence that Ford prints.

[Certainly negotiation then was his wish] and expectation. But the {1434} {168} it {1081} of the Emperor [Alex]an[der] to Wilna was im-{1425} {83} to the [Chan]cel[lor], and {1575} {1405}, and [if] his [active] influ[ence] had not been im[paired] {1112}-tion-ed before it, [it can] scarcely [fail] to have been [af]fected {by} his ill[ness] im[medi]ately [af]ter his ar[riv]al [thither], which was undoubtedly an apo[plec]tic {1060} s-tr-[oke]. Since then he has had a [second] and a more [seve]re one. In the German Gazettes even his [death] has been an-no-[un]ced; but that was a [mis]take. At all [events] it is scarcely possible that he should [rem]ain [much] [long]er in the department of [foreign] [affairs].

{by} is group 492, unread in the table; by is what the sentence requires.

Of the 130 groups in the gap, 81 have values attested by the clerk’s decodes elsewhere on the frames, 12 are aligned but uncertain, 27 are inferred from their slot, and 10 are unread. The skeleton rests on attested groups: his, illness, immediately, arrival, which was undoubtedly, stroke, since then he has had a, and a more, one, In the German Gazettes, even his, has been, but that was a, take, it is scarcely possible that he should, in the department of. The first sentences, where four of the ten unread groups fall, are the least certain part; the account of the strokes and of the false report of death is the most secure.

3.1 What it says

“He” is Count Nikolai Rumyantsev (Adams’s Romanzoff), Chancellor and minister for foreign affairs, who had gone to Wilna with Alexander. Adams reports that soon after arriving there Rumyantsev had an apoplectic stroke; that since then he has had a second and worse one; that the German gazettes had reported him dead, wrongly; and that he can hardly remain much longer at the head of foreign affairs. The first part of the passage, less securely read, seems to say that whatever influence the Chancellor had for negotiation was impaired by his illness.

This fits what is known: Rumyantsev had a stroke in 1812 that cost him his hearing, and he left office in 1814. It differs on the timing. Standard accounts (Britannica, Wikipedia) tie the stroke to the news of the crossing of the Niemen on 24 June. Adams, writing at St Petersburg on 25 June before that news could have reached him, places the first stroke soon after Rumyantsev reached Wilna and a second, worse one before the 25th. Ford printed none of this.

04 The rest of No. 88

Ford ends the despatch with “[remainder of letter with exception of few lines undecyphered.]”. That remainder is on frame 0207, left leaf: thirteen lines of code, then clear sentences (manifestos of both parties expected; peace with Turkey signed a third time; “It is not General Kutuzoff but Count Rostopchin who is appointed Governor, civil and Military, of Moscow”), then five more lines of code. Of its 250 groups 210 render, which gives readable threads and not yet a clean text: the evacuation of Prussia by the French troops as a preliminary to negotiation, Prince Kurakin’s last note, a proposition with which “it would be dishonourable in France” to comply, taken as a demonstration that the Emperor Alexander had determined not to negotiate; and after the Moscow sentence, an army of reserve forming there and the Emperor Napoleon’s plan to penetrate directly on the side of the Baltic. These are given as threads only; the groups are in jqa/rem88.txt.

05 Erving 1807: the holes in Madison’s decode

George W. Erving, chargé at Madrid, wrote privately to Madison on 24 March 1807 (No. 21) “In the Cypher of the Legation”, the code Charles Pinckney had used in Spain. He reports a secret conversation between the Prince of the Peace (Godoy) and the Prussian chargé Henry: Bonaparte has offered the Prince the Electorate of Hanover for himself, and the Prince hopes by holding Hanover to make a separate peace with England and exchange it for Gibraltar. The Madison editors (PJM-SS 13:529–31) print the letter from Madison’s interlinear decode on the first received copy and state that the key has not been found (Weber, pp. 152–53). The decode has holes: “Haweder”, “Ha——er”, “Giblartar [sic]”, “see the he was more so”, “sp[eak]”, and at note 8 “to the […]” where Madison wrote “se al e”.

Three sources were read: the first received copy with Madison’s decode (NARA RG 59, Despatches from Spain vol. 10, microfilm M31 reel 12, frames 0283–0286); the “Duplicate” in the Madison Papers at the Library of Congress (mjm014714), coded throughout and not decoded; and Pinckney’s own despatches in the same code (M31 reel 7 frames 0346–0352, 22 February 1803, one copy decoded by Jacob Wagner on frame 0348; reel 8 frames 0083–0084, January 1804). Pinckney’s pages confirm the common values: 1651 the, 133 of, 1343 and, 244 to, 1578 that, 69 in (Wagner: “the In-tend-ant” = 1651.69.1586.103), 1515 late.

So every hole in the printed text closes, and the only real loss is Erving’s own dropped -eak. Madison had the key: the holes are his slips, not missing entries. The scale of this should be clear. The letter was already in print and legible; two of the corrections (that, speak) the editors had themselves suggested in their notes, and Hanover they had nearly established. What is new is in the scale, the proof that the “Haweder” and “Giblartar” forms are Madison’s and not Erving’s, and the demonstration that the Pinckney code is recoverable from the microfilm by the same method as Armstrong’s.

06 What remains open

  • Erving to Madison, 10 August 1807 (M31 reel 12, frames 0362–0366): several pages in the same code with no decode on the NARA copy. Erving wrote on 22 June 1807 that the Department could not read some of his letters. This is the next target for a rebuilt Pinckney key; whether Founders prints a decode (Early Access, 99-01-02 series) has still to be checked.
  • A full Pinckney key. The pairs used here come from this letter and two Pinckney pages. Pinckney’s decoded despatches of 1802–04 (Founders 02-03-02-0170, 02-03-02-0313, 02-04-02-0419, 02-04-02-0611, 02-06-02-0346 with Brent’s three-page decipherment, 02-06-02-0560, 02-07-02-0022) are on M31 reels 7 and 8 and should give a few hundred more pairs.
  • Adams’s unread groups. Ten groups of the No. 88 passage ({1434}, {168}, {1081}, {1425}, {83}, {1575}, {1405}, {1112}, {492}, {1060}) and the 27 inferred syllables. More clerk-decoded pages (reel 3 frames 0182–0185, 0190, 0194–0199, 0211; reel 2 for 1810–11) should settle most of them, and the remainder page 0207.
  • Ford’s other gaps. No. 51 (26 May 1811, “[One line and a half of cypher not decyphered]”) and No. 55 (22 June 1811, “[one-half line of cipher not deciphered]”), both on reel 3; and reel 2 frame 0155 (1810), a long coded passage with no decode on the NARA copy.
  • The Forbes-collection letter. Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811, for which the editors name the Armstrong code, has not been checked against its manuscript.
