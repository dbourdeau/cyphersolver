San Antonio → Sheridan and Russell · word route transposition · 9–17 January 1874

Augur’s cipher telegrams, Texas, 1874

→

Go to the full text ↓

Four Army telegrams on the Davis–Coke crisis, copied in cipher into a general’s letter-book with no decipherment; the column routes were recovered here from the words alone, and all but seven code words now read.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 29 October 2026

The documents
Four telegrams from Brig. Gen. C. C. Augur, commanding the Department of Texas at San Antonio: two to Lt. Gen. Sheridan in Chicago (9 and 12 January 1874) and two to Major G. B. Russell in Austin (12 and 17 January).
Where
Newberry Library, Chicago, Ayer MS 3008 (C. C. Augur papers), box 02, letter-book pp. 84–85, digitised free in Newberry Digital Collections.
The cipher
Word transposition: the words of the message are written in rows and taken off down or up the columns in a set route; a city name in front (Baltic, Dresden) names the route; a few code words stand for names, and Texas place names fill the last column.
How it was read
A word-pair model of English scored every column order and direction; the four routes came out of the cipher words alone. An 1869 letter in the Grierson papers explains the method but not Augur’s tables.
What it says
Augur reports the Texas Supreme Court ruling and Governor Davis’s request for troops, and declines to act “without orders from the President”; he later tells Russell he need not recognize either governor.
Still open
Seven code words in the telegram of 12 January to Sheridan (nun, scatter, faint, table, novel, fussy, self).

01 The telegrams

Christopher Columbus Augur commanded the Department of Texas from San Antonio in 1872–75. His press letter-book for 1873–74 survives among his papers at the Newberry Library. Most entries are in clear. On pages 84 and 85 the clerk copied four telegrams of January 1874 as he received them from the cipher operator: rows of English words in no grammatical order, with no decipherment written anywhere near them.

Telegram 22 ends with a clear line, “Am I correct, please reply.” No other page of the Augur papers holds cipher: all 842 transcribed pages were scored for scrambled word order, and only these two pages stood out.

02 The cipher

The method is explained in a letter in Benjamin Grierson’s papers, also at the Newberry (Ayer MS 3039, box 3 f. 153). On 18 February 1869 Lieutenant L. V. Caziare, at the headquarters of the Fifth Military District in Austin, told the commander of Fort Concho that “your plan of setting up cypher is not correct” and worked an example through. The message is written in lines of four to nine words, code words (“arbitraries”) from the key replace names and phrases, an indicator word names one of the key’s route tables, and the columns are taken in the table’s order, some down and some up, with a check word after each. The letter gives only the example, not the tables.

Augur’s telegrams use the same family without the check words. Seen from the receiving end: write the cipher words in rows of seven (or nine), and the message runs down or up the columns.

  • Telegrams 30 and 45 (no indicator): seven columns, taken 1 down, 7 up, 4 down, 6 up, 2 down, 5 up, 3 down.
  • Telegram 22 (indicator Baltic): nine columns, all down, in the order 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, 7, 5, 3, 1.
  • Telegram 29 (indicator Dresden): seven columns, 1 down, 7 up, 6 up, 2 down, 5 up, 4 down, 3 down.

The last column ends in place names that fill the grid: Waco, Denison, Houston; Brownsville; Houston, Nueces, Cheyenne.

03 The reading

Word order as the routes give it; punctuation and capitals added. Square brackets mark code words left as written, with a guess after “=?”.

22. To Sheridan, 9 January 1874 (Baltic)

The Supreme Court of the State has decided the law under which the late election was held here unconstitutional. Governor Davis anticipates, however, that the legislature and governor then elected will meet in Austin on Tuesday next and attempt by force to get possession of the State government. The [He?] asks me to prevent this by use of United States troops. I decline to act without orders from the President [written “precedent”], who by law is made judge as to when United States troops are to interfere in the troubles of a State. (In clear:) Am I correct, please reply.

29. To Sheridan, 12 January 1874 (Dresden)

I have just received this following dispatch from [nun =? Governor] Davis with request to forward: “[scatter =? President] Grant, [faint]: as I anticipate unauthorized persons may attempt to [table =? take] possession of the state [novel =? capitol] buildings, I request [fussy]” — it, as he has no cypher to [self] that secure.

30. To Russell, 12 January 1874

I [written “In”] am informed through [written “though”] General Sheridan that the President has no instructions to give on the subject, but that if any change occurs I will have immediate notice. I have sent the Governor’s dispatch.

45. To Russell, 17 January 1874

It seems to me if Governor Davis yields to force without armed collision it is all that can be expected of him. Under the circumstances it is evident neither the President nor the party desire trouble. You need not specially recognize either governor. You can return to-morrow unless your further stay there is desirable. Instruct Sellers not to interfere in any way without orders from me.

The telegrams follow the end of Reconstruction in Texas day by day. The Republican governor, Edmund J. Davis, had lost the December 1873 election to Richard Coke; the State Supreme Court struck down the election law; the new legislature met in Austin on Tuesday 13 January and installed Coke while Davis held the ground floor of the Capitol. Grant refused Davis’s requests for troops, and Davis left office on 19 January. Augur’s first telegram already states the position the President took: federal troops would not decide the governorship.

04 Method

A model of English letters failed even on the 1869 worked example. A model of English word pairs, built from the project’s Gutenberg corpus, did better: a search over every order and direction of the columns recovered the example’s five columns and its row text. On telegram 45 the columns of a seven-word grid came out as English phrases at once (“It seems to me if governor Davis yields to force”, “without armed collision it is all that can be Expected”), and the search put them in order. Telegram 30 then read with the same route unchanged. Scoring every transcribed Augur page with the same model found telegram 22 on the facing page. There “Baltic” stood outside the grid; that suggested “Dresden” did too in telegram 29, which then fell into a seven-by-seven grid once “I. I.” was counted as two words. route.py in the target folder holds the four routes and prints the reading.

05 What remains uncertain

  • Telegram 29: seven code words, each once: nun (Governor?), scatter (President?), faint, table (take?), novel (capitol?), fussy, self. The Department’s list of arbitraries is not in the Augur or Grierson papers. The closing clause (“as he has no cypher to [self] that secure”) is uncertain.
  • Telegram 22: “The asks me” is probably the clerk’s slip for “He asks me”; “precedent” for President. Telegram 30: “In” for I, “though” for through.
  • Sheridan’s headquarters deciphered these at the time; his received copies, in the Division of the Missouri records, were not looked for and could settle the code words.
