… silver-inlaid letters on a sword blade, Alphen aan den Rijn, 12th–13th century

Alphen aan den Rijn · two faces of inlaid letters, an invocation in abbreviations · 12th to early 13th century

The Alphen aan den Rijn sword inscription

A sword blade found in the Rhine bed at Alphen in 2002 carries a line of silver-inlaid letters on each face. It belongs to a family of sibling blades; read against its siblings it is an invocation of Sanctus Dominus Christus and a saint (Martinius, perhaps Mauricius), and the rest of the letters have no accepted reading.

At a glanceNot solved · text not obtained

The document
Engraved, silver-inlaid capital letters on both faces of a sword blade, 285 mm per face, between crosses.
Where
Found in 2002 in the Rhine bed at Alphen aan den Rijn, in a layer later than the damming of the Kromme Rijn in 1122; Provincial Depot Bodemvondsten Zuid-Holland no. 8888. Script of the 12th or early 13th century.
The cipher
None shown. The text is an invocation in abbreviations (SCS, IS, X, DIC) and name-like groups, the same formula family as nine other blades.
How it was tried
The drawing from the published photographs, letter counts, an alignment of both faces to the published readings of the sibling blades, and a language check.
Why it resists
56 of 79 letters fall in recognised formula elements, but those expansions are the sibling authors’ tentative ones, and F T, RR, EC, NI, ICCC and the DIO groups have none.
Credit
Marc van Hasselt for the transcription; Thomas Wagner, John Worley, Anna Holst Blennow and Gunilla Beckholmen for the family readings applied here.
Still open
Everything outside the formula elements, and whether the formula itself means what they suggest.

01 The inscription

Marc van Hasselt prints the two faces in Madoc 28/2 (2014), drawn from Restaura’s photographs:

+BENEDOXOFTISSCSDRRISCDICECMTINIUSCSDNI+
+DIOXMTINIUSESDIOMTINIUSCSDICCCMTDICII7ISI+

The 7 is as printed and reads like a Z. The inlay is corroded, and earlier forum transcriptions (Peters, 2011) differ in several letters, so the drawing is used here. The published photographs show the letters but are not freely licensed, so none is reproduced. The vowel rate (32% and 41%) shows this is not an initials-only string, unlike the Witham sword.

02 The sibling blades

Eight other blades were used here: Karlstad, Fyris UMF/B 74, W 1830, W 897, W 879, Marburg, Saint-Omer and one from Finland. Wagner, Worley, Holst Blennow and Beckholmen (2009) read them as Christian invocations: SCS is S(an)c(tu)s (the abbreviation stroke is visible on the Fyris blade), D is Dominus, X is Christus, IS is the ligature for Iesus, and MTINIUS is M(ar)tinius or Mauricius. In Wagner’s 2013 account the Alphen blade shows the SCS, DIC, BENE and NED marks at once, which he takes to show that the older classification into groups does not hold.

03 A parse against the family

I aligned both faces to the published sibling readings. The longest matches are ISSCSD (face A, with Fyris 74 …ERNISSCSDX…), OXMTINIUS (face B, with W 1830 …ISROXMTINIUSRN) and BENED (face A, with the Finnish blade). Applying their expansions gives:

facesegmentation
ABENED | O X O | F T | IS | SCS D | RR | IS C | DIC | EC | MTINIUS | CS D | NI
BDIO | X | MTINIUS | ES D | IO | MTINIUS | CS D | ICCC | MT | DIC | II Z IS | I

That is, roughly: May he bless … O Christ … Jesus; holy Lord … Jesus Christ … Martinius, holy Lord …. Thirty of the 38 letters on face A and 26 of the 41 on face B (56 of 79, 71%) sit in these elements. F T, RR, EC, NI, ICCC, II Z I and the DIO groups do not, and every expansion is the sibling authors’ guess, so this is a family-level parse and not a decipherment. A language check on the letters puts Latin first, narrowly, which fits plain Latin in abbreviation and does not fit a substitution. No key exists to recover.

Measured family match (Brice Dudout, 11 November 2026). The same order test used for the Witham sword (longest common subsequence against 3,000 shuffles of each face) places both faces in the family, more weakly than Witham: with Fyris UMF/B 74, 20/38 (face A, p = 0.033) and 20/41 (face B, p = 0.026); with Marburg, 14/38 (p = 0.008) and 12/41 (p = 0.017); about 0.03–0.13 after Bonferroni over two references and two faces. Face A is not a prayer acrostic either (weak fit, p = 0.017, carried by the nomina sacra; face B p = 0.48). Two hypotheses, not checkable on the published 72 ppi images: the sign printed “7” may be the Tironian note ⁊ (et) rather than Z, and face A’s final DNI may be the standard contraction of domini.

04 What has been proposed

  • Marc van Hasselt, 2012 and 2014. BENED(ict)O CH(rist)O … S(an)C(tu)S, which he says “doesn’t make a lot of sense”; MTINIUSCS as Martinius Sanctus, with St Mauritius floated. J. P. Gumbert, Wagner and several Latinists consulted in 2014 reached no agreed expansion. He links the find to the flight of 1204 in the Loon War; that is his inference, unproven.
  • Matthijs van Halteren, 2014. Benedictus Sanctus Martinus Sanctus; source not given.
  • Wagner et al., 2009 and 2013. The SDX-family expansions used above; the family readings are tentative.
  • Craig Peters, 2011. BENEDICE with interrupting characters; the OXO possibly symbolic.

05 What would finish it

not solved  The formula-level parse is as far as the published material goes.

whatwhy it would work
The Restaura conservation report and the Provincial Depot photographsThey would settle the doubtful letters (the sign printed as 7, the M and T ligatures) and show the abbreviation strokes, and test the Tironian-note and DNI hypotheses.
An agreed expansion of the SDX, DIC and NED formulaeThe unexplained groups are the family’s own formula parts; reading them on one blade would read them on the others.
