The Senate of Venice to its ambassador in France, 1589

A Venetian state letter that Philip II’s cipher secretary deciphered at Turin, c. 1589, and whose solution is lost. The letter
The Doge Pasquale Cicogna and the Senate to Giovanni Mocenigo, Venice’s ambassador with Henry III, dated Venice, 27 April 1589 and signed by the Senate secretary Marco Ottobon. With it is a copy of the Senate’s answer of 24 April to the French envoy in Venice, Gerolamo Gondi.
Where
Biblioteca Nacional de España, Mss/994 ff. 34–38, the dossier of Luis Valle de la Cerda, Philip II’s cipher secretary. DECODE R2252, status N/A, cleartext language given as Latin.
The cipher
Groups of one base letter (a, c, d, f, g, h) and a figure from 1 to 99. They stand for single letters, syllables, whole words and nulls, all in one table.
How it was deciphered
The key is the Council of Ten’s Zifra Prima of 1577–78, a sheet in the Venetian state archive (DECODE R1789), matched here in October 2026. The first transcription, from a 130-dpi copy, could not be read as sentences. In November 2026 the contributor aaymeloglu transcribed all seven pages again from the library’s public scans and read both pieces with the sheet as it stands. Of 1,585 group slots, 1,537 are plain lookups. The lookups were checked here against an independent transcription of the key.
What it says
Gondi set out Henry III’s troubles, asked Venice for help against the danger to his crown, and invited it into a closer league. The Senate excused itself, sent Mocenigo a copy of its answer, and told him how to present it. In that answer Venice promises to intercede again with Pope Sixtus V, “come già facessimo per occasione delle cose di Saluzzo”, so that the Pope will favour the King’s cause.

01 The letter, and how it came to Madrid

Mss/994 of the Biblioteca Nacional in Madrid is the dossier Luis Valle de la Cerda put together of his services to Philip II and Philip III as secretario de la cifra: letters he had deciphered, alphabets he had rebuilt, keys he had made. Item 5, at f. 34, is headed in a cartouche in Spanish: a letter in cipher from Venice, which the Infanta Catalina (Duchess of Savoy) gave to Valle at Turin on the Duke’s orders, “que declaró” (which he deciphered), and which has neither its key nor its decipherment, “por haverse perdido”. The printed catalogue of 1957 adds that the letter is from the secretary Marco Otthobon to the ambassador Juan Mocenigo, 27 April 1589, with an autograph signature. Tomokiyo lists it as a letter of which no decipherment is known.

The letter itself opens in clear Latin, in a chancery hand: Pascalis Ciconia Dei gratia Dux Venetiarum etc. Nobili et sapienti viro Ioanni Mocenigo oratori nostro apud Serenissimum Regem Christianissimum fideli dilecto salutem et dilectionis affectum. So it is not a private letter of Ottobon’s. It is a ducal letter of the Doge and Senate to their ambassador with Henry III, signed at the foot by the secretary of the Senate, as such letters were. The address on f. 38v repeats the ambassador’s title. Pasquale Cicogna was doge from 1585 to 1595. Marco Ottobon (1554–1649), a cittadino originario, was a Senate secretary from 1584 and much later grand chancellor. Giovanni Mocenigo was with the King at Tours that spring, after Henry III’s truce with Henry of Navarre.

The cipher runs over six and a half pages, about 120 lines and 1,528 groups in the first transcription. Part of it is in clear. The last six lines of f. 36r and the first four of f. 36v say why the Senate wrote in cipher: the French postmaster in Venice had shown it a passage from the Lyon postmaster, who had forwarded only a few lines of the ambassador’s despatch of 28 March, “che il rimanente con tutte le altre [lettere] tre sono state intercette”. The dateline (Dat. in nostro Ducali Palatio die xxvii Aprilis … MDLXXXIX) and “Marco Ottobon Secr.” follow on f. 36v. Ff. 37r–38r are a second piece in the same hand, with no protocol, which turns out to be the copy of the Senate’s answer to the French ambassador in Venice (section 04). The Venice–France road crossed Savoy, and that is how the packet reached Turin and Valle.

02 The key: the Council of Ten’s Zifra Prima

The Council of Ten kept its ciphers in registers, now in the Archivio di Stato di Venezia (Consiglio dei Dieci, Cifre, chiavi e scontri di cifra). Register 16 of busta 4 is the cipher book of Hieronimo di Franceschi, described by Paolo Bonavoglia. Its ff. 64–65 hold the Zifra prima in two parts, per scriver (for enciphering) and per trazer (for deciphering). DECODE has the leaves as R1789, dated only “1500–1799”. Tomokiyo describes it as a letter from a to h with a figure from 1 to 99, for letters, syllables and words “without distinction”.

The table was transcribed here from the DECODE images in full: 527 codes. Applied as it stands to the first transcription of the letter, it gave nonsense, although 94.8% of the letter’s groups are codes it defines. The fault was in the transcription. In this hand the 7 is a z-shaped stroke and had been read as 2, the 8 is a small closed loop and had been read as 0, and 3, 5 and 8 had been confused. The first transcription had no 7 at all, in 1,528 groups. With those re-readings the commonest groups fall on the commonest units of the table: a20 becomes a28 che, the c20–c29 run becomes c70–c79 lla lu lo li le la ma me mi mo, g20 becomes g70 da. Usage of the table’s syllables then follows their frequency in Italian (Spearman 0.286, against 0.231 at the 99th percentile of a structured null; p = 0.0008).

The first transcription seemed to need two groups the sheet does not give: d83 (66 times) read a, and c86 (35 times) read o, where the sheet has Maone. Both were taken as extra homophones, with a note that they might be the sheet’s own d53 = a and h86 = o, misread. The transcription of November 2026, made from better scans, settles it. It has no d83 and no c86. It reads d53 80 times and h86 37 times, so the two values were misreadings, and the key is the sheet unchanged. A second key in the same register (f. 79, DECODE R1790, a small cipher for the Captain in the Gulf, the patrol against the Uskoks and the governors of Zara) and the zifra-granda keys of 1590 and 1595 on DECODE were checked, and they are different systems.

03 The first piece: the Senate to Mocenigo

The first piece reports Gondi’s request and the Senate’s answer to it, and tells the ambassador what to do. Counted strictly, 311 of its 349 words read (89.1%). The text below is aaymeloglu’s reading in regularised spelling. Square brackets mark what is supplied or doubtful.

ff. 35r–35v. Vegnù in questa città [fin] 15 del mese presente, vi [ricevemmo] il signor Gerolamo Gondi … quale che ci espose delli travagli et bisogni presenti di Sua Maestà Cristianissima. Egli così nella sua prima audientia come dopo in un’altra che ebbe da noi, commemorandoci l’amor et affetione grande che quel Serenissimo re porta alla repubblica nostra … ne ricercò con molta instantia di sovvenirle in tanta necessità et somministrarle alcun soccorso per resistere all’imminente pericolo di quella corona, et assicurarsi dalli nemici del[la cor]ona, voltar poi le sue forze alla recuperatione di Saluzzo, [tol]togli dal signor [duca?] di Savoia … [in]vitandoci appresso che lo [giudi?]cassimo bene a collegarsi […] et stringersi maggio[r]mente se[co] in qualche unione di lega.

“Gerolamo Gondi came to this city by the 15th of this month and set out the troubles and present needs of His Most Christian Majesty. In his first audience, and in another he had with us, he recalled the great love and affection the King bears our Republic. He asked us urgently to relieve him in so great a need and give him some help against the imminent danger to that crown, so that he could secure himself against its enemies and then turn his forces to recovering Saluzzo, taken from him by the [Duke] of Savoy. He invited us besides to join and bind ourselves more closely with him in some league …” Recessimo, the word read [ricevemmo], is what the key gives (g20 = rece), and it is counted as a hole, like every word with a restored part.

ff. 35v–36v. Venice excused itself because of “li presenti movimenti et per altri nostri gravissimi rispetti” and sends the ambassador “la copia” of its answer. If the King or his ministers raise the matter, he is to make “quelli prudenti et destri officii” that make known “li giustissimi rispetti che ci hanno intrattenuto di non poter in questa occasione soddisfare a noi medesimi”. The clear passage on ff. 36r–36v follows. The French postmaster in Venice had shown the Senate a letter from the postmaster of Lyon, who had forwarded only the last four lines of the ambassador’s despatch of 28 March, “il rimanente con tutte le altre [vos]tre sono state intercette”. The cipher then resumes. Mocenigo is to find a safe route for his letters and keep the Senate informed “delle co[se che?] occorrono lì alla giorna[ta]”. The losses in this piece are at the right edge of f. 35v, where one to three groups of each line run into the binding (del[la cor]ona, [tol]togli, col[…]), and two blotted groups on f. 36r.

04 The second piece: the answer to Gondi

Ff. 37r–38r are the copy the Senate sent. The heading reads in clear key values: “1589 a 24 april, copia della risposta fata al signor Gondi, ambasc[iatore] di Francia”. This piece is read: 381 of its 398 words read when counted strictly (95.7%). The Senate protests “la molta affetione che in tutti li tempi passati è stata portata et tuttavia porta la repubblica nostra alla Serenissima corona di Francia”. It regrets that it cannot now match its ready will with “pari effetti, come altre volte abbiamo fatto”. Then it comes to the point:

… nel desiderare et procurare il bene, la grandezza di quella Serenissima corona et particolar comodo et servi[tio] della sua persona, da noi tanto osser[va]ta, abbiamo deliberato, come già facessimo per occasione delle cose di Saluzzo, de interponer al presente ancor con nuova et efficace istanza presso il sommo pontefice la intercessione della repubblica nostra, perché Sua Santità si muova a dovere, con la sua somma autorità, favorire ove farà bisogno la causa giu[sti]ssima della Maestà sua, nell’acquietare le sollevazioni di quel regno et ammorzare questo incendio di guerra, il qual si vede con [certissimo] danno et maggiori pericoli di tutta la cristianità si va accendendo.

“… we have resolved, as we did before over the affairs of Saluzzo, to interpose again now, with a new and earnest appeal, the intercession of our Republic with the Supreme Pontiff, so that His Holiness may be moved to use his supreme authority, wherever needed, in favour of His Majesty’s most just cause, in quieting the risings in that kingdom and putting out this fire of war, which we see spreading with [certain] harm and greater dangers to all Christendom.” (Translated here after aaymeloglu’s English, keeping closer to the Italian word order.) The cipher has giussima and crirtissimo. Both are read as the clerk’s slips for giustissima and certissimo, and both are counted as holes. The piece closes on f. 38r. Venice’s reluctance to do more “non nasce da men pronta et disposta volontà, ma da assai convenientissimi et importantissimi rispetti”.

The answer fits what is known of Venice that spring. It refuses material help and offers mediation at Rome a month before Sixtus V’s monitorium against Henry III (24 May 1589). The Doge’s letters to the Venetian ambassador at Rome, Badoer, of 29 April and 13 May 1589 (printed in Hübner’s Sixte-Quint) use the same language, “la grandezza del pericolo iminente” and “questo incendio universale”. That parallel confirms the subject; it is not a copy of this text, and nothing here was taken from it.

05 Method, and what each step shows

The readings rest on five steps of different strength. They are kept apart here because the later ones depend on the earlier ones. Steps 1–4 were made on the 130-dpi copy; step 5 is the November transcription that the reading now rests on.

  1. Transcription (first pass, September 2026). All seven pages were transcribed from the BNE Digital images, which are the same scans as on DECODE at about 130 dpi: 1,528 groups, 196 distinct. One line read twice from two crops disagreed in three of five groups, so the first pass is about 70% reliable per group. Ciphertext-only annealers, run blind, failed on matched synthetic controls (0–9% of letters recovered with no transcription noise at all), so no attempt was made to break the cipher without a key.
  2. The key and the hand reading (9 October). With the Zifra Prima transcribed, three readers (this session and two helper agents) went through every line, choosing for each group the code that is compatible with the image and the digit confusions and that makes Italian with its neighbours, and grading each line high, medium or low. Result: 721 of 1,565 groups read (46.0%). The best evidence for the key is here: long runs that read as continuous chancery Italian, such as the end of f. 35r above, from a table made in Venice twelve years before.
  3. A calibrated decoder (dec.py). A noisy-channel decoder learns from the hand-read lines how the first pass misreads each base letter and digit, and searches the letter for the code sequence that best combines that confusion model, how often each code is used, and a model of 16th-century diplomatic Italian (Guicciardini, Machiavelli, the papal nuncios’ despatches, and Albèri’s Relazioni degli ambasciatori veneti at double weight). Trained on six pages and tested on the seventh, it agrees with the hand reading on 644 of 721 groups (89.3%), where taking the first pass as it stands agrees on 65.9%. On synthetic text corrupted the same way it recovers 82.6% of groups at the first pass’s error rate, but only 61% at a higher one. This shows the decoder is useful where some sense already anchors a line. It does not by itself read the hard pages: decoding ff. 35v–36v freely gives Italian fragments, not continuous sense.
  4. Fitting proposed lines, with a control (fit.py, fitcontrol.py). For the remaining lines the reading was done the other way round. An Italian text was proposed for a line (from the hand reading, the decoder’s draft and the context), and the program looked for the code sequence spelling exactly that text whose codes are each a plausible misreading of the transcribed group. A group counts as read only if it lies outside the gaps and its confusion score passes a fixed threshold. Such a fit can always place some letters, so every proposed text was also fitted onto fifteen wrong lines of the letter. At the threshold used, 10% of the groups of a wrong line pass by chance, against 80% on the true lines. Five lines whose fit did not beat the best wrong-line fit by three groups were left at low confidence. This step added 260 groups (46.0% → 62.7%).

What the control does and does not establish. It shows that a proposed text fits its own line far better than it fits other lines. A line that passes is evidence for the proposed text, but not proof of every word. The text came from the reader, a fitted line can still have a wrong word where a wrong code fits about as well, and roughly one group in ten would pass by chance. For that reason no line read only by the fit is graded high: all the high lines (236 groups read) come from the hand reading against the images, and every line on ff. 35v–36v is medium at best. The measure is measure_rd.py: groups in high or medium lines that carry no doubt mark.

06 What remains uncertain

  • f. 35v: short connections and line endings hidden in the gutter, one to three groups per line (for example del[la cor]ona, [tol]togli, col[…] on l. 19). They need the volume itself or a photograph of the opened leaf.
  • f. 36r l. 8: two blotted groups before benissimo. f. 36v l. 6: the ending after di nostra.
  • Three disputed labels (f. 35r l. 3 a84/a94, f. 35v l. 20 a92/a94, f. 38r l. 9 f63/f67) and the other uncertain signs, 48 slots in all.
  • Forms the cipher clerk miswrote: recessimo, [po]tergo, soletione (read [sollevazioni?]), giussima, crirtissimo, tempo for tempi. Each repair is marked in the contributor’s edition.
  • The four small groups at the top right of f. 37r: too small to transcribe at this resolution.
  • The transcription is context-assisted: a label read without a mark can still be wrong. About 50 groups were compared here, not the whole letter.

The Senate’s register (ASVe, Senato, Deliberazioni Secreta, reg. 87, under 24–27 April 1589) should hold the plain text of both pieces. It would settle the clerk’s slips and the gutter. Romanin (Storia documentata di Venezia VI p. 409), as the contributor summarises him, says that Venice, asked by Henry III for help in 1589, granted a loan but excused itself from other aid because it had to watch Spanish movements in Italy, and did every good office with Sixtus V; his note cites the Secreta of 3 June 1589, a later deliberation than this answer. That reference was not opened here.
