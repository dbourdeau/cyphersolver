Châteauneuf to Louis XIV and Pontchartrain, 1691–1694: five passages left in cipher

Five passages, in four despatches of the French ambassador at the Porte and one reply of the King, listed by the archive as not deciphered, are deciphered in part with a key learnt from another letter of the volume; that key missed its own test by 0.02.

The letters
Five pieces of 1691 to 1694: an extract of Châteauneuf’s letter to Louis XIV of 9 April 1691, the King’s reply of 27 June 1691, and three letters of Châteauneuf to Pontchartrain from Adrianople (21 October and 26 November 1692, 8 July 1694). Each has passages in numbers among clear French.
Where
Archives nationales, AE/B/I/381, ff. 152, 155, 232, 239 and 433 (catalogue 539). The inventory says of them that the part in cipher is not deciphered. No DECODE record was found. The images are a private reproduction and are not shown.
The cipher
One table of numbers from 3 to 368 for all five pieces: single letters with several numbers each, syllables, and words and names. 597 groups in all.
How it was deciphered
The key was learnt from the decipherment written at the time between the lines of the letter of 17 January 1694 in the same volume, then applied to the five pieces.

The key failed the test set for it in advance: 0.830 of characters on a second glossed letter, against a bar of 0.85. Fourteen values were added after that measure, and the key as used has not been measured on anything.
What it says

Still open
33 of the 271 cipher words: 14 numbers with no attested value, four groups cut by the binding, and words that rest on a doubtful figure or on the sentence alone. No person has checked the transcription against the images.

01 The five pieces

Volume AE/B/I/381 of the Archives nationales holds the consular correspondence from Constantinople for 1691 to 1694. The pieces here are letters of Pierre-Antoine de Castagnères, marquis de Châteauneuf, the French ambassador, and one reply of the King. By the contributor’s notes, eighteen pieces of the volume have cipher with a decipherment of the time written between the lines; two of them were reproduced and used for the key. Five pieces have none: the archive’s inventory (FRAN_IR_003977) says that their cipher is not deciphered (“la partie chiffrée n’est pas déchiffrée”; for f. 232 “le texte chiffré n’est pas déchiffré”). Those five are the subject of this page.

02 The key and its failed test

The table. One table serves all five pieces, from 1691 to 1694, for letters to the King and to Pontchartrain alike. The low numbers are single letters, each with several numbers (a = 9, 16; e = 29, 36; n = 20, 38; s = 22, 45, 47, 49; t = 27, 50). The middle numbers are syllables (104 re, 107 ce, 138 de, 333 que, 342 oit), and the high ones words and names (275 vizir, 284 seigneur, 285 vénitiens, 301 majesté, 325 vaisseau, 328 empire, 332 ottoman, 345 Venise, 362 occasion). Five numbers, 355 and 365 to 368, behave as nulls; that rests on the sentences alone. It is not the code of Louis XIV’s letters to Châteauneuf of 1690 described by Satoshi Tomokiyo: “le” is 195 there and 91 here.

Where the key comes from. The letter of 17 January 1694 (f. 376) has 119 lines of cipher, about 1,546 groups, with the decipherment of the time written above them. Brice Dudout’s agents aligned the runs of numbers with the letters of that decipherment (an alignment by expectation-maximisation, then curation by hand) and froze the result as KEY_v1, 169 numbers.

The test, set in advance. The protocol was written down on 20 November 2026; that it came before any key was built is what its own heading says. Its hash is recorded in one list with those of KEY_v1 and the transcriptions, under a single time, 13:32 on 20 November, so the order of these steps rests on the contributor’s record. The whole of a second glossed letter, that of 21 September 1694 (f. 452, 457 groups), was held out. KEY_v1 would be measured on it once, by the share of characters that agree with the decipherment of the time after both are reduced to plain letters. Two hundred control keys would be made by dealing the values of KEY_v1 at random to its numbers. The key would pass only with 0.85 or more and at least 0.30 above the best control.

What that means. The key is not validated at its own bar: 0.830 against 0.85, a miss of 0.02. The margin over the controls was met. The decode of the held-out letter is recognisable French, and the notes put the shortfall down to numbers left out of KEY_v1, three of them common values that stand in the decipherment of f. 376 and were dropped by oversight when the key was curated (81 la, 50 t, 70 guerre). That account was made after the measure and does not change the result. The held-out letter is now spent, and no other glossed piece was reproduced, so no second measure can be made for now.

What was added after the measure. The key applied to the five pieces, KEY_v2, is KEY_v1 with 14 values added after the measure: six from the decipherment of f. 376 that KEY_v1 had left out (81 la, 50 t, 70 guerre, 233, 217, 353), and eight taken from the decipherment of the held-out letter itself (145 turc, 209 françois, 270 ju, 276 bo, 318 estat, 3 il, 109, 262). KEY_v2 has not been measured on anything. Three more values (11 il, 43 o, 103 ont) and the nulls come from the sentences of the five pieces alone, and a word that needs one of them is counted as a hole. One number changed sides: the frozen keys give 355 as “a”, from its single occurrence in f. 376; in the five pieces that gives “re-a-lution” and nothing sensible in four other places, so the key file and the decryption treat it as a null. The frozen measure used “a”.

Of the nine files hashed, eight match the committed files (checked 24 November 2026); KEY_v2 matches its final hash, and an earlier KEY_v2 hash in the list was superseded the same day. The ninth file is the transcribers’ instruction sheet, edited after hashing; it is not an input to the key or the measure. That the key was frozen before the measure rests on the contributor’s record and was not checked here.

The layout of the table was worked out at the write-up. Set out in ten columns by the last digit, the values run in alphabetical order down each column, and each column takes up where the one before stops: for each letter the syllables first (97 ca, 107 ce, 117 ci, 127 co, 137 cu), then the words (197 Constantinople, 217 capitulations, 297 car, 317 commerce, 337 compte). All but two of the values attested in the two glossed letters fall in their place (233, given as ue, stands among the P words, and 200, given as ay, between the H and the J words). The layout cannot make a value sure; it tells with which letter a missing word begins, and section 05 uses it. Three keys of the same state and decade held in this repository were compared by number, and none is this table: George Lasry’s key for Vergier’s letters to Pontchartrain of 1696 (le 196, que 259), Bazeries’s table of the Grand Chiffre of 1691, and the 1690 code above; here le is 91 and que 333.

03 What the passages say

In the quotations, words in italics are in clear in the letter. A number in angle brackets has no attested value. A word in square brackets rests on a figure cut by the binding or on a value known from the sentence only, and a word in braces is spelled with a doubtful or odd figure. All of these are counted as holes. The complete French text, the English translation and the key are in the Full texts section.

9 April 1691, Châteauneuf to the King (f. 152). Venice was at war with the Porte. Châteauneuf puts to the King the question of the French protection that the Venetians enjoyed at Constantinople and awaits his orders; three numbers of that sentence have no value. The Republic, he writes, could be told that the Venetians had lived there in wartime only by the tolerance and simplicity of the Turks:

si ‹305› majesté ‹280› que les Vénitiens ‹177› doivent plus jouir {ici} à l’avenir de sa protection Elle me donnera ses ordres … ‹69›-dre à la République que ce n’est que par tolérance et la simplicité des Turcs que les Vénitiens [ont] vécu ici en tems de guerre

Two hundred Venetians were living there under protection (the word after “trois” has no value). The passage speaks of the fear that their removal would be demanded at once and that the iniquity of such an overture would be laid on the ambassador:

faire remarquer qu’il y avoit ici deux cens Vénitiens qui y vivoient sous la protection des trois ‹146›s de crainte qu’il n’exigeast sur le champ qu’on les fist sortir et qu’on ne m’imputast l’iniquité d’une telle ouverture

The King’s subjects would gain, he goes on, because the Venetians, no longer able to trade there with their own ships, would do it on French ones. Passports could be sent to Monsieur de La Haye, the French ambassador at Venice, or to himself, for a fixed number of ships that alone would carry the trade of Venice to the Levant, and a penalty set for French merchants who lent their names:

les Sujets de V. Majesté trouveroient un grand avantage dans cette résolution parce que les Vénitiens ne pouvant plus faire ici leur commerce avec leurs vaisseaux [ils] le feroient sur ceux de V. Ma.té qui pourroit envoyer des passepors à Monsieur de la Haye ou à moy … marchands françois qui leur preteroient leurs noms

One passage names a man: “du seul juif Conigliani” is the exception to the clear words “je n’en ay pas vu un seul”. An Israel Conegliano, a Jewish physician, acted for Venice at Constantinople in these years, by David Kaufmann’s study of 1895; that identification was not checked further here.

27 June 1691, the King’s reply (f. 155). One sentence in cipher closes the reply:

dont ils ne retirent que les marchandises de Levant mais quoy qu’[il] en soit vous ne devez pas vous {désister} par aucun {consentement} des ‹196›s qui [ont] esté stipulés par les capitulations faittes entre moy et le Grand Seigneur.

Whatever the case may be, the ambassador must not give up, by any consent, what the capitulations between the King and the Grand Seigneur stipulate.

21 October 1692, to Pontchartrain (f. 232). Three passages complete clear sentences about the reports of the Sieur Fabre. The order given to him was to keep the Venetians from coming into the Grand Seigneur’s ports under the protection of France, England or Holland; Châteauneuf had arranged that his own consent should not appear; and the moment of the siege of La Canée, which had stirred the Turks, had been missed:

l’ordre que je luy ay donné d’empecher les Vénitiens de venir dans les ports du Grand Seigneur sous les protections de France, d’Angleterre ou d’Hollande … pour qu’il ne parut pas que {j’eusse} donné mon consentement à ce [dessein] … il a manqué la conjoncture du temps du siège de la [Canée] qui avoit fort animé les Turcs ‹108› les Vénitiens

26 November 1692, to Pontchartrain (f. 239). Fabre has spoken to the Grand Vizier of the same plan; the Vizier said he would confer with the Reis Effendi:

Le Sr Fabre a aussy entretenu le G. Visir du dessein de ‹33› ‹232› qu‹59› ne plus {recevoir} les vaisseaux vénitiens dans les ports du [Grand] Seigneur sous aucune protection

8 July 1694, to Pontchartrain (f. 433). Had he solicited “ce Commandement” from the Grand Vizier, Châteauneuf writes, it would have been at a bad time:

si j’avois sollicité auprès du G. Visir ce Commandement dans un temps où nos ennemis publient que Sa Majesté est sur le point de conclure sa paix avec les ‹166›s ce ministre auroit pu se laisser [tromper] à ce faux bruit et d’autant plus qu’il auroit [veu] les françois abandonner Satalie de mon consentement

At a time when the enemies were publishing that the King was about to conclude his peace, the Vizier could have let himself be deceived by that false rumour, all the more since he would have seen the French abandon Satalia with the ambassador’s consent.

04 How much is deciphered

A cipher word counts as sure only when every one of its numbers has a value attested in the decipherments of ff. 376 or 452 and nothing is supplied. Probable words, restored words, words that need a value known from the sentence only, and numbers with no value are holes. Nulls give no word. The reading file first gave 240 of 272. On 24 November 2026 the “a” counted on the null 355 in the fourth passage of f. 152 was taken out of the count (239 of 271), and “consentement” in the King’s reply, which the cipher spells co-m-se-n-te-ment, with 28 (m) where 38 (n) is wanted, became a hole (238 of 271). Counted by groups, 560 of the 597 have a value attested in the two glossed letters, 17 have a value or a null from the sentences alone, 16 have none and 4 are cut by the binding.

The transcription was made by AI agents in two independent passes that did not see the key, one in page order and one in reverse, with a third agent deciding from enlarged crops the 12 places where they differed or the binding hid a figure. The mechanical decryption was reproduced at the write-up from the key file and the transcription (48 of 48 lines).

Key replay. The key file was then applied to the transcription and compared with the reading, word by word. The first run showed 18 words printed otherwise than the key gives them, with no mark: four already counted as holes, six printed in a regular spelling where the clerk’s own spelling is a word as it stands (the reading now prints passepors, preteroient, parut, faittes, seul, empecher, the last with 110 taken as he, as the glossed words show it, where the key file has che), and the “consentement” above. The others follow from the glossed letters. The decipherer of the time writes z as well as s for 47 and 49 (so devez), and writes Turcs and vous for the bare word codes 145 and 335. The word code 174 is printed as République; the glossed letters have it once, in “Republicains”, so the two République of f. 152 are counted as sure on the stem alone. After these changes the replay reproduces the reading with no unmarked difference. Of the 262 words it divides the cipher passages into, 239 are the key’s output letter for letter, one differs by i for y and one by z for s; 15 are marked and 6 are left unread (five on a number with no value, one on the 57). Beside them stand 10 bare numbers, nine with no value and the null 355. Those 241 are the 238 counted here plus ici and a stray y, which the count leaves out, and the first half of one unresolved word.

Each piece was gone through as sentences by an AI agent for the maintainers on 24 November 2026, before the replay (grade B on each: coherent but raw, the cipher passages holding together through the clear text around them). No person has reviewed the sentences, and no person has checked the transcription against the images.

05 What remains uncertain

Values taken from the decipherments of the time are grade C (from a known-plaintext letter). Everything in the list below is grade I (inferred from the sentence or from the place in the table) or M (uncertain), and none of it is counted.

  • The key itself is not validated (section 02).
  • Fourteen numbers have no attested value: 305, 280, 177, 69, 146, 152, 61, 64, 33, 59, 196, 108, 232, 166. Each occurs once or twice in the five pieces. The two glossed letters carry none of them except 61, once, where the decipherer of the time wrote nothing for it.
  • What the layout of the table suggests for seven of them (grade I): 305 among the V words, in “si ‹305› Majesté”, probably vostre; 280 just after 270 ju, probably juge; 146 among the A words, in “sous la protection des trois ‹146›s”, probably ambassadeurs; 166 among the A words, in “sa paix avec les ‹166›s”, alliés or allemands; 196 among the A words, in “des ‹196›s qui ont esté stipulés”, avantages or articles; 108 where the C words run on, in “animé les Turcs ‹108› les Vénitiens”, probably contre; 69 where the E words run on, before “-dre à la République que” and after the clear “on peut faire”, probably enten(dre), where the reading file restores répondre.
  • Two probable slips. 177 stands among the C words where the sentence wants “ne” (172); both transcription passes have 177. The first group of one passage of f. 152 was transcribed 57 (st), which gives no sense before “que si cette résolution”; the two passes disagreed on it, and “et” (52 or 54) would fit.
  • Possible cancelling signs (a hypothesis, grade I). 33 and 59 occur twice each, always as a pair around one or two groups, and both sentences are complete without what stands between them (“et imposer une peine considérable”, “du dessein de ne plus recevoir”). In the glossed letter of 21 September 1694 the decipherer wrote nothing for 61 nor for the group before it, as if 61 cancelled that group; Bazeries’s table of the Grand Chiffre of 1691 has a group of that kind. If so, 152 and 232 are cancelled groups, and 64 may be a sign of the same kind. Accepting this would take eight holes out of the count (238 of 263, 0.905, with f. 239 at 13 of 15). It is not accepted here: a sign fixed from the sentence alone is a context-only value.
  • Values from the sentence only (grade I): 11 il, 43 o, 103 ont, and the nulls 355 and 365 to 368. Five words need one of the first three.
  • Doubtful figures and odd spellings (grade M), seven words: ici (last figure at the fold), résolution (written re-‹355›-lu-ti-on, without so), a stray y after seul, désister (a stray f where an e is wanted), consentement (an m for the first n), j’eusse (a small figure at the fold), recevoir (spelled ri-ce-vo-i-r). Several of these, like 177 for 172, could be one figure taken for another by the clerk or by the transcription; the images would tell.
  • Four groups cut by the binding, in the words restored as dessein, Canée, Grand and veu. A flat photograph of the gutters would settle them.
  • What would settle the rest. Sixteen further glossed pieces of the same volume were not reproduced (ff. 131, 222, 261, 289, 317, 325, 330, 339, 398, 400, 409, 422, 425, 448, 470, 472). They would give a fresh held-out letter for the key, and may carry the missing numbers. Clear copies of these letters may also survive elsewhere. Hurmuzaki’s edition prints Châteauneuf’s despatches of 1691 and 1692 from the archives of the French foreign ministry, citing its volumes 23 and 24 (presumably the Turquie series of the Correspondance politique; this was not checked), and the Nantes répertoire lists a volume of his correspondence with the court (166PO/A/6). Neither was found online, and neither was seen.

No printed text of the five pieces was found. Hurmuzaki prints extracts of the despatches of these years on Moldavia, Wallachia, Transylvania and Poland, none of the passages in cipher here; Kaufmann tells the affair of the Venetians under French protection from the Venetian side and prints no French letter.

# chateauneuf1691 — English translation of the ciphered passages (holes in brackets)

**f. 152, Châteauneuf to Louis XIV, 9 Apr 1691 (extract).** If Your Majesty [judges] that the Venetians should [no longer] enjoy His protection here in future ... [the capitulations, taken] literally ... [answer] the Republic that it is only through the tolerance and simplicity of the Turks that the Venetians have lived here in time of war ... if this resolution is prejudicial to the Republic, it [should blame only the Dutch and English ambassadors] ... [he had pointed out that there were] here two hundred Venetians living under the protection of the three [crowns], for fear that he [would demand on the spot] that they be sent out and that the iniquity of such an overture be imputed to me ... [but I do not doubt that] he will want to have them withdrawn one day [when he has thought about it] ... [apart from] the only Jew Conigliani ... [Your Majesty's subjects would find great advantage] in this resolution, because the Venetians, no longer able to trade here with their own ships, would do it [on French ships]; passports [could be sent] to Monsieur de La Haye [French ambassador at Venice] or to me [for a fixed number of ships] which alone would carry the trade of Venice to the Levant ... [no goods to be loaded] addressed to our enemies, and a [heavy penalty] imposed [on French merchants] who would lend them their names.

**f. 155, Louis XIV's reply, 27 Jun 1691.** ... but whatever happens, you must not give up, by any consent, the [rights] stipulated by the capitulations made between me and the Grand Seigneur.

**f. 232, to Pontchartrain, 21 Oct 1692.** [Fabre finds it hard to carry out my order] to prevent the Venetians from coming into the Grand Seigneur's ports under the protection [of France, England or Holland] ... [I had taken the measures needed so that] it should not appear that I had consented to this design ... [the Grand Vizier missed the moment] of the siege of La Canée [Canea, Crete, besieged by the Venetians in 1692], which had greatly stirred the Turks [against] the Venetians.

**f. 239, to Pontchartrain, 26 Nov 1692.** [Fabre spoke to the Grand Vizier of the plan] no longer to receive Venetian ships in the Grand Seigneur's ports under any protection.

**f. 433, to Pontchartrain, 8 Jul 1694.** [had I solicited this command from the Grand Vizier] at a time when our enemies are publishing that His Majesty is about to conclude his peace with the [Allies/Imperials], this minister could have let himself be [deceived] by this false rumour, all the more since he would have [seen the French abandon Satalia with my consent].
