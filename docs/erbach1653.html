Georg Ernst zu Erbach to Ludwig VI of Hesse-Darmstadt, 1666

A count's eight secret reasons for not leaving his county, in a cipher whose key was a rhymed German Lord's Prayer. The verse was rebuilt from a Latin outline filed with the letter, and all three cipher letters are now deciphered.

01 The letters

The file D 4 Nr. 248/3 of the Hessian house archive at Darmstadt holds 39 letters from Count Georg Ernst zu Erbach to his friend Landgrave Ludwig VI of Hesse-Darmstadt (1630–1678), dated 1654 to 1670. The finding aid notes alchemy (it mentions a Dr Johann Tack) and “z. T. chiffrierte Briefe”, letters partly in cipher. The archive's 78 microfilm images are online. Most of the letters are about alchemical work and court news, and are signed with an anagram (“J. Strenuus Segrego” = Georgius Ernestus). Ludwig answers as “Tuus Totus 666”: LUDOVICUS read as Roman numerals sums to 666.

The cipher is in three letters only, all from a numbered series of 1666 addressed to Ludwig “à Stokholm”: No. 1 of 22 March, with two short runs; No. 2 of 27 April, with one; and an undated letter of two pages that is almost all cipher. Its points are headed in Latin, Primo to Octavo, and it ends with a question in Latin (Hic quaeritur…). The scans are microfilm frames, with the leaves small and turned on their side.

02 The cipher and the key

Each pair a,b is the b-th letter of line a of a short verse, counting letters only. The verse is a rhymed German version of the Lord's Prayer, in eight lines:

linetext (as rebuilt)letters
1O vatter aller frommen19
2geheiligt werd dein nahm21
3las dein reich zu uns kommen23
4dein will der mach uns zahm22
5gib brod vergib die sund20
6kein args das hertz entzunde24
7los uns aus aller nott18
8p ? x …(only 8,1 and 8,3 used)

So 1,1 is o, 7,18 is t, and the e of a word can be 1,6, 2,2, 3,5, 4,2, 6,2 and many more. Erbach usually ran forward along one line, so stretches of plaintext come out as rising numbers (1,10 1,11 1,12; 3,20 3,22 3,23). The 610 pairs use 123 different cells. Letters of the verse that no pair uses are filled in from the wording of the rhyme. Line 8 is known only from two cells (p and x). No printed source for this version of the verse was found.

About one pair in thirty is a slip, where Erbach miscounted by a letter or two: 5,14 (d) three times for t or u, consrliis for consiliis, beschafmenheit. Every slip lands on a neighbouring letter of the right line, and the context corrects all of them. They are listed in the reading file.

03 The decipherment

The undated letter answers a question Ludwig must have put: whether Erbach would leave his county, presumably to join him. The two had agreed that “inter nos conventum sit” each should speak “secundum conscientiam”. Erbach then sets out his reasons for refusing. Deciphered text is plain; the Latin headings and German connecting words in clear are in italics.

Primo; obstat turbulentus statu[s] publicus. Secundo; pericula ex bello electorum mihi proximo… Tertio; morbus uxoris quotidie crescens. Quarto et praecipue; fratris regimen partim per conniventiam, partim per veniam aetatis jam jam suscipientes. Quinto; ex ipsorum consiliis iuvenilibus pericula jamdum apparentia, jam quoad salutem subditorum quam meam domesticam. Sexto: subditorum proinde praecipua et bona in me fiducia. Septimo; divisio bonorum cum fratribus… Octavo; et quod maximum est, defectus fidelitatis consiliariorum.

First, the troubled state of the Empire. Second, the war of the electors close by. Third, his wife's illness, worse every day. Fourth and chiefly, his brothers taking up the government, partly by connivance and partly by a dispensation of age. Fifth, the dangers already visible from their young counsels, to his subjects and his own household. Sixth, his subjects' trust in him. Seventh, the division of property with his brothers. Eighth and greatest, the disloyalty of the councillors. The letter then asks:

Hic quaeritur; an rebus sic stantibus quidem dominus, cui deus salutem ecclesie, subditorum, domus et uxoris sue concredidit, sine lesione conscientie et offensione dei hec omnia possit deserere, mercenariorum directioni oves suas submittere, ac sic periculis jamdum praevisis exponere? Ego quidem non credo…

“Whether a lord to whom God has entrusted the welfare of the church, of his subjects, his house and his wife can, without hurt to his conscience and offence to God, abandon all this, put his sheep under the direction of hirelings, and so expose them to dangers already foreseen? I for my part do not believe it.” The hireling who leaves the sheep is John 10:12. For Ludwig, he adds, it would be quite another matter, since he need fear none of points three, four, five, seven and eight.

The short runs in the other two letters belong to the same business. No. 1 (22 March) speaks of “unsers vorhabens” (our plan) and promises news of the “mutationes” since. No. 2 (27 April) says that “im Majo oder Junio will Gott unsere bonorum divisio vorgehen”: God willing, the division of the property will go ahead in May or June. That is point seven of the long letter. No. 2 also says Erbach has already sent “meine rationes” by letter, hoping Ludwig will find them “relevant genug” and not accuse him of inconstancy: so the eight reasons were written before 27 April 1666. The cipher hides exactly the family and political matters, not the alchemy.

04 Method

The 571 pairs of the long letter were transcribed from rotated, enlarged crops of the microfilm, and checked against a second filming of the same leaves. The numbers looked like coordinates: no row goes past 24, and stretches climb inside one row. A homophonic solver with a Latin language model could not settle 124 symbols on so short a text.

The crib was on the same microfilm frame. A slip next to the letter carries a Latin outline in a hand of the time: Status turbulentus…, onerosa… quotidie crescens, subditorum fiducia, divisio bonorum, defectus fidelitatis. On its lower flap is an almost word-for-word gloss of the closing question, up to mercenariorum directioni oves suas submittere.

Laid against the cipher, turbulentus, defectus, dominus cui deus salutem, conscientie et offensione dei and oves suas fixed about eighty cells. Read row by row, those cells began to spell German: line 4 dein will … uns, line 3 las dein reich zu uns, line 7 los uns aus aller not, line 1 o vatter aller frommen. That is a rhymed Lord's Prayer, and its wording supplied the rest of each line. With the verse unchanged as the key, all three letters read.

Checks: 591 of the 610 pairs give the intended letter exactly. The letter-model test (docs/_validate.py) scores the reading at −2.39 per letter, against −4.60 for random keys of the same shape and at most −2.59 for keys fitted to shuffled ciphertext; verdict: pass. Before this work, no edition, study or modern decipherment of the letters was found.

05 What remains open

  • Nineteen pairs are slips (fifteen in the long letter, four in the short ones). Each reads from context and is listed with its correction. The least certain is zu b⟨r⟩ingen in the opening of the long letter.
  • One pair in No. 2 and a short blotted clause in the long letter are under ink on both filmings.
  • Some words of the clear German around the cipher are marked doubtful or unread. The text survives only on this microfilm, and the Kurrent is small.
  • The long letter is undated. It must be the letter of “rationes” that No. 2 refers to, so it was written before 27 April 1666.
  • Line 8 of the verse is known only as p ? x, and the source of this version of the rhymed prayer is not identified.

Letter 004, undated [before 27 Apr 1666], two pages

Erbach gives Ludwig VI eight reasons why he cannot leave his county: the troubled Empire, the electors' war nearby, his wife's illness, his brothers taking up government by connivance and a dispensation of age, their youthful counsels, his subjects' trust, the division of property, and disloyal councillors. He asks whether a lord may abandon church, subjects, house and wife and 'put his sheep under hirelings' (John 10:12). No. 2 (27 Apr 1666) refers to this letter of 'rationes', so it predates it.

# Letter 004: English translation of the cipher passages and their frame

[I have had to form] my thoughts about it, to disclose them obediently to Your Highness and to ask humbly for Your
Highness's understanding opinion. Your Highness will graciously remember that it was agreed between us that in this
matter each should speak and act according to his conscience. Accordingly I cannot refrain from saying it out, as I
ought: for the following reasons, however hard it is for me to bring myself to it, I have had to change the
resolution ...

First: the troubled state of public affairs stands in the way.
Second: the dangers from the electors' war, which is closest to me, and if armies and war parties pass over or
through it, of devastation.
Third: my wife's illness, growing daily.
Fourth and chiefly: my brother's government, which they are about to take up, partly by connivance, partly by a
dispensation of age.
Fifth: from their youthful counsels dangers are already apparent, both for the welfare of the subjects and for
my own household.
Sixth: hence the subjects' particular and good trust in me.
Seventh: the division of the property with my brothers, which is still to be made.
Eighth, and the greatest: the want of loyalty in the councillors.

If Your Highness will consider these eight points carefully, you will find them of no small weight, for in my
judgement they could well confuse many a compass. The question is whether, things standing so, a lord to whom
God has entrusted the welfare of the church, of his subjects, of his house and of his wife can, without hurt to
his conscience and offence to God, leave all this, put his sheep under the direction of hirelings, and so expose
them to dangers already foreseen. I for my part do not believe it; but I anxiously await Your Serene Highness's
heartfelt opinion [by letter, for I fear writing may not be safe]. With Your Highness it would be quite another
matter, since you need fear nothing of the third, fourth, fifth, seventh and eighth points above. I will not trouble Your
Highness longer now, but commend it all faithfully to God's protection[?], and myself to your lasting grace ...

The "hirelings" and "sheep" are John 10:12. Who the brothers are and what move Ludwig had proposed are not said
in the cipher; the 1666 letters in the file (Nos. 1 and 2, below) speak of a planned undertaking ("unsers
Vorhabens") and of the division of property due in May or June.

No. 1, Fürstenau 22 Mar 1666

Two short runs, 'unsers vorhabens' and 'mutationes', in a letter about a planned undertaking and the changes since.
