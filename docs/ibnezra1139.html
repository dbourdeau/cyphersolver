Unsolved Historical Ciphers

Isaac Ibn Ezra’s cipher letter of 1139

Cambridge University Library, T-S 13J18.19. Isaac wrote to Ḥalfon b. Netanel in Judaeo-Arabic about a wedding, commercial dealings and a sea voyage. Three short passages use numeral signs. Their plaintext remains unresolved. Manuscript, transcription and editorial discussion.

Catalogue status. This letter is a lead (research lead 2885), not a catalogue entry. The vetting gate holds it as partly read, because Gil and Fleischer printed proposals for the two recto words, though those proposals are disputed. The verso passage has no published reading. It can become a catalogue target of its own only after Martínez Delgado and Ashur’s chapter on this manuscript has been checked.

Four marks for a proposed three-letter word

The photograph shows four marks on recto line 12. The digital transcription encodes three numeral characters there. The edition’s Hebrew gloss gives four equivalents. Counting from the physical right, our working values for recto 11 are 400, 70, 300, 3, 40. For recto 12 they are 10, 40, 70, 8. The values are provisional.

Friedman reports that Gil and Fleischer proposed מרשעת and תרך for the two passages and questions whether a single method explains them. Reading right to left under a fixed substitution, their first guess assigns 40 to tav and 70 to resh. The second group therefore has tav and resh in its middle. To obtain their exact three-letter word we must also omit the rightmost mark and assign 8 to kaf. No independent evidence establishes that omission. The wedding term ערוסה, “bride,” also fits the first group’s five distinct signs. Isaac uses it elsewhere, but it yields a different middle pair on recto 12 and no confirmed continuation. What the back can test

The eleven marks appear in groups of five, two and four. The first sign appears at the start of each group. The third sign recurs at the end of the middle group. The two marks at the far left both have hooked strokes. Whether they share a value materially changes the results.

Across 8,523 other Geniza transcription files, no three-word sequence with those lengths matched the full working pattern with identical final signs in either reading direction. Relaxing three uncertain positions and tying the inner hook to the last sign of recto 12 gave six distinct parallels. Tying the outer hook to it instead gave eight. Neither search produced a phrase whose assignments also yielded both front words in their tested contexts. Rare expressions, names, spelling variants and different word divisions can all escape an exact search.

One parallel illustrates the risk of taking context as proof. The Hebrew marriage formula להיות לי לאשה, “to be my wife,” has the right word lengths and initial repetitions. It requires the second and last signs to represent the same letter, he. In the photograph one is rounded and the other hooked. An independent identification of these signs would test the conjecture. Evidence and limits

This AI-assisted analysis tested the reported readings against a single one-to-one substitution. The evidence supports retaining four marks in a working transcription of recto 12. It supplies neither a verified key nor a new decipherment. Gil and Fleischer’s original discussion and Martínez Delgado and Ashur’s chapter “Encriptando mensajes” remain to be checked directly.
