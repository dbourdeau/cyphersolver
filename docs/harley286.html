Sir Simonds D’Ewes’s cipher log of his son’s fits, 1635–36

British Library, Harley MS 286 f. 61 (DECODE R7759, “unknown sender to unknown recipient, 1635”): a small slip, one line for each convulsion fit of the antiquary’s infant son Clopton, written in D’Ewes’s private alphabet. No decipherment of the slip and no printed key were found. The alphabet was recovered from the slip alone, from probable words, and 96.5% of the words are deciphered. Measured on the running Latin reading, the log builds coherent sentences but reads at 89.4% of its words, under the 95% bar.

Not a letter: Sir Simonds D’Ewes’s private Latin record of his infant son Clopton’s convulsion fits, one fit to a line, April 1635 to January 1636.

British Library, Harley MS 286 (the BL catalogue files it under 289; not settled here). DECODE R7759, listed as a non-decrypted letter from an unknown sender.

A simple substitution alphabet D’Ewes invented at school. No key to it is printed.
How it was read
The key was recovered from the slip alone by placing probable words (convulsio, plena luna, difficilis) against recurring cipher words, then checked against the calendar and D’Ewes’s Autobiography.
What it says
Forty-four fits to 4 March 1636; the child died on 9 May 1636.

The last line (trimmed under the mount), one word at the edge of line 13 and a faint phrase in lines 24–25.

01 The slip and its writer

Sir Simonds D’Ewes (1602–1650), the parliamentary antiquary whose manuscripts make up much of the Harleian collection, wrote his private papers in an alphabet of his own. His diary of 1622–24 (Harley MS 481) is partly in it, and a 1915 University of Minnesota thesis deciphered that diary without printing the key. Folio 61 is a small slip mounted on a larger leaf, headed 1635 in ordinary figures, with the margin dates 1635/1636 in clear beside the January entry and a running count in cipher, huc usque xxxii, “thus far 32”, at the end of the August entries. A rule separates the summer entries from those of September and January. The foot of the slip runs under the mount and its last line is cut.

02 The alphabet

One symbol per letter, words divided, numerals written with the letter symbols (x, v, i as ξ, S, r). Several symbols are ordinary letters used for other values, so at first sight the text looks like garbled Latin script.

The Autobiography gives the end: the fits came more thickly in the spring of 1636, twelve in one night on 8–9 May, and the child died about two in the afternoon of Monday 9 May 1636, a year and nine months old. D’Ewes blamed “a proud, fretting, ill-conditioned woman for a nurse”, an issue opened in the child’s neck and the violent remedies of Dr Despotine of Bury, and the rickets that followed.

04 Method

  1. The images of R7759 were fetched from DECODE; the second image is only the verso showing through. A search of the BL catalogue named D’Ewes.
  2. No key to D’Ewes’s alphabet was found in print: the Minnesota thesis on his diary and Notestein’s 1923 edition of his parliamentary journal mention the cipher but give no table.
  3. Probable words: a long word recurring on almost every line, <oηSSθ2ro, fits convulsio; a two-word phrase 9θcηd θSηd fits plena luna; Xrφφr<rθr2 fits difficilis. The month names and numerals at the start of each line supplied the rest.
  4. Checks against the calendar: 3 April 1635 was a Friday (die Veneris), 23 April a Thursday (die Iovis), 23 May a Saturday (die Saturni). The moon was full on 23 April, 19 July and 18 August and new on 2 August, as the slip says. On 25 May (“plena luna”) and 12 June (“decrescente”) it was not; the numerals are unambiguous at high magnification, so these are D’Ewes’s own slips or loose wording.
  5. The Autobiography (Halliwell’s edition, 1845) confirmed the start on 3 April 1635 and the count of forty-four fits.
  6. Two full transcription passes over line crops: 92.7% of words deciphered after the first, 96.5% after re-cropping the doubtful words at three times the size. Measured on the words of the running Latin reading (October 2026), 89.4% read as sense, under the 95% bar.

05 What remains uncertain

  • Line 30, the last line (about six words): trimmed under the mount; only “…am nonam” shows. Needs the leaf lifted.
  • Line 13, last word 9oθS∂- (p/g, o, l, u, a): cramped at the edge, not deciphered.
  • Lines 24–25, sed an 2∂c<- / Scθ: faint and cramped; septima, pede and nativo in the same lines are doubtful (grade M).
  • Doubtful words elsewhere (grade C): ix and tum (line 3), the suspension after luna (line 6), mitis (line 7), tum (line 11), vexit (line 14), absolutus (line 24).
  • The shelfmark: DECODE says Harley MS 286, the BL catalogue Harley MS 289. Not resolved.

# Harley MS 286 f. 61: D'Ewes's log of his son's fits, 1635-36: English translation

Heading, clear: 1635.

## Translation

April 3. In the morning about the ninth hour he had a convulsion. Friday.
April 23. In the afternoon he had convulsions twice. Thursday. With the full moon.
May 14. At the end of three weeks [?nine] convulsions again. The moon [?then] changed.
May 23. Saturday, an open flux.
May 25. Between the hours of 12 and 1 he had a convulsion, the full moon being on that day.
June 12. With the moon waning [?] he felt the first convulsion.
June 13. Another mild [?] convulsion followed. And on June 14 another.
July 17. Before the full moon, which fell on the 19th following, two mild convulsions.
July 23. Another severe convulsion.
August 2. The moon [?then] new, a severe [struck] convulsion.
August 3. A mild convulsion in the morning. About the first hour, when he was in the arms of my people, another
terrible convulsion, which after dinner [?shook] him with no slight, great trembling. In the afternoon again
[?afterwards] another severe convulsion.
Aug. 24. In the morning another severe convulsion.
Aug. 20. A little after the full moon (which fell on the 18th of this month) three very severe convulsions, in the
third of which his mouth was drawn almost to the right ear, which I had never observed before. The same night before
the first hour two other convulsions, but milder.
Aug. 21. After the second hour in the morning and before the fifth, two other mild convulsions. Before the twelfth
hour another severe one. [margin: up to here 32.]
Aug. 31. [?released] [lighter ink, above rule]
Sept. 21. In the day, and before the first hour of the night, a [?seventh] agony; but whether {?} [?or] other preceding
[?at the foot] a convulsion, but also [?inborn] epilepsy.
Sept. 27. After the second hour in the morning and before 7, three other agonies.
Jan. 24. After seventeen weeks' freedom, at night between the hours [ninth struck] 8 and 9, a severe convulsion. I
hoped, however, that it had come from too large a supper, which he vomited up after the convulsion.
{?} ... the ninth [hou]r
