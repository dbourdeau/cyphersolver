Rome → the Court · homophonic substitution cipher (Gramont’s cipher, 1529) · 11 October 1529

Gramont to Montmorency, Rome, 11 October 1529

A five-page letter wholly in cipher, written as Clement VII left for Bologna, deciphered here with George Lasry’s 2023 key: the Pope’s dread of a general council, a feigned illness, soothsayers and a dream.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 18 September 2026 · updated 15 October 2026

Method: read with known key · Extent: complete

Credits · George Lasry (key or decipherment used, unpublished earlier solution)

Summary. Gabriel de Gramont, bishop of Tarbes and Francis I’s ambassador to Clement VII, wrote to Anne de Montmorency from Rome on 11 October 1529, as the Pope left for the congress of Bologna. After one clear line the letter is cipher for five pages: 171 lines, 4,002 signs. The key was already known: George Lasry reconstructed “Gramont’s cipher (1529)” in 2023 from sibling letters, and Satoshi Tomokiyo published his table as an image on cryptiana. Lasry also deciphered the letter at the time, but the decipherment was not published. The work here was a new glyph-level transcription and its decryption with that table. The letter covers the Pope’s dread of a general council and his plan to grant one and then stall it, his offer to feign illness on the road until the Admiral Chabot arrives, the soothsayers who foretell his deposition, a dream he had the night before he left Rome, and a Florentine deal the prince of Orange was floating. Extent: complete; every line deciphers to French, 12 of the 4,002 signs unidentified.

01 The letter and its key

The letter is BnF fr. 3091 no. 23, ff. 45r–47v, in the volume of Raince’s papers (Gallica btv1b9060253s, filmed as two-page openings, views 49–52). It came to the project as one of four letters to Montmorency grouped as catalogue item 6, which guessed “four letters by three hands, probably three keys”. Matching each against Tomokiyo’s pages on the ciphers of François I showed four keys, one per letter. Since 14 October 2026 each key has its own page: Langeac at Venice in 1529 (Avranches’ cipher 1), Gramont again in 1530 (Gramont’s cipher, 1530) and the cardinal de Mâcon in 1537, now with his other letters in Mascon’s cipher. Each is a different cipher, with its own page.

This letter’s key is Lasry’s “Gramont’s cipher (1529)”, built from the siblings fr. 3040 f. 16 and Clairambault 330 f. 53 and dated 5 November 2023. It is a monoalphabetic substitution with two to seven homophones for the common letters, signs for doubled letters, a handful of nulls, and a few code signs for persons. Tomokiyo notes that the published image carries no plaintext, and none of the letter is transcribed or printed elsewhere that we could find.

02 Gramont, Rome, 11 October 1529: the Pope on the way to Bologna

The letter runs to 171 lines across ff. 45r–47v, closing A Romme le xime jour d’octobre over the signature G. de Gramont e. de Tarbe. The year is 1529. The Pope has just left Rome (Clement VII set out for Bologna on 7 October 1529), and Gramont leaves “demain” to join him at Perugia. The letter opens with the bull of the four décimes on the French clergy, raised for the princes’ ransom under the peace of Cambrai, and the prince of Orange is before Florence. In outline, modernised (the full decipherment, page by page, is in the repository):

  • The council. Whatever the Emperor says or does only deepens the Pope’s distrust of that side. What won him to the King was the King’s message about the concile general, “lequel il craignoit sur toutes les choses de ce monde, combien qu’il monstrast … qu’il en ait grande envie”. Pressed, he confessed that he knew the council was being pushed to ruin him, and that he meant to grant it and then “s’arrester sur aucunes particularitez pour le differer, et que le delai lui succedast en rupture”.
  • Into the King’s arms. He would “du tout se jecter entre ses bras et se fier en luy seul”. Because the times do not allow open trust, the King and his ministers may say publicly, whenever it helps, that they have little reason to trust the Pope, so long as the goodwill underneath stays whole. “Avec bonnes parolles vous aurez de luy ce que vous voudrez”. His one aim is to get the Emperor out of Italy.
  • Feigning illness for the Admiral. The Emperor is bound “par ses chappitres”, the treaty of Barcelona, to bring the allies to full restitution or make war on them. Gramont has told Philippe Chabot, the Admiral, then on his embassy to Italy, to come as far forward as he can. The Pope offers to slow his journey, or to “faire le malade” in whatever town he is in, to wait for him. On 10 October he told Gramont he had already begun.
  • Soothsayers and a dream. The Pope, though he should not believe “tout plein de gens qui se meslent de deviner”, greatly fears what they say of him. Several have foretold that he will be deposed or will die within the year, and that it will come “du costé dudict” [the Emperor]. The night before he left Rome he had a dream, which Gramont encloses, “lequel l’a mis en merveilleusement grande fantasie”, because he dreamed it at the kind of hour at which earlier dreams of his had come true. He told it to one man only, and Gramont asks that only the King, Madame and those they choose should know of it.
  • Florence. The prince of Orange wanted to deal with [the Florentines]: restore [the Medici] to the state of Florence “comme il souloit”, in return for Pisa and Livorno being put in [the Emperor’s] hands. That is “chose grandement contraire” to what the Emperor had agreed with the Pope.

Gramont’s letter adds detail to the French side of the weeks before the congress of Bologna. The council, Florence and the Venetian and Ferrarese restitutions all serve as bargaining counters in it, and the Pope keeps a private line to France through the ambassador. The enclosed dream is not in the volume.

03 Method

No cryptanalysis was needed: the work was transcription and decryption. The key table was checked on the first full line (d r R + W C 4 # L ● T Δ K Lo ʒ r R ● decodes to tredecime v & avltre v). Each glyph was then given a one-character ASCII alias and a script turned the alias strings back into letters, so that every decryption can be checked against the transcription and re-run. The five pages were transcribed separately and in parallel, each by a model instance working from the same brief, key and crops, and then joined. The joins fall mid-sentence and agree. Points the transcribers found that the table does not give:

  • In Gramont’s hand the loose ǂǂ for a and the compact # for m cannot be told apart, so # is taken as a or m by context. A is the more frequent.
  • A triangle or ring on a stem over a foot bar looks like ♀ (o) but is a null, Lasry’s “4 with a flag”. It occurs about twenty times. Taken as o, it produces doepesche.
  • The heart, which Lasry lists only as a person sign, is always the King (“escript au ♡ et à vous”). Three large signs outside the table stand where the Emperor, the Florentines and the Pope or the Medici are expected. Those identifications come from context and are marked as such.

04 What remains uncertain

  • Twelve unidentified signs among the 4,002 and single letters on each page (listed in the transcriptions), a phrase on f. 45r (fier ni a nui) and one on f. 45v (en voi[e]), and the first word of f. 47v l. 7. The transcribers graded each page 90–95 % secure.
  • The identity of four code signs, which is inferred from context.
  • Prior art not yet seen: B. Wirtz-Daviau’s edition of the Admiral Chabot’s 1529 embassy to Italy, which may quote Gramont’s letters from a contemporary decipherment.
