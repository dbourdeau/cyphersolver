01 The letter and the code

In June 1761 the British had taken Dominica and were gathering troops there; Martinique expected to be next, and was taken in February 1762. On 12 June the governor general of the Windward Islands, Louis-Charles Le Vassor de La Touche, and the intendant, Pierre-Paul Le Mercier de La Rivière (later known as a physiocrat), wrote to the Navy minister. The copy in the archive is the duplicata. Its first page is in clear: the enemy at Dominica, a second convoy expected from New England, the fleet before Belle-Île that could turn on the islands. Then, “voicy ce que nous faisons aujourd’huy”, the letter changes to numbers.

The minister in June 1761 was Nicolas René Berryer (secretary of state for the Navy from 1758 to October 1761). The Duc de Choiseul, who took the Navy after him, appears in the cipher as the minister who had promised provisions; his name is one of the words the encipherer left in clear: “423, D.. 614. Choiseul”, that is le Duc de Choiseul.

A clerk wrote in the margin of the first page: “déchiffrée et remise à M. Accaron par M. Villain”. The bureau deciphered the letter when it arrived and gave the decipherment to Accaron, the head clerk for the colonies. That decipherment is not filed with the letter, and the inventory notes that the letter “n’a pas été transcrite en clair”.

The code has numbers for single letters (674 = s, 321 = r, 280 = n), syllables (81 = ma, 313 = che, 667 = tion) and whole words (533 = notre, 380 = vivre, 460 = farines). The numbers are not in alphabetical order: it is a two-part code, as was the printed form the Navy used for Martinique in 1765 (C8 A 67 f° 178, “Chiffre utilisé pour les correspondances avec M. d’Ennery”). That later key does not fit this letter: in it 614 is voisins and de is 428, and its numbers run to 800 while this letter never goes past 696.

02 The key from a sibling letter

Searching the inventory of the Martinique correspondence for cipher found one other letter of these months: La Rivière’s no. 178 of 21 August 1761 to Berryer (C8 A 63 f° 208), on the Saint Eustatius supply contract and the colony’s credit. It uses the same code, and a clerk wrote the decipherment above every coded line.

The 19 pairs of clear line and code line in that letter were aligned group by group. “Saint Eustache” comes three times as 371 201 674 361 313 (Saint, eu, s, ta, che); notre is 533 twice, je 286, de 614, le 423, s 674. About 110 values came out of it, each graded by how many places support it. Applied to the June letter they gave a value to 67% of its groups at once.

The rest came from the June letter itself, one repeated phrase at a time, and each value had to fit every place where its number occurs:

In English: the Spanish ships and Choiseul’s provisions have not come, and the colony has food only for day-to-day use. The two officials have asked their [unread word] at Saint Eustatius, the Dutch free port, for 3,000 barrels of flour and 1,200 of beef, at 60 and 100 livres the barrel, and expect prices to rise. If the attack comes only in December the colony must be fed until then; at least ten thousand people, “blancs que noirs”, eat a thousand barrels of beef a year. They will pay half in bills of exchange on the treasury and half in money or produce, and ask the minister to see that the bills are honoured. A full translation is in translation.txt.

04 How it was done

  1. Every coded line was transcribed from the ANOM images: 818 groups, 217 different numbers (transcription.txt, groups.txt).
  2. The 1765 Martinique key was transcribed in full (800 entries) and tested: it does not fit.
  3. A ciphertext-only attack was tried first: simulated annealing mapping each number to an entry of the 1765 vocabulary, scored by a French language model of the period. On a control letter enciphered the same way at the same length (818 groups, 218 numbers) it got only 5–9% right, and its wrong keys scored better than the true one. Its output on the real letter was therefore discarded.
  4. The sibling letter of 21 August 1761 was found through the inventory, its 19 line pairs transcribed and aligned, and the key built from them.
  5. The key was extended from context in the June letter over several passes, every value checked against all its occurrences, and the reading measured.

The share that reads as sense is measured, not estimated (measure_sense.py): each coded passage is decoded, split into dictionary words (a French corpus plus a declared list of the letter’s own spellings), and a number counts only if everything it yields falls inside a word. 771 of 818 numbers have a value (94.3%); 754 read as sense (92.2%). A statistical check of the key against random and refitted keys (_validate.py) passes: the reading scores −1.97 per letter against −2.28 for keys fitted to shuffled text and −3.59 for random keys.

05 What remains

  • About 40 numbers that stand for whole words and occur once, 47 groups in all: 408 and 451 (“ni en [408] ni [451] dans la colonie”), 339 and 75 (where the “revolution” would happen, and where “it is happening to us”), 100 and 511 (“l’[100]ce[511] est à notre porte”), 183, 403, 154, 110, 138, 413, 459, 638, 82, 173 and others. Their sentences admit several words; they are left open rather than guessed. Blocker: no key material beyond the two letters.
  • Values that rest on a single context are graded C in the key and marked [?] above (for example 525 vaisseau, 193 vi, 409 secour, 394 M., 273 peu, 88 cas, 540 port, 392 très, 122 jours).
  • Two slips of the encipherer, checked on the image: “à r-ro-i-s livres” with 321 (r) where trois needs t, and “nous [16]frons” where the sense is offrons but 16 is ge elsewhere.
  • The key itself, and the clear decipherment that Villain gave Accaron, were not found online. The minister’s outgoing registers for 1761–62 (COL B 113, 114) and the Navy’s file of colonial keys of 1741–1776 (Archives nationales MAR/G/178, with keys of 1758, 1766 and 1771) are not digitised.

Translation

sibling178_translation.txt

# English translation of the decipherment of the time on ANOM COL C8 A 63 f. 208 (La Riviere to Berryer, no. 178,
# Martinique, 21 Aug 1761), with the clear passages, from sibling178_decipherment.txt. [...] = word not legible here.

The delay in a ship's departure for France lets me tell you the following. Our contract with Saint Eustatius for
our provisioning has broken down, and it could not be replaced, because there are at Saint Eustatius neither beef
nor flour, and none is expected. I turned back quietly; I am taking advantage of several fortunate circumstances
whose combination has suddenly brought a great deal of provisions into the colony. I am paying in large part with
my own bills, at 25 per cent, and I have bought at more than 20 and 25 per cent; [the] contract [is] better than the
price agreed with Saint Eustatius. This leads me to place other funds in the hands of Messrs Delaborde and De
Montmartel, in bills on the treasurers of the colonies. The news of the capture of Belle-Isle does not help to
restore their credit; however that may be, as long as mine can hold, you will want for nothing here. I hope that
with this help, the provisions now in the island and those that may come from here in two months will put us in a
state to withstand our enemies' efforts for a very long time; we expect them always in December at the latest.
