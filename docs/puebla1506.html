De Puebla to Almazán, London, 21 April 1506

Ferdinand’s ambassador in London wrote to the King’s secretary in cipher about Katharine’s household while Philip and Juana waited to sail; Tomokiyo’s table deciphers four of its passages into Spanish sentences.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 27 October 2026

Method: read with known key · Extent: partial (56% of the cipher)

01 The letter

The unit has four images on PARES: the letter (image 1), two blank pages, and the address leaf (image 4). The address reads Al muy noble señor el señor Miguell Perez dalmaçan secretario y del consejo del Rey nuestro señor; the endorsement, written across the leaf, gives del doctor de la puebla … xxj … abril 1506, and the date line on the letter itself is de Londres a xxj de abril. The image carries the archive’s watermark PTR,LEG,54,78, a pencilled “PR54-78” and the foliation 179. The PARES id is the one Tomokiyo gives for this letter; the catalogue first had 2208061, which is Puebla’s letter of 15 April 1507.

Philip and Juana, King and Queen of Castile since Isabella’s death, were driven ashore in England in January 1506 on their way from Flanders to Spain, and stayed until late April, when they sailed from Falmouth (Falamua in the Spanish of the time) to La Coruña. The clear opening of the letter says that Puebla had written at length to the King and to Almazán by way of Martín Sánchez de Zamudio, the Bilbao shipowner, and that the King and Queen were waiting for the weather at Falmouth. Those clear readings are uncertain at the size PARES serves and are not part of the reading files.

02 The cipher

Tomokiyo calls it Puebla’s First Cipher and identifies it with Bergenroth’s key no. 6. He reconstructed the table from the letters of 1496–1507 before finding the original, and lists this letter among those that use it, without a reading. The table gives one sign for most letters and two for a, i and u/v: a is written q or M, i is b or a 5-like sign, u and v share a small o with the b of b(v). Several letters are written with the shape of another letter (e for l, g for f, f for g, a for h, R for t), and r is the figure-like sign 46. The transcription names each sign by a short ASCII label (key_first_cipher.tsv), and the decoder works on those labels.

Words that the Great Roman Numeral code holds were written as code words instead. Only one occurs here, three times: DCCCLXXXVIII, “el Rey de Inglaterra” on key image 51 of the contemporary code book (AGS Patronato Real leg. 52 doc. 33). The scribe writes CC and CCC as ligatures distinguished by a small mark, and the mark was not checked on this letter, so the value stays doubtful at every occurrence.

The letters run on without word division. Doubled letters are written twice. One sign in l. 13 (in ra?on) is not in Tomokiyo’s table, and the table has no z: in l. 12 a hooked form of the o-sign seems to stand for it (plazer).

03 How it was deciphered

WAINUT transcribed the cipher passages of lines 11–29 from three-times enlarged crops of the PARES image, 370 signs and three code words, and decoded them with the table unchanged (decode.py). A second agent then re-read lines 14–20, 24–26 and 29 on fresh crops in its own sign labels and reproduced every reading letter by letter, with three corrections: abra quien, not abra bien, in l. 16; a single c in inclusas; and the words va a at the start of the passage in l. 29.

The controls ask whether the table could produce Spanish from these signs by chance.

  • Language model (the project’s shared es-golden-age model, Spanish 1490–1650, five-letter context, no spaces): the decode of lines 14–29 scores −1.69 per letter. 1,000 keys made by shuffling the table’s values among its signs score at best −4.73 (median −6.12); the true key on 1,000 texts with the signs shuffled within each line scores at best −4.26 (median −4.77). On the word-divided reading, the language check ranks Spanish first (−1.62), ahead of Catalan (−1.95) and Portuguese (−2.09).
  • Word list: a short list of common Spanish words, without the rare content words, covers 0.61 of the decoded letters; shuffled keys cover at most 0.29 and shuffled texts at most 0.26.

The decode is therefore not an artefact of the table or of the transcription; what limits the reading is the clear text around it.

04 The passages that read

[…] por aquella gente hazia, por su propio interese, que la princesa me escriviese estas cartas que aqui van inclusas. […]

Los de la señora princesa veo tan bien ataviados que mas no pense ver. Por la codicia de algunas personas [?] [?] es tal que con [?el] tesoro de {el Rey de Inglaterra?}, dudo que se [?contentasen] […]

[…] va a beneficio de natura con este […]

[…] those people brought it about, for their own interest, that the princess wrote me these letters, which go enclosed here. […] I see the lady princess’s people so finely dressed that I did not think to see more. [?For] the greed of certain persons [?] [?] is such that, even with [?the] treasure of {the King of England?}, I doubt they would be [?content] […] is going along at the mercy of chance with this […]

“A beneficio de natura” is a set phrase of the period, “left to nature”, that is to chance: Cisneros’s secretaries write in 1516 that something “no se ha de dexar a beneficio de natura”.

The day after this letter, Katharine wrote to her father from Richmond that her people were ready to ask alms and that she herself was all but naked (CSP Spain I no. 459, 22 April 1506). Puebla, whom she distrusted and blamed for her poverty, tells the King’s secretary in cipher that her household is finely dressed beyond what he expected, and that the letters she writes are prompted by people around her for their own interest. The two letters belong to the long quarrel over Katharine’s maintenance and her dowry after Prince Arthur’s death, in which Puebla took Henry VII’s side often enough for Katharine to call him more the English King’s vassal than Ferdinand’s servant.

05 Lines 11–17

The earlier cipher lines decode as well, but between them are clear words that could not be read, so they do not build sentences. Secure words are y de la partida, de todo el [?aresto], con mucha [?razon], señora princesa, conviene, bien y acrecentamiento, de {el Rey de Inglaterra?} y del principe and abra quien le [?pedir] que ningun malcontentamiento. The departure and the “arrest” may be Philip and Juana’s detention in England and their sailing, and the prince is presumably the Prince of Wales, Henry; with the clear words missing, neither can be confirmed. The decode of these lines is shown in the full texts below as decoded, not read.

The first words, y de la partida, are also in a note Tomokiyo left in the source of his “Spanish ciphers” page next to his entry for this letter (an HTML comment, not shown on the page). He had begun to decipher it and published nothing further.

07 What remains uncertain

  • The clear text of the letter, about twenty lines in Puebla’s cursive: read only in part. At 1017 × 1413 pixels, the largest size PARES serves, many words cannot be read with confidence; a better scan from Simancas would allow it.
  • Lines 11–17: the fragments do not build sentences, because the clear words between them are not read and descenders from the lines above run through ll. 11–13; l. 12 ([?desto] [?stares] … [?aresto]) divides uncertainly. Graded C (conjectural) as a whole.
  • Unread signs: one in l. 13 (ra?on, presumably z, not in the table) and two after personas in l. 25.
  • Doubtful readings: pedir for the decoded pedri (l. 16, C); contentasen across the line end (ll. 26–27, M); the article el before tesoro (l. 26, clear, M); plazer with the hooked sign (l. 12, C).
  • The code word DCCCLXXXVIII, “el Rey de Inglaterra”: its value fits each of the three places, but the ligature mark that fixes its C-count was not checked on this letter (M).
  • A parallel reading: Brice Dudout read the same letter the same day, independently, with the same table, and published it on Europe chiffrée; his files are in the repository folder (dudout/). The two decipherments agree word for word on most passages, and he adopted two readings from this one (“a beneficio de natura” and “por aquella gente hazia”). Where this page has a doubt, he reads z in razón (l. 13), de la for da la, and pedrique (from pedricar) for pedri (l. 16), and he does not count the two signs after personas. He also transcribes the clear text of ll. 11–17. None of this is taken here yet: it needs a look at the image, which PARES refused on 27 October 2026.

# English translation

By Martín Sánchez de Zamudio I wrote at length to the King our lord, and the same to Your Grace. […] Doña María de Rojas […]. What I wish to say in this one is that the King of England […] so fair a wind that […] tomorrow […] he will embark. And he also says […] Doña María de Rojas […].

[…] when he pleases; and the departure […] and all the rest greatly please the King of England, and with much reason. […] that, on the part of my lady the Princess, […] is well fitting, as is the increase and {respect} of the King of England and of the Prince; and there will be no one to preach any discontent to her. Those people, for their own interest, were making the Princess write me these letters, which go enclosed herewith.

[…] My lady the Princess’s people I see so well attired that I never thought to see better; but the greed of certain persons is such that even with the treasure of the King of England I doubt they would be content. I trust in the prudence of Your Grace and of the King our lord, who will not allow […]. And because […] is left at the mercy of chance with this one (?). I say no more […] [may Our Lord] increase and prosper the life and estate of Your Grace as you desire. From London, 21 April 1506.
