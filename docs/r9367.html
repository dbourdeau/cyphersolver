A son to his father, 22 March 1535

Go to the full text ↓

A young man writes home that he was questioned and denied everything; the dangerous lines are in a key copied in the Bavarian chancery’s key register, and the key gives phrases from them, but not sentences.

At a glanceKnown key applied · partial

The letter
A son to his father (“gunstiger herr vater”), Monday after Palm Sunday, 22 March 1535, in German; the father and the place are not named in the clear text.
Where
Bayerisches Hauptstaatsarchiv, Munich, Kurbayern Äußeres Archiv 4591 f. 169; DECODE R9367, part of catalogue entry 161, listed with no decipherment.
The cipher
Runs of invented signs inside the clear German: one sign per letter, a few signs for words (und, auch, das) and for ch.
How it was deciphered
R9423, a register in the same volume copying out nineteen keys of the 1520s and 1530s, has a block headed “H. Wilh. [15]25”; once its frequent signs were matched to the letter’s by shape, it gives German. Five values were added from the letter itself.
What it says
Phrases only, not sentences: he was asked about strange arts (“[?selczam] khinst”) and denied everything; he is taken for a poor writer and has learned nothing reliable yet; he fears for his person; the Turks are coming up.
Still open
The cipher as sentences. The re-reading of 23 October 2026 built a full reading of the letter, but four of its values rest on sense alone, two signs and the passages of lines 9–10, 14, 16, 31 and 34 stay open, and with those counted as holes the letter is still decoded, not read.

Until 15 October 2026 this letter was filed on one page with thirteen other ciphertexts of the same key volume. They are different ciphers, each with its own page; the largest group is System A′ of the dukes’ agents in Hungary.

01 The letter

The clear German opens with the date, “Anno dni. 1535. den 22 marcij an montag nach palmarum”. From the partial reading of 23 October 2026 (r9367/R9367_reading.md, English R9367_en.md), with the cipher passages set in the clear text: he was summoned and questioned about strange arts and denied everything (“[?selczam] khinst gefragt, ich hab aber alles [g] verlaugnet”); he gave himself out as a poor writer, sent to learn because he is still young (“ich sey am schlechter shreiber der mainung gesent worden nach dem ich noch jung bin etwas zu lernen und erfaren”); he has learnt nothing reliable yet and will report once he reaches the place; he fears for getting himself through (“[?meinesn] leib hindurch bringen”); and many Turks are moving up (“vil Tircken [?uerauf] ziechen”). The name read earlier as Nothaft is the word botschaft. The postscript reads “Ich vernim Friderich Schmalcz vergunt euch nicht guets”: one Friedrich Schmalcz begrudges the father anything good. The back of the leaf (image P2) has no address, only two pen crosses, so the father and the place are still unnamed.

Decoded, not read. Several values of this reading rest on sense alone (below), and the passages of lines 9–10 (“die [#] [?fleih] [?danck] geben worden”), 14, 16, 31 and 34 do not build sentences. Counted as holes, they leave the letter under the reading bar (check of 23 October 2026), so the reading above is partial. The DECODE image was fetched again and viewed rotated: the failures are key values, not transcription.

02 The key

Block 2_4 of the key register R9423, headed “H. Wilh. [15]25”, gives A 9, B v, C Δ, D +, E 6, F ⊥, G o, H 4, I w, K ‡, L 8, M ¥, N 3, O a barred D, P two rings, Q 2, R 7, S y, T ♉, V a doubled ‡, X o-o, Y ε, Z π, with und 9, auch Z, ch t, das T. Applied with the original sign assignments it failed; it fits once the letter’s frequent signs are matched to the block by shape. The block’s name signs (for Bavaria and Duke Ludwig) do not fit the letter: here they behave as nulls or word dividers. The letter adds five values of its own (q a, Λ r, C s, H k, K z). The re-reading of 23 October 2026 re-transcribed every cipher run on the full-resolution image and added three values that hold at every occurrence (v = b or w, ‡‡ = u, boxed H = k) and four that rest on sense alone, for review: cc = z (zeit, ziechen, Schmalcz), Φ = b (bin, bringen, botschaft), S = sch, U = ll.

03 What stays open

  • The letter is decoded, not read: the four sense-only values (eleven words), the open signs and the passages of lines 9–10, 14, 16, 31 and 34 need the open signs or a second letter in this key. (The first count, 78 of 143 cipher words, 54.5%, is withdrawn as a reading figure.)
  • Signs not in the key block: a triple-barred cross [#] (13 times, mostly where nothing is needed), an arch sign [Π] (4 times, a word or name sign), and R and d once each; the contexts do not fix them.
  • The place name in lines 1–2; and the sign pairs in the right margin beside lines 30–33, probably a reader’s equations.
  • The back of the letter (image P2) was examined on 23 October 2026: no address, only two pen crosses.

KAA 4591 f. 169, 22 March 1535 decoded, not read

A son to his father, Monday after Palm Sunday 1535, in German with cipher passages: he was questioned about strange arts and denied everything, claiming to be a poor writer sent to learn because he is young; he has not yet learnt anything thorough and will report when he reaches the place; he will bring his body through; someone asked how 'he' brings [a person, code sign] the message; many Turks are moving up. Postscript: Friedrich Schmalcz begrudges the father nothing good. Cipher runs in key R9423 block 2_4, extended.

A mechanical decode with holes in most sentences: shown so that the transcription and the key can be checked and improved, not as a reading.

Summary: A young man (still "jung", sent to learn) writes to his father on Monday after Palm Sunday 1535 from a
court or journey. He says he has been questioned about strange things ("seltsam künste gefragt") and denied
everything, that he is held a poor writer, that he has not yet learned anything friendly/reliable, and that he
carries great worry for his own body/safety. In cipher he reports news: the Turks are to come up ("Tirckhen herauf
ziechen"), that he was asked by some people, a name like Nothaft, and in the postscript something about (Count
Palatine?) Friedrich and Schmalkalden(?) and "euch nicht guets" (nothing good for you). The father is told he will
be informed more fully in the next letter.
