Marshal Matignon to Henri III from the camp before Monségur, May 1586. Two letters of autumn 1586 from the Duke of Mayenne’s side, sender not named; the cover of f. 120 (f. 121v) is addressed to Villeroy.

01 The three letters

Satoshi Tomokiyo (cryptiana, Henry III’s Cipher with Ambassadors) distinguishes several ciphers in the papers of the Guyenne war of 1585–86 in BnF fr. 15572–15573. He lists these three letters as having no known decipherment and as written in the cipher he calls Mayenne–Forget’s Cipher-2, and he publishes a reconstruction of its key. The project keeps one entry per key. These three letters are in Cipher-2, so they form this entry. The Mayenne–Forget despatches in Cipher-1 and Matignon’s slips in Cipher-3 have their own entries, and their code numbers mean different things.

02 The key

Tomokiyo’s table is a homophonic substitution: several invented signs and figures stand for each common letter. Other signs stand for syllables and short words such as de, la, que, est, pour and monsieur, and some figures for words, for example 23 apres, 24 peu and 31 pas. A slash marks some word ends.

Burford checked the table on Mayenne’s letter to the King from the camp of Libourne, September 1586 (fr. 15573 ff. 103v–104r), whose decipherment of the time is written in the margins. He then read five more letters in the same volume that carry their decipherment (ff. 7v–8r, 32r–33r, 97r, 149r–150r). From them he took values for code signs: armée, tout, the ending -ée and 17 tant, and two more that he marks doubtful. Harumi-Matsuba used Tomokiyo’s table for ff. 218–219 and Burford’s key for f. 120.

Two readings of the key still conflict between the contributors. For Harumi-Matsuba a barred h is armée, while Burford separates two similar signs, one tout and the other armée. A sign the table gives as q also stands where the decipherments of the time have pour.

03 Monségur, May 1586

Matignon wrote to the King from the siege of Monségur, which Mayenne’s army took in May 1586. The despatch opens in clear (the battery began at first light and lasted until sunset), and the cipher continues the story. Harumi-Matsuba’s reading begins:

Et y ayant esté tiré pres de douze cens coups, mais ne feirent pas toutesfois tant d’effect que l’on s’estoit promis, parce que la muraille se trouva aussi beaucoup meilleure que l’on ne cuidoit.

Three breaches were made, meant for a lodgement on one of them. The sieur Sacramore de Birague, on guard on that side, tried for it that evening, was badly wounded and had to fall back. Matignon held back the young nobles and now means to advance step by step. The army needs relief, as the sieur de Sailly has already reported. Something the cipher hides behind code 36 will have to come from Brittany to last until the harvest. The forces shrink every day, and pay matters most of all. The letter closes on Mayenne’s fever, which the physicians judge free of danger.

This reading rests on a single pass by Claude over the signs. On six lines of another Cipher-2 letter with a known answer, the same method agreed with the decipherment on 74.8% of letters. A second transcription by OpenAI’s Codex, made without the first, agrees with it on 72–86% of values per page. The gist is secure, but individual words are not.

04 Autumn 1586

f. 120 has no clear text in the cipher, but its cover survives as f. 121v, addressed “Pour Monseigneur de Villeroy” and docketed 1586 (found on the images, 23 October 2026), so the letter went to the secretary of state Villeroy. It is decoded, not read: the partial reading below rests on the decode, and its open stretches fall in most sentences. In Harumi-Matsuba’s partial reading, “this prince” is more vexed than ever. The treasurer Bilart has had thirty thousand écus in gold ready for ten days, and the Cardinal of Guise writes that five thousand are on the way. But “not five thousand but a hundred and five thousand” would not set the army right. The Marshal of Biron cannot be kept, deputies of the army have come, and papers of the secretary La Marsilière have fallen into “our” hands. The transcription was checked against an independent one by Codex (92% and 90% agreement on values), but not by a person. A word-by-word check of the reading against the decode (23 October 2026) restored the cipher’s own spelling and bracketed words only partly carried by the signs; recto lines 15–19 and 24–31 and verso lines 3–5 still do not hold, and one line checked on the image shows that finances rests on a look-alike sign.

f. 385, in Burford’s reading, describes the royal army in Guyenne breaking up as winter begins. Fewer than a thousand foot are left, men ask for leave, and there is no powder. The writer weighs what to do over the winter with the regiments and the foreign troops. He considers sending the landsknechts who were in Champagne and keeping a company of light horse, and he names Monsieur d’Halincourt, Monsieur de Matignon, La Roche and probably the young Aubeterre. Five passes of Claude were voted sign by sign, and on known text the voted reader got 93% of signs right. Burford’s v0.2 is itself a partial, unspaced decode; divided and punctuated here on 23 October 2026, it still has holes in most sentences, so f. 385 too is decoded, not read, and the account above is of its readable stretches only.

05 How it was checked

  • One measure for all three. Both contributors’ files were put in one format (to_read.py) and measured with the rule used for the Cipher-1 and Cipher-3 entries. A sign counts only inside a run of at least three words of a period lexicon (fr-1600-letters) totalling ten letters or more. The result is 1,856 of 2,138 signs for ff. 218–219, 1,002 of 1,103 for f. 120 and 1,702 of 2,052 for f. 385.
  • Shuffled keys. With the letter values shuffled, 20 keys give medians of 10–16% per letter and at most 31%.
  • Sense is not correctness. The measure shows the key turns the transcriptions into French. It does not show that each sign was named right, and the calibrations put that at 75–93% per sign.
  • On the images. Here the line counts and layout of ff. 218v–219r, f. 120r and f. 385r were compared with the contributors’ files, and the clear closing and signature of f. 219r were transcribed. All three letters show the same signs (barred crosses, three dots, double slashes, figures). f. 120r also has a few small superscript marks over some signs, which neither contributor mentions; examined on 23 October 2026, they are cipher signs, not glosses.

06 What stays open

  • About 22 code signs without a value, most used once: on f. 385 the figures 14, 15 and 30 and a sign that probably stands for vostre majesté; on ff. 218–219 code 36 (perhaps victuals) and eight others; on f. 120 thirteen kinds of sign.
  • Short stretches that do not read in all three letters. In ff. 218r–219r, which had one reading pass only, they are scattered; in f. 120 (recto lines 15–19, 24–31, verso 3–5) and f. 385 (36 stretches) they fall in most sentences, which is why those two letters are decoded, not read. The next step is a sign-by-sign re-reading of those lines on the images (Gallica views 246–247 and 752–753).
  • A check of the transcriptions by a person, and the conflicting key values above.
  • Whether George Lasry, whom Tomokiyo credits with identifying f. 385 and with its opening fragment, read more of it.

## Summary
From the camp before Monségur to the King (May 1586).
- A battery from dawn to sunset, of about 1,200 shots, did less than hoped: the wall proved stronger than expected. Three breaches were made, the plan being to lodge on one of them.
- In the evening the sieur Sacramore de Birague, on guard on the side of the breach, went for the lodgement, was badly wounded and had to withdraw. The breach was defended from behind by a piece of wall that the guns had not brought down, and the lodgement was not taken. The defenders lost many of their best men; on this side some soldiers were lost and about twenty "b…" (with "…essés" at the head of f. 218v, read as blessés, the wounded: an inference).
- The young nobles were held back. From now on, less firing and a step-by-step approach by lodgements.
- The army is worn out and needs relief and provision for the future. The writer awaits the King's decision on how to use the army after the siege, on the Duke of Mayenne's proposals.
- Something (sign 36, value unknown; guessed to be victuals, since it must last until the harvest) will be hard to find in sufficient quantity in the province and must be brought from Brittany. The forces shrink every day, and the troops not from the region want to leave. Most important of all is the payment of the troops.
- The Duke of Mayenne has a fever, but the physicians are hopeful and there is no sign of danger.
