The Great Captain to Lorenzo Suárez, 17 August 1500 (Espagnol 318 no. 92)

Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba’s price for taking the fleet against the Turk, in the Catholic Monarchs’ general cipher; the key, proved against a later hand’s glosses, deciphers about three quarters of the letter.

At a glanceKnown key applied · partial, about three quarters of the words

The letter
Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, the Great Captain, to Lorenzo Suárez de Figueroa, Spain’s ambassador at Venice, 17 August 1500. This is the copy sent to the secretary Almazán.
Where
Bibliothèque nationale de France, Espagnol 318 no. 92, f. 116r–v (Gallica, canvases 440–441). It is one of five ciphered letters in the volume, catalogue item 9.
The cipher
The Cifra general de los Reyes Católicos: invented signs for letters, up to five for a vowel, mixed with three-letter code groups (683 in the key) for words and names.
How it was deciphered
Galende Díaz printed the key in 1994 and Tomokiyo assigned the letter to it. A later hand’s glosses between the lines confirm it: eight words, no misses. Both pages were then transcribed and deciphered with the key.
What it says
He will take the fleet against the Turk, but at a price. He wants harbours in Italy for his sick, his wounded and his stores, as the Pope had given him before.
Still open
Five code groups that contradict the printed key, and about ten stretches of letter signs.
Other letters
The other ciphered letters of the volume use other ciphers and have their own pages: no. 5 (Escrivà’s cipher), no. 95 (Lasry’s alphabet) and nos. 93–94 (key not identified).

01 The volume and this letter

The volume (ark btv1b52503046q, 486 canvases, 127 folios) is the autograph collection of the Ruffo di Scilla princes, sold in 1854 and bought by the BnF in 1857; Morel-Fatio catalogued it in 1892 as no. 172. Its hundred items are letters to Ferdinand and Isabella from most of the crowned heads of Europe. Five are wholly or partly in cipher, and the catalogue had listed four. Until 14 October 2026 this page covered all five. They use at least four different ciphers, so each now has its own page:

All five are in one office’s style. A cipher of Ferdinand’s secretariat is two things at once: a homophonic alphabet, three to five invented symbols per letter, and a nomenclator of two-, three- and four-letter groups (consonant, vowel, consonant) written in ordinary Latin letters in the same hand, standing for words, names and syllables. The nomenclator is one-part: the vocabulary runs alphabetically and the groups run in a fixed order beside it, so the initial letter of a group tells you roughly where in the alphabet its plaintext sits.

02 The key confirmed against its glosses

F. 116 is headed by its sender, la respuesta q[ue] enbio a los s[eño]res en xbij de ag[osto] 1500, and opens in clear:

Muy mag[nifico] Señor: a los xiiij d[e]l presente recebi v[uest]ra letra con hun breve d[e]l papa y otras dos cartas del embax[ad]or veneçiano con uno suyo, dando priesa en my yda a la resistençia del turco. E porq[ue] esto es conforme a la voluntad e mandami[ento] q[ue] yo tengo de sus al[teza]s, en virtud de Jhu Xpo yo me dispongo a ello con toda la celeridad y presteza q[ue] el caso requiere…

and then alternates clear and cipher for two pages. It is Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, the Great Captain, writing to the Lorenzo Suárez of the notice (Lorenzo Suárez de Figueroa, Spain’s ambassador at Venice until his death there in 1506, whose own ciphered letters Galende Díaz lists), about the expedition against the Turk. This is the copy kept for the secretary Miguel Pérez de Almazán. Tomokiyo’s page on the Spanish ciphers notes that the Great Captain “used this cipher” (Ferdinand and Isabella’s general cipher of about 1500–02) in this letter, and lists the letter itself as having no known decipherment.

This could be tested, because a later hand wrote a partial decipherment between the lines of f. 116r. The glossator deciphered a few words and stopped; his words are the control.

Juan Carlos Galende Díaz printed the key in 1994, from a file of twelve ciphers in the Real Academia de la Historia (B.R.A.H. 9/15): his appendix 1 is the Cifra general de los Reyes Católicos, ff. 7–9 of that file. A gloss-anchored line then decodes straight through:

giving …y tiempo, por estar en tanto…, which is what the glossator wrote above it. F. 116v ends half in clear, and the codes sit inside sentences whose sense is clear without the key: …no desconfio que mucho se pudiera aprovechar … esta var … zin se ha ofreçido … yo me desnudo y descargo y me satisfago en que n[uest]r[o]…, with zin = Nápoles, xar = después, biz = toda, xuy = en. Below that a later inventory hand: Inventº 11 Ottº 53.

The key is transcribed here as keys/cifra_general.txt: 683 code groups from the plates (pp. 167–173), plus the twenty-one personal signs: Papa = A, Emperador = B, Rey de Françia = F, Rey de Spanya = G, Reyna de Spanya = 4, Principe de Spanya = C, Rey de Inglaterra = S, Rey de Napoles = □, Duque de Calabria = R, Infanta doña Juana = oTT, Don Fadrique prinçipe de Altamira = S†.

03 The two pages deciphered

Both pages were transcribed line by line on 21 September 2026, 36 lines on the recto and 11 on the verso, against the symbol alphabet Galende prints on p. 167. The hand leans on a few forms the specimen line had not shown: the fifth form of A (a reversed C with a hash) and the fifth form of E (a q with a k written below it) carry most of the vowels, the crossed T is the key’s “duplicate” sign for LL (vituallas, Pulla, llamó), and the clear hand’s con- abbreviation turns up inside the cipher (contenté con el, con color de). About three quarters of the words are deciphered. Put together with the clear passages, the letter says this:

…no deve pasar … si no grande remuneración … por estar en tanto peligro el negocio, e a tanto lexos … [Venice holds] Brindizi y Otranto [in] Pulla … que sera bien que [they give us] puertos de Italia donde esta armada se pueda recoger quando el tiempo lo requiriese, por no aver de bolver tan lexos para … poner nuestros dolientes y heridos y ropa e vituallas, [and refit] estos navios … sin los ynconvenientes de estar en casas agenas. [When the Pope called me] para el cerco de … le demandé lo mismo por [the means of] Garcilaso, y lo ovo por bien … de buena voluntad; e por que los alcaydes eran españoles y criados del Rey nuestro Señor, yo me contenté … me parece deveys, Señor, fablar en esto con color de nuestro descanso y reparo. … segund su necesidad no creo que nada desto les sea ynposible … yo me desnudo y descargo y me satisfago en que [the business is] en las manos.

That is Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba bargaining with Venice, through the Spanish ambassador there, before the joint campaign against the Turks at Cephalonia in the autumn of 1500: he will bring the fleet, but he wants Italian harbours of his own for it, as the Pope had given him. The full line-by-line transcription and decipherment are in f116_reading.md.

Two outside checks. Tomokiyo’s cryptiana page carries, commented out in its source, his own partial decipherment of the opening, “[paresce?] me que tan buena obra como esta con tanto diferencia e trabaio desto armada no deve pasar caso solo cargo sin grande [r]emuneración”, which corrects three groups first taken for misprints here: zap is zag = me, bac is vac = armada, and xie = cargo as printed. Sanuto’s Diarii (vol. III, cols. ~708–709) report the same demand from the Venetian side eight days later: the Spanish ambassador told the Venetian envoy in Rome that Gonsalvo wanted a cruciata, precedence, “qualli porto li piace a li bisogni” and victuals; off Corfù in October he told the Venetian proveditor he wanted a harbour “seperato” from theirs.

A third pass tested Sanuto’s terms (cruciata, jubileo, precedence) as cribs on recto lines 8–11. They are not there, since those lines weigh whether the enterprise suits Their Highnesses, but the pass settled zie as parescer, the value Tomokiyo guessed: zie followed by the letters R I A and tener gives pareceria tener, and the same value fits all five places. Lines 8–11 now decipher as si empresa a propósito de sus altezas … no hablo en ello … segun la necesidad dellos ay … en semejante acatamiento pareceria tener mas respeto a ….

04 What is not deciphered

Five code groups still contradict Galende’s printed values wherever they stand: bog (three times, printed Rey de Portugal, probably boz = todo), and zeb, zems, xob, bue. Either the 1994 plates misprint them, as they visibly misprint others, or the letter used a variant copy of the table; the manuscript, BRAH 9/15 ff. 7–9, which is not digitised, would settle it. About ten stretches of letter signs are also unplaced (grade I), mostly where the q-shaped N and the reversed-9 S cannot be told apart at this resolution. Words in the blockquote are grade C where the signs are certain and the Spanish runs, M where a word was completed from context. The share of about three quarters is an estimate by line from the prose reading; no token count was kept.

05 Method

Almost all of the work was bibliographic, and the order mattered: shelfmark first, cipher second. Searching for the cipher family found Galende Díaz’s appendix, which is the key; the interlinear glosses proved it; the reading is transcription against the printed alphabet. No key was recovered here. Line pitch comes from the autocorrelation of the ink profile (113 px on f. 116r), and three-line half-width strips at 2.6–3× are the readable unit for this hand.
