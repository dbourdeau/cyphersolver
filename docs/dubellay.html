At a glanceExisting decipherment · complete

The letters
Jean du Bellay, bishop of Bayonne and French ambassador in London, to Anne de Montmorency, grand master of France: four letters of 1529 with passages in cipher, and others of 1529 in the same cipher found in the sweep (16 June, 22 June, 30 June, 22 October). The item’s fifth letter, of 28 October 1528, is in another cipher.
Where
Bibliothèque nationale de France, fr. 3077 and fr. 3078 (catalogue item 4). The 16 June letter is fr. 3078 p. 21, DECODE R3688, catalogued there as not decrypted. The BnF notice gives day and month but no year.
The cipher
“Bayonne’s cipher” of 1529: an alphabet of drawn symbols with two to five signs for each letter, a few nulls, and single signs for words such as roy, madame, vous, faict.
How it was done
The letters were matched to their printed texts once their years were known. The key was re-derived from a sibling letter of 22 June 1529 that has the decipherer’s reading written over every sign. With it, lines 0–6 of the 16 June block were deciphered from the leaf before any printed text was found. Scheurer’s edition then supplied three word signs and the last line, and agrees word for word.
What it says
On the Cambrai peace talks of summer 1529: du Bellay advises that the powers for the treaty be dated de ce temps là et du lieu ou sera le Roy.
Still open
Three isolated signs in the last cipher line. Whether the boxed cross is one word sign or two (madame Marguerite here, le Cardinal on 17 October). The 22 October opening: Scheurer’s text is seen only in Google Books snippets, and the reading made here from the leaf (fr. 3005 ff. 165r–166r, 23 October 2026) leaves stretches of f. 165v and f. 166r unread, so it counts as decoded, not read.
Credit
Rémy Scheurer, Correspondance du cardinal Jean du Bellay t. I (1969), prints both passages in clear: no. 12 p. 41 (16 June) and pp. 111–112 (22 October). The other letters are in Joachim Le Grand (1688). Satoshi Tomokiyo’s table of the cipher (Cryptiana) was the starting point for the key.

01 The letters of the item

Jean du Bellay was ambassador of François I in England from 1527 to 1529, in the years of Henry VIII’s divorce suit and Wolsey’s fall. His despatches to Montmorency are bound in BnF fr. 3077 and fr. 3078 by day and month, across several years. Omont’s inventory, followed by the BnF notice, gives only the day and month and calls the letters lettre chiffrée or avec chiffre. The catalogue took this to mean that no decipherment was known. Once each letter was dated from its content and matched to Le Grand’s Histoire du divorce (1688), Brewer’s Letters and Papers IV and Bourrilly’s edition of the first embassy (1905), all five turned out to be printed in clear. Two more passages came up in the sweep of the volumes. The first is fr. 3078 no. 3, 16 June 1529 (p. 21), a clear letter with eight lines of cipher in the middle. It is not in Le Grand, Brewer or Bourrilly, and DECODE lists it as not decrypted. The second is the opening of 22 October 1529, which is not in these volumes (the signed original is fr. 3005 ff. 165r–166r). Le Grand prints the rest of that letter with the note le commencement de cette lettre est en chiffre non déchiffré. Brewer (L&P IV 6019) and Tomokiyo say the same. A DECODE search for the same correspondence found one more letter, fr. 3040 f. 68 (R4229), of 30 June 1529. It is also printed in clear by Le Grand (III 333 ff.) and calendared in L&P IV 5742.

Both of the passages left blank in 1688 are printed in clear in Rémy Scheurer’s Correspondance du cardinal Jean du Bellay, t. I (1969): the 16 June block as letter no. 12, p. 41, and the 22 October opening on pp. 111–112. So nothing in the item is without a printed decipherment. Scheurer’s volume was seen only in Google Books snippets.

02 Bayonne’s cipher of 1529 and its key

Tomokiyo reconstructs the cipher from two deciphered letters of June 1530 (Clair. 333 ff. 24, 27). It is a homophonic alphabet of drawn signs, with two to four signs for most letters, a few nulls, and word signs for roy and madame. There is no k or w, and u and v are one letter. Paul Friedmann’s nineteenth-century reconstruction is BnF NAF 4206 no. 5 (DECODE R9467), but no image is attached to that record.

The key used here was re-derived from fr. 3078 itself. The letter of 22 June 1529 (pp. 23–27, DECODE R3689) has the Court decipherer’s reading written above every cipher sign, and Le Grand prints its clear text, so sign and letter can be lined up one by one. This added signs that are not in Tomokiyo’s table: B and ∩ for o and a, and the word signs V = bien, R = fault and —ʔ = paix. Some examples:

03 The block of 16 June 1529 (fr. 3078 p. 21)

The letter is in clear except for eight lines. In the clear part du Bellay writes about a bishopric he hopes for and la menée qu’on me veult faire against him, and asks Montmorency to send his decision with the next despatch. The cipher begins after Monsr, where some clear words have been struck out, and the clear text resumes with Toutesfoiz, Monsr, vous ne luy en ferez semblant.

The block was transcribed sign by sign from the high-resolution crop on DECODE: 194 signs in eight lines (p21_signs.txt; an earlier count of 167 had skipped line 6). Before any printed text was known, lines 0–6 had been deciphered with the key above and Tomokiyo’s table:

si vous [?] trouvez bon l’advis que piecza vous ay envoé de faire semblant que madame ne fust allée pour conclure, et qu’estant là [boxed cross] luy ayt par importunité [R] envoyé querir pouoir, il ne [ℒ] faudroyt oublyer a le faire dater de ce temps là et du lieu …

This pass also fixed several signs. ẍ (x over x) is l, as in allée. ⊑ is q and stands for que. ⊽ is i, as in piecza and importunité. The diamond signs split into m, n and t by their stroke. One “=” stands for b in bon, where it is f everywhere else, which is either a slip or a homophone.

Scheurer’s edition then turned up. His letter no. 12 prints the whole block in clear, and it agrees with the reading from the leaf word for word. It also gave what the leaf reading lacked: ℒ = vous; R = faict here (the 22 June gloss has fault, so R is the word sign for fault/faict); the boxed cross with a loop at its centre = madame Marguerite; and line 7 = ou sera le Roy, ending in ꝛe, Tomokiyo’s word sign for roy. The sign ꝡ, which comes after vous in line 0 and after roy in line 7, has nothing matching it in the print either time, so it is a null.

The deciphered block, signs to letters, as transcribed here (word signs in small capitals in the reveal):

Monsr, si vous trouvez bon l’advis que piecza vous ay envoé de faire semblant que Madame ne fust allée pour conclure, et qu’estant là madame Marguerite luy ayt par importunité faict envoyé querir pouoir, il ne vous faudroyt oublyer a le faire dater de ce temps là et du lieu ou ser[a le] Roy.

My lord, if you approve the advice I sent you some time ago, to give out that Madame had not gone [to Cambrai] in order to conclude, and that, Madame Marguerite being there, she pressed her into sending for powers, you must not forget to have them dated from that time and from the place where the King will be.

Madame is Louise of Savoy and Madame Marguerite is Margaret of Austria, who were about to negotiate the Peace of Cambrai (signed 3 August 1529). Du Bellay’s advice seems to be about presentation in England: the French powers should look as if they had been asked for at Cambrai, not prepared in advance.

There are two small differences from the snippet of Scheurer’s text. The signs give et qu’estant là madame Marguerite (⊑ ✗ -ʊ- t a n t l a), where the snippet shows et que ostant madame Marguerite, which is probably a misprint or an OCR slip. And envoyé in line 1 is enciphered envoe, with no y. Of the 194 signs, 191 have a value (a letter, a word sign or a null), 98.5%. Every word of Scheurer’s text is accounted for by the signs.

04 The opening of 22 October 1529

Le Grand (III 377–379) and Brewer (L&P IV 6019) print this letter from Escripvant ceste lectre, ay sceu que monsr le Legat vient d’estre mys hors de sa maison onward and say that the opening is in cipher with no decipherment. Tomokiyo lists the copy, fr. 3005 f. 165, as unread. Scheurer (pp. 111–112) prints the letter from its first words, Monseigneur, la ou je vouldroye, si ne me sçauroye-je guarder d’accorder au cardinal d’York sa requeste, about a request Wolsey sent through an Italian servant who was still loyal to him (Agostino, his physician, according to Scheurer’s note). The snippets do not show whether Scheurer deciphered the passage himself or found a deciphered copy.

On 23 October 2026 the leaf was reached through the BnF’s IIIF service (fr. 3005, Gallica btv1b90599952, canvases 259–261): the signed original, 34, 35 and 5 lines of cipher on ff. 165r, 165v and 166r, with no decipherment on or near it. It was deciphered sign by sign with the 1529 key (texts/22oct1529_reading.md, with English). The first five lines agree with Scheurer’s snippets word for word, and the reading found new values (the cup = a, the barred cup = s, ƕ = f; R = faict, V = bien, 4 = madame confirmed). The first half builds sentences:

Mardi luy fut osté le seau, faict inventaire de tous ses biens jusques a ung escu, comandement faict a tous ceulx qui ont [?eu] manié quelque chose de luy depuys vingt ans de rendre compte de tout, dont ilz sont bien empeschez, car ne se trouant la sixiesme partie de [?ce] qu'on esperoit, ilz sont bien aseurez d'avoir maint tour de corde.

“On Tuesday the seal was taken from him; an inventory was made of all his goods down to a crown; an order was given to all who [?have] handled anything of his these twenty years to render account of everything, which troubles them greatly, for since not a sixth part of [?what] was hoped for is found, they are sure to get many a turn of the rope.” Wolsey chose to answer before the King rather than Parliament; the charges include the plunderings, lese-majesty, the legation, and bringing Ferrara into the league at Amiens without the King’s knowledge. The rest (f. 165v lines 21–35 and f. 166r lines 1–5) keeps unread stretches, and two word signs read as the two Kings rest on sense only, so the opening counts as decoded, not read; Scheurer’s complete text, seen only in snippets, would settle it.

05 Method and what was independent

  • The years of the five catalogue letters were found from their content and matched to Le Grand, Brewer and Bourrilly. That closed the item as already deciphered.
  • The key was re-derived from the 22 June interlinear decipherment and Tomokiyo’s table, and checked on the 17 October letter against Le Grand.
  • The 16 June block was transcribed and its lines 0–6 deciphered from the leaf before Scheurer was found. These lines hold 181 of the 194 signs, and only four of them were left without a value: ꝡ, ℒ, R and the boxed cross. That part of the reading is independent of the print. The values of those four (ꝡ a null, ℒ vous, R faict, the boxed cross madame Marguerite) and the reading of line 7 came from Scheurer.
  • Scheurer’s text for the 22 October opening was checked against the cipher only for its first five lines, the part the snippets show; they agree.

06 What remains uncertain

  • Three signs in line 7 of 16 June. Between lieu and ou are three signs with no counterpart in the print: ρ with a triangle foot, ȸ, and a small x. ȸ is probably Tomokiyo’s ƒ-shaped null; the other two are unexplained. A smudged sign stands where the print has a le before Roy. These are probably nulls or a slip, and they are too few to settle.
  • The boxed cross: one sign or two? On p. 21 Scheurer’s text gives the boxed cross as madame Marguerite. On p. 113 (17 October) a boxed cross lines up with le Cardinal in Le Grand’s text. The p. 21 sign has a loop at the centre of the cross, so the two may be different word signs. If they are the same sign, one of the two identifications is wrong. The p. 113 sign has not been re-examined to tell them apart, and this question is left open.
  • Scheurer’s text was seen only as Google Books snippets. A full copy would confirm estant against ostant and show which copy he used for 22 October.
  • 22 October 1529. Read sign by sign from fr. 3005 ff. 165r–166r on 23 October 2026: f. 165v lines 21–35 and f. 166r lines 1–5 keep unread stretches, and the word signs ꝑ and ℋ (the two Kings, by sense only), ꝏ and # are open.

# Jean du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to the Grand Maître (Montmorency), London, 22 June 1529: English translation

Letter of Monsieur du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to Monsieur the Grand Maître.

Monseigneur, what has given them the hottest alarm is that the Duke of Suffolk has written to the King his master — I
have seen his very letter — that the King had told him that, when the Ladies came to this parley, everything would be
done and concluded in two days; on that they judge that matters are already agreed, that nothing is needed but a little
ceremony, saying that in this you have dealt too falsely with them, always showing them, saying and writing that you saw
no foundation in the matter. I assure you, Monseigneur, that without pretence the Lord Legate is in terrible distress,
for until this very hour he had always assured his master and everyone, in private and in public, that it would come to
nothing, and that you would do nothing whatever without them. He now strongly suspects the contrary; and I leave you to
imagine whether he speaks to me, and whether he says that I deceived him and was the cause of his deceiving his master,
and consequently that I put him on a very bad road. I help myself at this and defend myself tooth and nail; yet in the end
he will come to seek me out again, and already, as you see, he has wanted me to go and see the King, and to serve him the
best talk in the world, without among other things putting him entirely out of hope that Monsieur Feuguillaume's coming
was not to put some delay into this Assembly; which I well feel I must do, partly to a good end and to keep these things
on a good course, partly so that he may extricate himself, so that, my having my share of the burden towards his said
master, his own may be so much the less. It will be very necessary, Monseigneur, that whoever comes to bring the news of
peace be well primed with fair words.

Also, Monseigneur, I have heard from a good source that Madame Marguerite is pressing you hard to come to a good
conclusion (you know whether it is true), alleging the trouble she had in getting her commission from the Emperor,
although all the ambassadors of his relatives and allies, the greatest lords of Spain, fell on their knees to him for it,
[saying] that he saw Monseigneur d'Orléans despairing of his life, his affairs in the worst state in the world in Italy,
everyone discontented in Spain and minded to mutiny, Germany so troubled, and the Turk's preparations as great as could
be; and that now, some of those causes having ceased and the others being able to cease in time, she feared greatly that
he would revoke the said commission, and that she already had some good diversion from it. I have heard nothing of it;
but it seems to me a good thing to say, and even to the Duke of Suffolk. It is true, Monseigneur, that having the children
is the main thing; but whoever can satisfy everything does well.

I believe Feuguillaume brought Monsieur de Suffolk orders to return; I understand they have letters from the King of the
16th, in which he put quite crudely that he was leaving Paris to go towards Cambrai, as if he were to be there himself,
with which I assure you the Cardinal of York would not be pleased. Monseigneur, the Legate would much like that, when the
King writes to me of his good affection and will to help the King his brother in his affair, he should expressly mention
that he [?comes] to employ himself in it all the more because he is and has continually been asked and pressed to do so by
my said Lord Legate; and it seems to me, Monseigneur, that it would be well to do so. You may perhaps take it ill that I
send you express couriers so often; but, Monseigneur, I would have to not write at all, for in the first place the packets
would not be carried, and besides, they would stay lying on the table, as I am sure they would; at every post there are
men of the Duke of Suffolk who would not miss me in so fair a field. The Cardinal of York has warned me of it closely
enough, and I find that a packet has been lost since I have been here, about which I am troubled.

You will see from the King's letters in what state they are in their divorce; all the help they ask of you with the Pope
is that he should not revoke his commission. As for your affairs, they are going well enough here; they have almost got
over half their grief, and hold the peace as made. Seeing they had gone so far, I preached with the Cardinal of York a
sermon that served the matter; I reckon that, since they have had a good [?July] preparation, it will be all the easier to
make them swallow the medicine one of these days. What greatly vexed the Lord Legate was a report he had had, from Rome as
I believe, that things were long since arranged and agreed through the Duke of Savoy, which I have shown him well is not
true. The King was very pleased with his artichokes, coming from where they come from, and makes much of them among his
friends; it was a small gesture well timed. My said Lord Legate asked me whether the Chancellor would be at this meeting; I
answered that I thought not, unless there were too great a likelihood of matters being concluded. He said he saw clearly
that Madame Marguerite, in wanting the said meeting to be from the 18th, is pressing on for two reasons: one, to prepare
the Emperor's passage; the other, to keep him [the Chancellor] from being there, thinking well that his presence would
not improve the said Emperor's affairs, knowing him to be quite clear-sighted. Therefore he advises that Madame should not
fail to bring my said Lord Chancellor, for if anything is done, he will be able better than another to see that you do not
lose in the bargain. Which will be the end of this letter, after I have commended myself humbly to your good grace and
asked our Lord to give you, Monseigneur, a good and long life. From London, the 22nd of June. Thus signed, your humble
servant J. du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne.

There can be no danger in having Campeggio's packet delivered to Salviati; I beg you, Monseigneur, to be pleased to order
it. On the back of the letter is written: To Monseigneur, Monseigneur the Grand Maître and Marshal of France.

# Jean du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to the Grand Maître (Montmorency), London, 17 October 1529: English translation

Letter of Monsieur du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to Monsieur the Grand Maître.

Monseigneur, since the letters I thought to send at once, this despatch has been delayed until now, because the letters
I am sending you had to be made and remade a great many times, and for that reason there was much going and coming to
Windsor, both of the Dukes themselves and of others of this Council; and only now have they sent them to me in the form you
will see from the copy of them. They beg me as hard as they can to see that no one takes it ill if in these despatches,
and especially in what touches the main point of the despatch, I am not altogether satisfied as I would wish, nor they
themselves, excusing themselves that their way of negotiating with their master is not yet well settled; but for the
future they are to do wonders, and they give such great assurances of it, and so well sworn, that I cannot keep from
believing them. I have not renewed my letters to the King, for I do not see that there is matter for it.

For the rest, I went to see the Cardinal in his troubles, where I found the greatest example of fortune one could ever
see. He set out his case to me in the worst rhetoric I ever saw, for heart and speech failed him entirely; he wept much
and begged that the King and Madame would be pleased to have pity on him, if they had found that he had kept his promise
to be a good servant to them as far as his honour and power could extend; but in the end he left me without being able to
say anything better than his face, which has indeed fallen by half its just value. And I promise you, Monseigneur, that his
fortune is such that his enemies, although they are English, could not keep from pitying him; nevertheless they will not
fail to pursue him to the end, and he sees no means of his safety, nor do I, unless it please the King and Madame to help
him. Of legation, of the seal, of authority, of credit he asks nothing; he is ready to give up everything down to his
shirt, and to be left to live in a hermitage, provided this King does not hold him in his displeasure. I comforted him as
best I could, but I could not do much. Since then, through someone he trusts, he has sent me word of what he would like to
be done for him for the greater part, and, seeing that it did not touch the good of the King's affairs, that the most
reasonable thing he asks should be granted him: namely, that the King should write to this King that there is a great
rumour over there that he has put him away from about him and far from his good grace, so that it is said he is to be
destroyed, which he does not think is altogether as it is said; nevertheless, for the good brotherhood they have together,
and the great communication of all their greatest affairs, he has wished to beg him to have regard to it, so that no bad
fancy should suddenly arise towards those who have seen that, with such great solemnity and authority, he served as
instrument in this perpetual friendship so renowned throughout Christendom; and that if by chance he had become
discontented with him, he would be pleased to moderate his feeling a little, as he is quite sure those who are about his
person and in the handling of his greatest affairs will advise him. That, Monseigneur, is the most reasonable of all his
requests, in which I do not wish to intrude by giving my opinion; but I will say that there is no one here who ought to
take such a letter ill, and especially when they consider, as indeed they do, that they are forced to take and hold your
side more than ever. And moreover I will assure you that the greatest hold they could have on him at the beginning, and
what most served them to set him at odds with this King, was that on my coming here he declared too openly that he wished
to go to Cambrai; for the others persuaded the master that this was only to avoid being present at the dispatch of the
marriage. And besides that, I promise you that without him the others were putting this King on a terrible course of
breaking off the peace negotiations, of which I wrote you a word at that time; but I left ten times more of it in my pen,
seeing that everything had been patched up; I shall tell you of it when I am there, and I am sure you will find it very
strange.

It seems to me, Monsieur, that all this, and several other things that you well understand yourself, deserve some regard.
You will, if you please, inform the King and Madame of all this, so that they may consider what they will be pleased to do
about it; if they think they will not worsen their affairs by it, I believe they will gladly — besides its being a work of
charity — wish it to be known that they have drawn back from the gates of hell one of their devoted servants, held as such
by everyone. But above all, Monseigneur, he wishes that this King should not know that they have been asked to do it, and
that he had them asked in any way in the world; that would finish driving him mad. For to tell you the truth, and setting
aside all affection, I assure you that the greatest hold his enemies have had on him, besides that of the marriage, has
been to persuade this King that he always had, in time of peace and war, a secret understanding with Madame, from whom,
during the said war, he had received great presents, which were the reason that, when Suffolk was at Montdidier, he did not
help him with money as he should have, whence it came about that he did not take Paris; but they speak of this in
whispers, so that I may not be informed of it. As to the said presents, he hopes that Madame will not harm him where it is
spoken of; for all other things he commends himself to her good grace.

The fancy of these lords is that, once he is dead or ruined, they will at once overthrow the state of the Church here and
take all their goods, which I would hardly need to put in cipher, for they shout it at a full table. I believe they will
work fine miracles. Yet your great Prophet with the bronzed face told me that this King will not live much longer than
[…], to whom, as you know, so far as I see from his writings, he gave no term beyond the muster of May. I do not want to
forget to tell you that if the King and Madame wish to do something for the Legate, they would have to hurry; even so the
letters will never be here before he has lost the Seal; however he no longer thinks of that; they will serve for the
rest. Also, when my successor comes here, as everyone expects him to within a few days, they should give him charge to
speak of it. The worst of his trouble is that Mademoiselle Boleyn has made her lover promise that he will never hear him
speak; for she well thinks that he could not keep from pitying him.

Monseigneur, whatever is good in all this discourse you will know how to take as such; if there is anything that seems
to come from too much affection, I beg you to help me excuse it and that it be taken in good part; for even where the
matter might be bad, I can still assure you that the intention is not; and thereupon it is high time, for you and for

# Jean du Bellay, Bishop of Bayonne, to the Grand Maître (Montmorency), London, last day of June 1529: English translation

Monseigneur, you will see from the King's letters part of what I could tell you of late, except to assure you that if the

without words. Nevertheless I forgive him, in my opinion, for he is somewhat timorous, and not only fears but is dying of
fear that, if the Emperor comes into Italy, the Pope will revoke his commission; whoever said I had done the work, when I
am with him, would be very much inclined to lie. I shall leave this talk; it would serve for nothing at present. I can
indeed tell you that when he does nothing of what you would wish, it will not be the fault of the Cardinal, who is wiser
than he, nor of any servant he has. Consider a little how he leads his people for you: it was by the contrivance of […]
and […] that the Duke of Suffolk had gone to you, thinking a [*] at Amiens when I arrived here […]. The Cardinal wanted to
break off their journey and go at once to Cambrai; he saw that this King took it ill, he gave way, but he made their
commission worthy of Master Bryan, then by good means had them recalled so as not to be at this Assembly; and do not think
that he would be the sorriest man in the world if everything were concluded before the arrival of those who are going
there, whatever face he may put on it, provided nothing is done to the prejudice of this King in returning to the
marriage.

I had written to you that there would be great delay, but I see that since the coming of Stephen and Bryan, bringing, as I
certainly think, [evidence] of the falsity of the brief, Campeggio is half won over to cut it short, for several reasons
too long to explain, unless the Pope should hurry to revoke the commission; and I do not think it will not be done within
a month, unless something else intervenes, and perhaps sooner; and if anything hinders Cardinal Campeggio in it, it will
in my opinion be the fear he will have — without cause, however — that the King and the Emperor may come to terms and
become friends, leaving this one a little behind, and consequently him too. I do good service in this, but without going
too far into it: I have begged on the King's behalf that they attend to it and hasten it, with many good words, saying I
had a letter, and this has already served them. On this subject, the Lord Cardinal most humbly and most affectionately
begs the King to write a word in his own hand to the said Campeggio, asking him to expedite this affair, and assuring him
that wherever he does something for its good he will hold it as done to himself and will remember it in time and place,
with other such or similar words. To tell you the truth, Monseigneur, I see for the present nothing whatever that would
serve their matter better; and its coming to an end can only be marvellously convenient for you, for besides the
friendship the King bears the King his good brother, it will be a means in future of bringing this King to his knees
before you. Perhaps the King need not write so openly: the letter remains, and there would be no need for a date, but to
refer the rest to what I shall lately have told him about it, in accordance with what I had discharged by my letters.

Your ambassadors will leave on Friday and travel by long stages: I hope it will be the return from matins. I did not
want to send you an express courier, so that, when the packet reaches you, the ten days will have expired. When you send
Doctor Benet the Lord Legate's letters, have an excuse made for the delay, that my courier broke a foot on the way and
gave the packet to another; they will believe it, for I have provided for that; otherwise they would see that I had put
in this delay on purpose. Monseigneur, I shall commend myself most humbly to your good grace, and pray our Lord to give
you a good and long life. From London, the last of June. At The Hague I am not writing to Madame, hoping that the King's
letters and yours will serve her; I would only be repeating myself to her.
