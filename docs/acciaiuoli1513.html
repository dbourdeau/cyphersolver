The Florentine ambassador at the court of Louis XII reports in cipher on Bourbon’s march to Burgundy, the king’s anger and the English embarkation; about seven words in eight are deciphered with an archivist’s key.

The letter
Roberto Acciaiuoli, Florentine orator at the French court, to the Signoria, Amiens, 23 October 1513. Both faces of one leaf are in cipher, 41 lines, with a closing and the date in clear.
Where
Archivio di Stato di Firenze, Mediceo avanti il Principato, filza LXXII, doc. 192 (old c. 234). It was catalogue entry 2351 here. The letter is not on DECODE; the key is DECODE record R5807.
The cipher
A Florentine syllabary: a consonant sign with the vowel written above it as a figure (1 to 5 for u, o, i, e, a), bare letter signs, signs for doubled consonants and common words, and numbers for names. The letter has 1,887 signs of 153 kinds.
How it was deciphered
With the key that the archivist Pietro Domenico Gabbrielli rebuilt in the nineteenth century for Acciaiuoli’s cipher of 1512–14 from other letters. Brice Dudout’s key for the Pandolfini letters first showed the system, and a cipher letter of the same day with its clear copy fixed several signs.
What it says
Louis XII has ordered Monsieur de Bourbon into Burgundy; news of a rout has upset a plan; the king, “loro soprano signore”, is angry at people who excuse themselves by fear of the English army; the English king has shipped his baggage and his army is embarking, part of it at Calais.
Still open
97 of the 757 cipher words: 25 unread, 67 proposed or carrying a bracketed letter, and 5 numbers with no value. The archive’s online images are 1272 pixels wide and do not separate some raised figures or the two forms of the sign 2; the numbers and two word signs need a key source.
Credit
Contributed by Andrew Aymeloglu (pull request 841): transcription, key table, reading and translation made with Claude (Anthropic), a first pass and a review with OpenAI Codex. The system was recognised from Brice Dudout’s key. No person has checked the reading against the manuscript.

01 The letter

Roberto Acciaiuoli was the Florentine orator at the court of Louis XII. This dispatch to the Signoria is one leaf. After a short address in clear, the cipher fills the recto (22 lines) and most of the verso (19 lines) as a continuous run, with no word division and no decipherment on the leaf. It closes in clear with his commendation and the date “Ex Amiens, die xxiii Octobris M.D.XIII”.

Two neighbours in the same filza matter for the key. Doc. 190 is Acciaiuoli’s cipher letter of the same day to Giuliano de’ Medici, and doc. 191 is a copy of that letter in clear, made at the time. Docs. 189 to 197 were checked: none is a clear copy of doc. 192, and the letter is not printed in Desjardins and Canestrini’s Négociations, which give Acciaiuoli’s letter from Amiens of 26 September 1513 and nothing from him for October.

The first words of the cipher, as transcribed, with the value the key gives each sign:

α · p t⁵ · 6² 6 0 7³ n 8⁵ · 3⁴ H · p c 8³ 5⁵ · 5³ h · 3⁴ · 9⁴ y 8³ · W O · r³ G t³ y³ 2⁵ W
per · u na · po s c ri p ta · de ll · u l ti ma · mi a · de · ve n ti · i o · si g ni fi ca i

“Per una poscripta dell’ultima mia de’ venti io significai”: by a postscript to my last, of the 20th, I told. The letters and figures stand for the shapes of the signs, named in the key file; a raised figure is the vowel.

No image of the leaf is shown. The archive allows its images on a free site without advertising, but asks to be notified of every publication and for the credit line “su concessione del Ministero della cultura”. No notice has been given, so the page is text only: the archive’s scans are linked under Sources.

02 The cipher and its keys

The values come from three outside sources and from this letter itself, and the key file marks each row by its source.

  • Gabbrielli’s key no. 120. Pietro Domenico Gabbrielli compiled the cipher alphabets of the correspondence of the Dieci di Balìa in the nineteenth century. His no. 120 is headed “Acciaioli (de) Roberto”, 1512–13–14, and gives the alphabet, the syllabary, the doubled consonants, 35 word signs (36 with a pencilled bene), the nulls and thirteen numbers (2 Papa, 4 Re di Francia, 5 Re di Spagna, 6 Inghilterra, 20 Svizzeri, 22 Lanzichinecchi and others). It is his reconstruction from letters deciphered at the time, not a chancery key sheet, and the letter of 23 October 1513 lies outside the filze he lists, so the key does not depend on this letter. It was transcribed from DECODE record R5807.
  • Brice Dudout’s key for the Pandolfini letters. It first opened this letter and showed which system it was. Almost all of its values used here are also in Gabbrielli’s key.
  • Docs. 190 and 191. The cipher letter of the same day was set sign by sign against its clear copy. That fixed several shapes of this writer’s hand and several word signs. On held-out signs whose clear word had been noted before decoding, the corrected key gives the letters of the clear copy at 84% to 94% (93 signs).

By the source column of the key file, 1,774 of the 1,887 signs take a value from Gabbrielli’s key, 50 from the pair 190 and 191, 33 from Dudout’s key alone and 21 rest on this letter alone (a plain cross for n, an E-like sign for e, ora, the plain numerals, and the number 15, which the reading leaves open); 9 have no value. Gabbrielli’s two other keys for Acciaiuoli, no. 89 (1505–12) and no. 111 (1511–12), are different ciphers.

The Pandolfini letters. Francesco Pandolfini’s letters from the French court of 1514–15 have their own page. As far as the two key files can be compared, they are in the same cipher as this letter: the syllabary, the word signs and the numbers 2, 4, 6 and 20 have the same values in both. Applied by sign name to this letter, Dudout’s key gives the same value as the key used here for 1,690 of the 1,887 signs, another value for 31 and none for 166, signs his key does not name (67 of them a tall form of a). The two transcribers’ shape names were matched by description, so the count is indicative. The pages are kept apart because the basis differs: there the key was rebuilt from pairs of cipher and decipherment and tested on held-out pairs; here a letter of another writer and an earlier year takes its values from Gabbrielli’s key for Acciaiuoli. Giovanni Corsi’s cipher letters from Spain of the same weeks are in another key.

03 What the letter says

In the quotations, letters in square brackets are supplied or corrected, [?word] is a word proposed for signs that do not spell it, [...] is an unread word and a name in braces is a number resolved by Gabbrielli’s key. The whole Italian text and the English translation are in the Full texts section.

Acciaiuoli recalls his postscript of the 20th: the king “haveva ordinato di mandare in [B]orgogna monsignor di [B]or[b]ona con 8 cento lance et 10 mila {Lanzichinecchi}”. A plan had been in the air that involved a passage of the mountains, the French galleys and 12 thousand Landsknechte. Then a letter from Florence of the 11th arrived with a copy of a letter of Martelli “significativa della ropta di [15]”, and the plan “in tucto è necessario si ponga da parte”.

He has just spoken at length with the king, who is torn between fear of the Swiss, should they come into Burgundy, and anger at two parties written as numbers. The king’s words:

e’ si excusano col dire [?d’essere] suti forzati per la paura dello ex[e]rcito anglese; et sendo io loro soprano signore, non hanno hauto rigvardo di mettermi el reame in pericolo, né per loro si è tenuto di farmelo perdere.

They excuse themselves by saying they were forced by fear of the English army; and I being their sovereign lord, they had no regard for putting my kingdom in danger, and it was no thanks to them that I did not lose it. They had sent a man to excuse themselves, and were told that nobody could know the king’s mind better than they, who knew whether they had wronged him. Acciaiuoli adds that the truce with Spain and the winter may cool the anger.

The troops assigned to the garrisons will not move until the English king has left. News has come that he has put his bed and baggage aboard and lacks only his own person, and that [an unread word] went to Calais and [?embarked] there with part of the troops, the crossing divided in three parts. If the Swiss attack they will meet 2 thousand 5 hundred lances and 14 thousand Landsknechte, to be placed in Champagne and Burgundy, and the king tells him to write that he is secure and “se li {Svizzeri} ci movono per assaltarsi, noi ci [b]atterèno al ogni modo”. The prelates will leave for Rome some days after All Saints, and the king is disposed to please the pope in everything.

The rout. Sanudo’s Diarii, vol. XVII, cols. 321–324, have a separate report of Bourbon assigned to Burgundy with 900 men-at-arms and 10,000 infantry, and print a Florentine letter of 28 August 1513 citing reports from “nostro Piero Martelli” at Vicenza, where the Venetians were routed on 7 October. That is the ground for taking the number 15 as the Venetians. It is a proposal, and the reading prints the number.

04 How it was checked

The transcription was made by a model with the key in hand, so it is not blind. Two further model instances then transcribed all 41 lines from shape names alone, with no key: decoded with the same key, their signs give the printed letters at 85% of the signs counted as deciphered. A second pair went over the thirteen hardest lines from tighter crops, and where both agreed against the first transcription their sign was usually adopted. A second model (OpenAI Codex) reviewed the reading against the images; its confirmed corrections are applied. The first transcription was not hashed before decoding.

The count is strict. The decryption file is the unedited output of the key on the transcription. The reading departs from it at 20 declared places, each marked in brackets and counted as a hole: the sign 2 taken as b at 14 places, the triangle taken as the word sign bene twice, and four single signs. Of the 757 cipher words, 660 are the key’s output with nothing supplied or changed (87.2%), under the 95% needed to call the text deciphered in full.

The sign 2. Gabbrielli’s key has a plain 2 for b and a z-like 2 for c, and both forms are on the page. Four readers classed the form at each of the 67 places from crops with no reading and no key. On the criterion written second, both of its readers saw the squat form at 12 of the 14 places taken as b, and at 4 of the 53 taken as c. The bar set before that survey allowed 3, so no sign was renamed and the 14 words stay holes.

An AI reviewer for the maintainers went through the Italian as sentences against the English on 21 Nov 2026 and graded it B: coherent but raw, with proposed words, marked repairs and unresolved groups.

05 What remains uncertain

  • 25 unread words, the longest stretches at R5, V1, V5, V13 and V14–15. A raised 1 cannot be told from a raised 5 at 1272 pixels, the largest size the archive serves.
  • 67 words proposed or with a bracketed letter: words marked [?word], among them costà, successo, posta, sono, mesi and Signore, where the signs as transcribed are mostly one raised figure or one similar shape away; the 20 declared departures; and words with one letter supplied, such as passaggi[o], ing[i]uria and Ciampa[g]na.
  • The number 15, twice: the party whose rout Martelli reported. It is not in Gabbrielli’s key or in Dudout’s. The Venetians are proposed from Sanudo. In doc. 190 a 15 stands where the clear copy has Rubertet, written over a struck word that begins with what looks like a v and cannot be made out on the online image; elsewhere in that letter 51 stands where the clear copy has Rubertet.
  • The numbers 26 and 47 at R12, the two parties the king is angry at. Neither is in Gabbrielli’s key. Dudout’s key, from the Pandolfini pairs of 1514–15, has 26 as Arciduca. That fits the king’s words: the king of France was overlord of the Archduke Charles for Flanders and Artois, from where the English army had campaigned. (This background is not from the sources and was not checked here.) It is a proposal and is not counted. 47 is in neither key.
  • A doubtful 20 at R5, proposed for two signs that the key gives as letters.
  • Two word signs. A B with a raised 1 (V15, V16) is in no key. An F with a small raised mark stands at R13 and V5. In doc. 190 the same-looking sign faces essere in the clear copy, and essere gives sense at both places; Gabbrielli’s key gives effetto for an F with a raised 0, and the mark cannot be told apart on the online image. Both places stay holes.
  • Two signs after nientedimeno at R19 are struck through, and the last sign of the cipher, after desidera, is not deciphered.
  • Gabbrielli’s key is a reconstruction, and the transcription is a model’s. No person has checked either against the manuscript.

The unread and doubtful words wait on better images than the archive serves online; the numbers and the two word signs need a key source. The neighbouring leaves, the three keys of Gabbrielli for Acciaiuoli, Dudout’s key and its two folders, and the printed collections named under Sources were tried on 20, 21 and 24 Nov 2026.

reading-it.md

# Roberto Acciaiuoli, Amiens, 23 ottobre 1513 (MAP LXXII, doc. 192)

The cipher of both faces as continuous Italian. The unedited output of the key is `192-literal.txt`; this file is the reading made from it. Spelling is the cipher's; word division, capitals and punctuation are editorial. Everything the key does not give for the signs as transcribed is in brackets, and a word with a bracket in it is counted as a hole:

- `[...]` is an unread word and `[... 2]` two (sizes are estimates from the number of signs). `[?word]` is a word I propose for signs that do not spell it.
- Letters in brackets inside a word are supplied or corrected. Among them are the 20 places of `192-repairs.tsv`: `[b]` where the sign 2, c in the key, is read with its second value b (14 places); `[bene]` where the triangle, m in the key, is read as the word sign (twice); `[z]` in *[f]ran[z]ese*, `[e]` in *vostr[e]*, `[a]` in *trov[a]ndosi* and `[o]` in *f[o]rza*.
- `[+]` after the last word is the final sign of V19, a plain cross (n in the key), which I do not read.
- `{Name}` is a code number resolved by Gabbrielli's key no. 120: 2 Papa, 4 Cristianissimo, 6 Inghilterra, 20 Svizzeri, 22 Lanzichinecchi. `[15]`, `[?26]` and `[?47]` are numbers with no value. Plain figures are numerals.

After the cipher the letter closes in clear, with Acciaiuoli's commendation and the date *Ex Amiens, die xxiii Octobris M.D.XIII*; that is not part of the reading below.

## Reading

Quando fussi [?successo] felicemente, non saria suto ancora maraviglia per [...] [?potevono] et passati et [?avanti] che l'impeto de' [?20] si fussi messo insieme con le spalle de' [15], et che la f[o]rza delle galee [f]ran[z]ese et di 12 mila {Lanzichinecchi} si poteva pensare [i]n ogni gran factione.

Hora, sendo conparsa la vostra delli 11 con la copia della lettera di [...] Martelli significativa della ropta di [15], a rivolto sottosopra questo disegno et pensiero, et in tucto è necessario si ponga da parte. Né so quello sien per [?deliberare] di [?nuovo].

Per le parole del {Cristianissimo}, col quale ora a lungo parlai, si vede li ha dato questa cosa molestia et ropto qualche disegno; et di quanto sia per fare di presente non pare [bene] resoluto, perché da un canto lo preme la paura de' {Svizzeri} quando venissino in [B]orgogna, dall'altro lo sdegno contro a [?26] et [?47], de' quali [?mi parlò] ora molto iratamente, dicendomi: e' si excusano col dire [?d'essere] suti forzati per la paura dello ex[e]rcito anglese; et sendo io loro soprano signore, non hanno hauto rigvardo di mettermi el reame in pericolo, né per loro si è tenuto di farmelo perdere.

Nientedimeno el respecto della triegva di Spagna et la stagione del verno potria ramorzare lo sdegno et voltare li animi a riposarsi questo verno; et potria questo romore et demonstratione ancora farsi per assicurarsi in futuro che qual[che] principe non dessi loro più ricept[o] alli nimici loro.

Le gente disegnate per andare in gvernigione non sono ancora andate, né son per moversi insino che non ci è la partita di {Inghilterra}, la quale non può tardare a venire. # Roberto Acciaiuoli, Amiens, 23 October 1513 (MAP LXXII, doc. 192)

The holes are those of the Italian reading: `[...]` an unread word, `[?word]` a word proposed for signs that do not spell it, `[15]`, `[?26]`, `[?47]` numbers with no value. Words in which the Italian brackets a single letter or a second key value (*[B]orgogna*, *[bene]*) are translated without the mark; they are holes in the count all the same.

In a postscript to my last, of the 20th, I told Your Lordships that the Most Christian King, in assigning the garrisons, had ordered Monsieur de Bourbon sent into Burgundy with 8 hundred lances and 10 thousand Landsknechte; and that if the Swiss did not stir from home, it would have been no wonder if, with the expense unexpectedly on his back and the fortresses at risk, a passage were made [?to your side] of the mountains. Had it [?succeeded] happily, it would again have been no wonder [...] [?they could] and, once across and [?before], that the drive of [?20] were joined to the backing of [15], and that with the strength of the French galleys and of 12 thousand Landsknechte any great enterprise could be thought of.

Now your letter of the 11th has arrived with the copy of the letter of [...] Martelli reporting the rout of [15]. It has turned this plan and thought upside down, and it must be set aside entirely. I do not know what they will [?decide] [?next].

From the words of the Most Christian King, with whom I have just spoken at length, one sees that this has vexed him and broken some plan. What he will do now he does not seem to have fully settled, because on one side fear of the Swiss presses him, should they come into Burgundy, and on the other his anger against [?26] and [?47], of whom [?he spoke to me] just now very angrily, saying: "They excuse themselves by saying [?they were] forced by fear of the English army; and I being their sovereign lord, they had no regard for putting my kingdom in danger, and it was no thanks to them that I did not lose it."

He told me they had sent a man [?expressly] to Monsignore to [...] excuse themselves and to draw out something of the king's mind; and that Monsignore had answered them that no one could know the mind of the Most Christian King better than they themselves, who knew whether they had wronged him or not; that they should examine their consciences, and if they found they had not [?offended] him, they could hope for good; but if they found they had wronged him, they should expect punishment.

Nevertheless regard for the truce with Spain and the winter season could damp the anger and turn minds to resting this winter; and this noise and show may also be made to ensure that in future no prince gives shelter to their enemies.

The troops assigned to go into garrison have not gone yet, and will not move until England has left, which cannot be long in coming. [...] news came to the Most Christian King how that Majesty was at [...], and had already put on board [...] bed and all [...] baggage, and nothing [?was wanting] but his own person; so [...] had [?gone] to Calais and there [?embarked] with one part of the troops, and another part had embarked at [...] another port of [?Flanders], so that they have divided their crossing in three parts for greater convenience; and [... 2] the king's affairs secure and His Majesty's forces in such [?a state] that he has nothing to fear.

And if the Swiss attacked on [?this side] at this season, [?they would find] an opposition of 2 thousand 5 hundred lances and 14 thousand Landsknechte, an army that is not about to shrink or be dismissed; and all will be placed within a few days in Champagne and Burgundy, and the rest of the men-at-arms will be put on these frontiers. And just now the Most Christian King said to me: "Write [...] freely that I [?am] secure and without suspicion; and if the Swiss move to attack, we will fight in any case."

On the spiritual question nothing [?can] be said except that what was ordered is being done [... 2] sending the prelates to Rome, and I do not think many days after All Saints will pass before they leave here. And the Most Christian King [?could] not [?be] better disposed than he [?is] [?to] please the Pope in everything; and His Majesty and those of the council and all the [... 4] are resolved on the same and minded to refuse him nothing.

And with this [... 2] for [... 2] in [...] good repose and of [...] so much disturbance, the storm and rain that showed itself four [?months] ago not having followed, [?they have] gained [...] time, and matters brought to a point where they will not change this winter, God seems to be showing our [?Lord] the way to reach [?his] desired end of quieting Christendom. May God grant him the grace for it; I believe that on this side he will find the readiness he wants.
