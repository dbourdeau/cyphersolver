IRISH WORLD
77 = A · 85 = I · 60 = J · 76 = Z

London → an unnamed “Dear Friend” · shifted alphabet in two-digit numbers, English · 24 June 1885

Fenian letter No. II, London, 24 June 1885

Go to the full text ↓

An unsigned report of a meeting abroad, in English, hides its names and key words in pairs of numbers; one shifted alphabet gives them, but the man interviewed is still only “DEC”.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 27 November 2026

Method: ciphertext-only break · Extent: partial (56% of cipher tokens valued, not the word count)

Contributed by Malcolm McBratney (new cipher with solution)

At a glanceCiphertext-only break · partial (56% of cipher tokens valued, not the word count)

The letter
“Letter No. II”, London, 24 June 1885: one folded sheet, four sides, in English. Writer and recipient are not named. The writer reports that his “mission has been a most complete success”.
Where
The Catholic University of America, American Catholic History Research Center, Fenian Brotherhood Collection, “Letter, no recipient and author given, June 24, 1885”; on JSTOR as community.38709311. Not on DECODE.
The cipher
Thirteen groups of two-digit numbers, 71 pairs, in the clear text. Each pair is a letter of one shifted alphabet: A = 77 to I = 85, then J = 60 to Z = 76. The writer often writes only the first letters and the last letter of a word.
How the key was found
From the ciphertext: the group after “we had the whole” starts with a repeat, x-y-x, which fits IRI-; IRISH WORLD then fits all ten pairs under one shift, and that one parameter fixes all 26 values. WOLFE TONE, CANADA and the other groups were not used to build it.
What it says
With “the Infant”, the writer met a man written DEC, put the advantages to Russia of an understanding, said the whole Irish World was with them, and was asked whether a diversion in Canada was feasible.
Still open
DEC (four times) is not identified; R—s is not settled; the letter breaks off at the foot of side 4.
Credit
Found, transcribed and worked by Malcolm McBratney with AI agents (Claude), pull request #997. The checks were done with AI tools and no person checked each record by hand.

01 The letter

The item is catalogued as “Letter, no recipient and author given, June 24, 1885” in the Fenian Brotherhood Collection of the Catholic University of America, now a JSTOR Community collection. It has two images, each an opened side of one folded sheet; the four pages run, by sense, from the right half of image 1 to the left half of image 2, the right half of image 2 and the left half of image 1. JSTOR shows the rights statement “No Copyright – United States”. JSTOR’s terms for reproducing Community images were not checked, so no crop is shown here; the images can be seen on JSTOR.

The writer is back in London. With “the Infant” he went to a rendezvous, then to “a certain place”, where they had a long interview with DEC, “a polished gentleman sitting in a magnificently furnished Apartment”. The writer presented himself as the English representative of “the firm”. A note upside down in the top margin mentions “Madame H—” and a letter from “O’N.”.

02 The cipher

Thirteen groups (G01–G13) of two-digit numbers stand in the clear English for names and key words: 71 pairs, 19 different. All are in 60–85 except one doubtful pair. The letters run in one shifted alphabet: letter n of the alphabet (A = 1) is written 60 + (n + 16) mod 26.

A 77 · B 78 · C 79 · D 80 · E 81 · F 82 · G 83 · H 84 · I 85 · J 60 · K 61 · L 62 · M 63 · N 64 · O 65 · P 66 · Q 67 · R 68 · S 69 · T 70 · U 71 · V 72 · W 73 · X 74 · Y 75 · Z 76

The writer runs the digits together, and often writes only the first letters and the last letter of a word, with a dash: RUS-A, NAT-E, L-D, PAT—S, R—S, OR—. Word division follows his spacing and punctuation.

03 The decipherment

Cipher words in ⟦ ⟧, letters supplied by us in [ ], from READING.txt:

  • He set forth “the mutual advantages likely to accrue to ⟦Rus[si]a⟧, and ourselves in the Event of a common understanding” (G01).
  • “a long and most interesting interview with ⟦DEC⟧” (G02; DEC again in G03–G05).
  • “for the moment I was ⟦Wolfe Tone⟧ before the Directory” (G06).
  • “told him we were not alone a ⟦secret⟧, ⟦or[ganisation]⟧ —— but of which the members referred to were only a tithe, but that we had the whole ⟦Irish⟧ ⟦World⟧ with us” (G07, G08). The r of secret is a plain letter written above the line.
  • “we were not ⟦R[…]s⟧ in the strict sense of the word but ⟦Pat[riot]s⟧ struggling for the ⟦Aut[ono]my⟧ of ⟦our⟧ ⟦nat[iv]e⟧ ⟦l[an]d⟧” (G09–G12). The writer wrote AUTNONMY.
  • “do you think a diversion in ⟦C[a]nada⟧ feasible” (G13). The sheet ends here, in the middle of the quotation.

The letter is in English, so no translation is needed. Who wrote it, to whom, and who DEC is are not settled. Our own reading of the context (1885, the Afghan crisis between Britain and Russia, Irish-American approaches to Russia) is inference and was not checked in the literature.

04 Method and tests

The transcription was made from screen zooms of the JSTOR viewer and frozen on 25 November 2026 at 23:31:52 UTC. The transcriber saw the x-y-x pattern of G08 and formed the key idea before the freeze, and looked again at two doubtful pairs with it in mind; this is disclosed in freeze/DISCLOSURE.txt. The key was frozen at 23:32:04 UTC and the 13 groups were decoded once.

  • Pairs giving the letters of the reading: 67 of 71 (94.4%), the four others being the doubtful pair of G13 and the three out of order in G11.
  • Groups with an identified meaning: 8 of 13 (61.5%), under the project’s own 65% line, because one unidentified person recurs four times and R—s is open.
  • Controls on five test words fixed in advance (IRISH, WORLD, WOLFE, TONE, OUR): the real key 5 of 5; the other 25 shifts 0; 5,000 random keys and 5,000 shuffles of the ciphertext at seeds 20261112 and 7, none reaching the real score (maximum 2). WOLFE, TONE and OUR are independent of the crib. No held-out test was possible: the whole letter was seen before the freeze.
  • The repository’s statistical test (docs/_validate.py) gives “too short” (64 letters, under 80): it neither confirms nor refutes the key.
  • A blind second reader (an AI agent that saw only the image crops) agrees on 70 of 71 pairs (98.59%). It differs on the doubtful pair of G13 and reads one more pair, 64 (N), after the dash in G07, which would make OR—N.

05 What remains open

The count is of the 18 cipher words only. A word counts as deciphered when the key gives every letter from a clean pair with nothing supplied, or when it is the writer’s dash abbreviation and the written letters with the clear context allow only one word (Rus-a, Pat—s, nat-e, l-d). That gives 10 of 18 (0.556); the strictest count, with no abbreviation, gives 6 of 18. The extent is partial. The eight holes:

  • DEC, four times (G02–G05): the letters are given, but the man, and whether DEC is a code name, initials or an abbreviation, are not identified.
  • OR— (G07): the expansion organisation (or order) is ours.
  • R—s (G09): only R and S are written. Malcolm McBratney reads it as “revolutionaries” from the sense; revolutionists, rebels or republicans are also possible.
  • Aut[ono]my (G11): written 77 71 70 64 65 64 63 75, AUT-N-O-N-MY; the blind reader also sees 64 65 64, so it is likely the writer’s slip.
  • C[a]nada (G13): the second pair is transcribed 7?7 by the first reader and 4?7 by the blind reader; the digit bottoms are clipped at the page edge in the viewer.

Also open: a struck word on side 3 line 20, which cannot be read; Letter No. I and the continuation of this letter, not found in the collection; and the books that may print the letter (Devoy’s Post Bag, Short’s The Dynamite War, Whelehan’s The Dynamiters, Kenna’s War in the Shadows, Jenkins on F. F. Millen), which could not be reached. Two other letters to Rossa in the same collection, from F. F. Millen (23 February 1885) and Richard Bennett (16 December 1885), were looked through on 27 November 2026: both are in clear English, with no number groups, and neither mentions DEC. No person has yet compared the transcription with the image or with the JSTOR PDF; the reading review is left to the maintainer.
