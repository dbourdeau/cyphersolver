An unaddressed Spanish cipher of 8 January 1497 (Espagnol 318 no. 95)

A leaf of dense signs with no clear word, about the French and what a Señoría can be made to do; George Lasry’s alphabet, applied here to machine-clustered glyphs, deciphers about two thirds of the recto.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 15 October 2026

Method: read with known key · Extent: partial

At a glanceKnown key applied · partial, about two thirds of f. 122r

The letter
An unaddressed cipher letter dated 8 January 1497, three days before Federico of Naples’s letter in the same volume. It is wholly in cipher, with no word breaks.
Where
Bibliothèque nationale de France, Espagnol 318 no. 95, f. 122r–v (Gallica, canvases 458–459). It is one of five ciphered letters in the volume, catalogue item 9.
The cipher
A homophonic alphabet of invented signs, one to three per letter, with code groups written in clear letters (bac, bnh, bon …).
How it was deciphered
George Lasry reconstructed the alphabet in 2022, and Tomokiyo published it as an image without a plaintext. Here the 963 glyphs of the recto were segmented and clustered by shape. The clusters were labelled from Lasry’s key and checked by eye.
What it says
Spanish, about the French and about what a Señoría (Venice?) can or cannot be brought to do.
Still open
About a third of the recto’s signs, where two signs touch. The five lines of the verso have not been decoded yet. This entry is in progress.
Credit
The key is George Lasry’s. Cryptiana says he solved the letter in 2022; his plaintext has not been published.
Other letters
The other ciphered letters of the volume use other ciphers and have their own pages: no. 92 (the Cifra general), no. 5 (Escrivà’s cipher) and nos. 93–94 (key not identified).

01 The leaf and the key

F. 122 is a single leaf of 31 dense lines, no address, no salutation, no clear word anywhere, dated 8 January 1497, three days before Federico’s letter at the other end of the volume. In May 2022 George Lasry sent Tomokiyo an “approximate” solution, and Tomokiyo published it: an image of the alphabet, one to three signs per letter, with a row of unassigned signs and a handful of code groups — bac bnh bon dah daf dae oht bed dal boo del. Cryptiana says the letter “is solved”, but no plaintext has ever been printed. Until 14 October 2026 this letter shared a page with the volume’s four other ciphered letters, which are in other ciphers.

Matching the symbols by eye against that image does not work: ÷ against ≐, the plain 7 against the 7 with a small o and the crossed 7, are distinctions a printed reconstruction makes crisply and a hurried 1497 hand does not. So the leaf was taken apart mechanically instead. Adaptive-threshold binarisation; connected components grouped into glyphs by horizontal overlap with diacritics attached, which gives 963 glyphs over 31 lines, a median of 31 to the line; Ward clustering of 28×28 normalised bitmaps; then the clusters, rather than the individual symbols, were labelled by eye from contact sheets against Lasry’s key.

The check that the labelling is right came before any decipherment, and it is the frequency profile. The clean clusters carry about two thirds of the 963 glyphs, so their shares of the whole leaf are all deflated by roughly a third; what matters is the order. Counted against every glyph on the page, e takes 9 %, a 7 %, o 5 % and n 5 %, then s, c, u, r, d, m, t and i, with q last at about 1 %. That is the Spanish ranking, e–a–o–n–s, obtained from a key reconstructed by someone else and a clustering that used no language information.

02 What the recto says

What comes out, checked line by line on 31 annotated strips, is Spanish about the French. The verified stretches: …emos franceses… (l. 4); bac que puedan merc[ed] … de Francia… (6); …dos cosas … a traer… (7); …con esto… (8); señoría … que esta … por (11); que me toca … mucho … sera … que bnh … haga (12); con esto … acorde … lo bueno… (13); …quedar … que … Francia… (15); …si a tratado … que gouernad … pero (16); …muy grandes cosas … no creo que… (17); …certaran … pues que se … esto … la parte aun (18); que se … encubren … que … quieren go- | -uernar (19–20, across the line break); por obra creo que … amigo (22); escreuir a… (23); dificultad … en los… (25). The nomenclator groups appear in clear letters in the middle of the sign runs: bac, bon twice, bnh four times, dal, and probably bed. bnh is the frequent one and is most likely a person or a place.

Two of Lasry’s assignments were corrected on the way: the sign shaped like an n with a cross-tail is m (Lasry: y); and his two 7-forms are confirmed as distinct: the plain 7 is d (it makes puedan) and the 7 with a small o is s (it makes franceses and escreuir). They can be told apart by cluster though not by eye, which is why the first transcription by eye had them reversed.

The line-by-line decipherment is in the repository as f122r_reading.md, with … where the leaf is not deciphered.

03 What is not yet deciphered

About a third of the symbols on the recto are still unresolved. Three independent methods were tried: transcription by eye on the strips, the cluster-and-language-model hybrid, and a segmentation-free decoder that slides every template along the line and tiles it by dynamic programming. They agree on the same two thirds and fail on the same third, which puts the limit in the symbol shapes at Gallica’s 4,077-pixel scan rather than in the method. The five lines of the verso have been segmented but not yet decoded with the recto’s templates; that, and a line-by-line correction of the recto against the annotated strips, is the work still to do. It is transcription work, not cryptanalysis.

04 Method, including what failed

The pipeline turns a page of signs into a sequence of letters: seg.py (binarisation), glyphs.py (component grouping), cluster.py and montage.py (Ward clustering and contact sheets), assign95.py (the cluster-to-letter table), lm.py (a Spanish 5-gram model built on Don Quijote, normalised to the twenty-two letters of the period’s alphabet), and hybrid95.py (a decoder that searches for the best-scoring Spanish with the verified clusters held fixed). It lives in targets/esp318/, shared with the volume’s other entries. Two attempts on the unresolved third are recorded because they did not work.

  • Clipping each glyph to its line band before segmenting, to stop descenders from the line above contaminating the boxes. It made things worse: the band cuts the tall signs in half, and nearest-neighbour matching of the mutilated shapes collapses onto d and n. The junk boxes are touching signs, not descenders.
  • A segmentation-free decoder: slide every labelled template along the line by FFT correlation, tile the line left to right by dynamic programming with area-proportional scores and a penalty per column of skipped ink, then decode the tiles with the language model. With the tile count right it recovers the same lines as the transcription by eye and no more; 23 % of tiles have their top two letters within 0.05 of normalised correlation, and the language model, given that freedom, writes Don Quijote over them (don quiiote que el que).

Scipy’s signal module is blocked by an application-control policy on the machine this ran on, so the correlations are done with numpy.fft directly.
