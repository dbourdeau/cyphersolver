The Catokwacopa advertisements of 1875

Go to the transcriptions ↓

Two advertisements signed “W.” split an Oxford memoir of the 1850s across two days; the letters force about half the lines, and the rest fit many unrelated readings, so the text as a whole is not read.

At a glanceNot solved · text not obtained

The document
Two personal advertisements signed W., The Standard, p. 1 col. 2, 8 and 20 May 1875. The second ends in clear: it is “intelligible if read in connection with” the first.
Where
British Newspaper Archive (paywalled). Text as checked against the originals by Thomas Ernst (2018); number 8 on Klaus Schmeh’s Top 50.
The cipher
No substitution. Line i of the first ad and line i of the second are the two order-preserving halves of one phrase, from which W. also dropped letters.
How it was tried
Every published reading costed exactly; open-vocabulary searches in English (30,000 words) and Latin (19,000); a phrase-level 5-gram search; name-frame tests; the Hertford Scholars list of the 1850s.
What it says
An 1850s Oxford undergraduate recalls summer term 1853, Conington’s and Jowett’s lectures, a cap taken at a college party, the Hertford scholarship examination, and closes with a DYING DECLARATION.
Still open
Lines 9/26, 12, 23, 29 and the tails of 5, 6, 14, 19, 21; the figures of lines 13 and 20; the identity of W.
Credit
Readings by Thomas Bosbach, Lance Estes, “Dave”, Thomas Ernst and Jozef Krajčovič on klausschmeh.net.

No scan can be shown here. The originals are only on the paywalled archive, and the images on klausschmeh.net are re-typeset from the old, uncorrected text.

02 The mechanism

Both ads have the same token skeleton, with numerals and dashes in the same slots. Over the 24 letter lines, the summed length difference between paired halves is 33; no random re-pairing in 100,000 comes as close. So W. split each phrase roughly in half, and the pairing is real.

The halves differ in kind. Consonant-initial words begin in the 8 May half almost always (summer, term, cap, college, conington, dying, declaration); vowel-initial words mostly begin in the 20 May half (away, at, attended, us, in). The first ad carries word onsets; the second is rich in vowels and endings. This positional rule ranks rival readings.

03 What the letters force

For each reading, the cost is the number of letters W. must have omitted and the number of cipher letters that must be misprints. A reading with misprints is an emendation, not a reading of these letters.

The name lines carry real weight. In each frame (I ATTENDED ___ LECSURS; TOLD ___; ___ SCHOLARSHIP EXAMINATION) all 1,645 proper names of a 30,000-word corpus were tried, and exactly one fits each: Conington, Jowett, Shirley, Hertford. The Oxford reading is right in outline.

04 The Hertford Scholars, 1852–56

The ad’s numerals 53, 54 and 55 sit next to SUMMER TERM, the Hertford examination and HOLIDAYS. The Historical Register of the University of Oxford (1888, p. 120) lists one Hertford (Latin) Scholar a year, elected in Michaelmas Term from undergraduates within two years of matriculation:

  • 1852 James Beaumont Winstanley, University
  • 1853 William Lambert Newman, Balliol
  • 1854 Frederick Butler M. Montgomerie, Balliol
  • 1855 Charles S. C. Bowen, Balliol
  • 1856 Reginald Broughton, Balliol

The 1854 Calendar (p. 99) shows the election was then held in Lent Term and names no examiners. This fits a Balliol freshman of 1853 who sat the Lent 1854 examination, which Montgomerie won. Line 10 does not say W. won, and the list makes it unlikely. Krajčovič’s candidate Jex-Blake never held the scholarship; Ernst’s unnamed commoner of 1856 would have matriculated too late. The list narrows the field but does not name W.

Foster’s Alumni Oxonienses lists every Balliol matriculant of 1852–54. Among those with W surnames, Main Swete Alexander Walrond fits the timeline best: he matriculated in March 1853, so summer term 1853 was his first full term, he could sit the 1854 Hertford in Lent Term, and from 1873 he was vicar of St Lawrence Jewry in the City of London. But he lived until March 1899, so if the dying declaration is W.’s own, Walrond is not W. Added as cribs, his name and the other Balliol surnames do not decide any open line.

05 Why the rest does not read

With three to twelve letters freely inserted per line, short lines admit many unrelated exact readings, scoring within a nat or two of each other. Three searches were run on every open line: an exact-fit search over 30,000 English words, the same over 19,000 Latin words, and a letter-by-letter phrase search under a modern English 5-gram. A fourth run (24 Oct 2026) added about 90 Oxford words of the 1850s: the Hertford scholars, Balliol tutors, terms, examinations and set texts. Each recovers the known lines as controls and gives nonsense on the open ones:

  • lines 9 and 26 (mistrl / otenpu, mistrl / oatvpu): omit stern pull, moist relative put; MASTER PUPIL needs 4 misprints, MOST PRIVATE 5
  • line 12 (caselcluchozamot / ndtsfindseseo): no sense
  • line 23 (48 letters): with doubt portion from gift first mending …
  • line 29 (ereflodbr / rileohmae), the end of the dying declaration: retire followed both mare in English, junk in Latin
  • tails of lines 5, 6, 14, 19, 21: several exact fits, none forced (line 19 begins BALLIOL MAN; line 21 HOLIDAYS EXAMINE is third)
  • lines 13 (1.6.9 / cotegr) and 20 (A.P. 138): no reading of the figures

Fifteen of 28 lines carry a forced phrase; thirteen are open. The forced phrases account for 257 of 470 letters, but they are telegraphic fragments, not sentences, so the note is not read.

06 What would finish it

  • W.’s identity. A contemporary identification of the advertiser, or 1875 records for the other Balliol men of 1853. A memoir or college record could confirm the £138, the cap, the motto and the dying declaration, and give cribs for lines 9, 26 and 29.
  • A quoted source. If line 29 or 23 is a stock formula, the source decides it at once.
  • The originals. A British Newspaper Archive subscriber could rule out misprints on the open lines.

The Standard, 8 and 20 May 1875 (both halves) decoded, not read

Two personal advertisements signed W. in The Standard, whose lines pair up as two order-preserving halves of one plaintext with letters dropped. The forced lines give the Oxford reading in outline: SUMMER TERM 1853, a cap taken away at a college party, Conington's and Jowett's lectures, the Hertford scholarship examination, a second motto (QUI FIT, Horace), TOLD SHIRLEY, DYING DECLARATION.

A mechanical decode with holes in most sentences: shown so that the transcription and the key can be checked and improved, not as a reading.
