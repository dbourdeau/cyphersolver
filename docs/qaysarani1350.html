Ibn al-Qabāqibī’s cipher verses, about 1330–1370

Go to the full text ↓

A cipher in a Mamluk secretary’s book of cipher verses whose solution was lost with the end of the manuscript: two Arabic love verses, deciphered from a five-letter gloss, repeated words and the metre.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 16 November 2026 · updated 24 November 2026 · corrected 23 November 2026 after Alan Elbaum’s review

Method: key from adjacent plaintext · Extent: complete

Credits · Alan Elbaum (verification)

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · complete

The document
Seven lines of invented signs that Ibn al-Qabāqibī wrote to the unnamed compiler of a book of cipher verses. This project’s survey places the text of the book at about 1330–1370, an estimate from an anecdote dated 730-odd AH and the people named. Penn dates the manuscript “14--” and gives no date for the text.
Where
University of Pennsylvania, Kislak Center, LJS 51, f. 106v, the last page of text. Not on DECODE. It was no. 2882 of this project’s catalogue of open ciphers.
The cipher
One sign for each Arabic letter, 22 signs in all, written right to left with a dotted stroke between words. Lam-alif has a sign of its own.
How it was deciphered
Red letters over the first five signs give four of the six letters of the first word (the second red letter is a ت, where the word needs ن). The word for “eyelids” comes three times, and the metre and the rhyme fixed the rest.
What it says
Two verses: love has made the poet’s heart a drowsy-eyed girl’s dwelling; he lies awake with sound eyelids while she sleeps with “sick” ones.
Still open
One word of 21 is not read: the first word of the second half-verse, in a damaged patch of line 2 (first read lahā, which the metre allows but the page does not show). Three more signs are damaged and take their value from their words. A model’s review for the second maintainer (issue #714), not a person’s, graded the reading B before the corrections. The compiler’s own solution is lost. The second verse is quoted by Ibn al-ʿAdīm under an earlier poet; al-Qalqashandī and Arabic Origins of Cryptology were not searched.
Corrections
On 23 November 2026 Alan Elbaum (Princeton Geniza Project) corrected this page in eight places: the sender’s name (it was given as Ibn al-Qaysarani), the compiler’s remark, the red letters, one word of the second verse and four points of transcription. All were checked on the master image and are adopted below (section 04).

01 The page

LJS 51 is a first-person account by a Mamluk secretary of the cipher verses he exchanged with colleagues. A message is introduced by “so-and-so wrote to me”, the cipher follows in an alphabet invented for the occasion, and then the compiler writes that he worked it out and gives the verses. The manuscript has lost its beginning and its end. On the last page, f. 106v, a message from Ibn al-Qabāqibī (ابن القباقبي) stands in seven lines of signs. Under it the compiler writes فحللتها في الحال بحضوره وجدته جعل اللام الف, “I deciphered it immediately in his presence, and I found that he rendered the lām-alif [as a single letter]” (Alan Elbaum’s reading and translation; the bracketed words are his supplement), and there the manuscript breaks off. The leaf with his solution is gone. The name and the remark are as Elbaum read them on 23 November 2026. This page first gave the sender as Ibn al-Qaysarani, from the project’s survey of the volume, and a separate attempt on the same leaf read Ibn al-Qubabi (or al-Qabbani); both were wrong, and the address of this page keeps the first name only because addresses are not changed.

The transcription has 89 signs of 22 kinds in 21 words. A word runs over the end of a line twice. Two signs of line 1 are rubbed, the last sign of line 6 is broken, and three signs of line 2 stand in a damaged patch where at least two more are lost or illegible. A small plain alif is written above the line before the lam-alif sign of line 1.

02 How the key was recovered

  1. The gloss. The red letters over the first five signs were first taken here as و ن ا ع س. With the sixth sign as ة the first word is وناعسة, “and many a drowsy-eyed woman”. The red letters are in fact و ت ا ع س (Elbaum): the second is a ت. The word needs ن there, and ن fits the sign in its five other places, so whoever added the red letters was, in Elbaum’s words, still trying to work it out.
  2. A repeated word. The same four signs, ending in ا ن, come three times: after one rubbed sign in line 1, after وا in lines 4–5, and after وا in line 7, there with ها after them. They are اجفان, “eyelids”: الاجفان, واجفاني, واجفانها. This gives ج, ف and ه, and the rubbed sign is the lam-alif the compiler mentions.
  3. The metre. With قد for the two-sign word, line 1 and the first two words of line 2 scan as a half-verse in the ṭawīl metre: wa-nāʿisati l-ajfāni qad jaʿala l-hawā. جعل gives ل.
  4. The sleepers. ولم انم, ونامت and ولم تسهر in lines 5 and 6 give م and ت. (The last word was first read تسهد here; its last sign is broken, and is the ر sign.)
  5. The rhyme. The last words of the two verses differ in one sign and share a middle sign with a word of line 4: ارضا, مرضا and سهرت. This gives ر and ض.
  6. The rest. The second half-verse has to scan and to end in لها ارضا, which leaves مهجتي مثوا وخدي after one damaged word, first read لها and now left open. The last open word, four signs in line 5, is صحاح, “sound”, set against مرضى, “sick”.

Two guesses failed on the way. The damaged word of line 2 was first taken as جهنم, and the sign for ر was first read as ع, which gave no word in line 4.

03 The verses

As the cipher spells them, letter for letter: final alif maqṣūra is written as alif, and there is no hamza and no doubling mark. [..ه..] is the damaged word that is not read.

وناعسة الاجفان قد جعل الهوا [..ه..] مهجتي مثوا وخدي لها ارضا
سهرت واجفاني صحاح ولم انم ونامت ولم تسهر واجفانها مرضا

In normal spelling, with vowels:

وناعسةِ الأجفانِ قد جعلَ الهوى / [..ه..] مهجتي مثوًى وخدّي لها أرضا
سهرتُ وأجفاني صِحاحٌ ولم أنمْ / ونامتْ ولم تسهرْ وأجفانُها مرضى

Many a girl with drowsy eyelids: love has made my heart [her] dwelling, and my cheek the ground beneath her.
I lay awake, though my eyelids are sound, and did not sleep; she slept and did not lie awake, though her eyelids are sick.

“[her]” stands for the unread word. The second verse is not the sender’s own: Ibn al-ʿAdīm (d. 1262) quotes it in Bughyat al-ṭalab under al-Agharr ibn Sinān al-Ḥalabī, with صحاح and تسهر as on this page (text; found after the reading). The verse that goes with it there is a different one.

The second verse turns on the two pairs: the lover with sound eyes does not sleep, and the girl whose eyes are “sick”, that is languid, sleeps. All four half-verses scan as ṭawīl (the second with lahā supplied for the unread word), and both verses end in -rḍā.

04 What is uncertain

One word is not read, and three signs are damaged and take their value from their words (grade M where the sign is damaged, I where it is inferred):

  • Line 2, the word before مهجتي: not read. A whole ه stands in a damaged patch with a stroke and a loop before it and a sign and a tall stroke after it. It was first given as لها here; Elbaum points out that at least two more letters stand there, and has no reading of them either.
  • Line 1, sign 6, the ة of وناعسة: a rubbed circle (I).
  • Line 1, sign 7, the lam-alif: rubbed (I). A small alif is written above the line before it.
  • Line 6, the last sign, the ر of تسهر: broken in the middle (M).

Corrections of 23 November 2026. Alan Elbaum read the page against the manuscript and sent eight corrections. Each was checked on the master image, and all are adopted. (1) The sender is Ibn al-Qabāqibī, not Ibn al-Qaysarani. (2) The compiler’s remark reads as given in section 01, with بحضوره, “in his presence”. (3) A small alif stands above the line before the lam-alif sign; this page said none did. (4) The damaged word of line 2 has more letters than لها. (5) The last word of line 6 is تسهر, not تسهد, in the manuscript as in the known verse. (6) The red letters are و ت ا ع س, not و ن ا ع س. (7) The ع of جعل is a regular ع; this page said it was as close to the ر sign. (8) The last letter of صحاح is a regular ح; this page said it was written like ج and that the writer might have slipped. The key is the same 22 signs as before.

The signs read ق and خ differ only at the top and come once each, in قد and وخدي. If they are one sign, the second word is وقدي, “and my stature”, which reads less well.

Besides the metre, the rhyme and a key in which no sign has two values, the reading was put through the project’s statistical test (docs/_validate.py), with a letter model built for it from 2.17 million letters of writers of the compiler’s own time and circle: the diwans of Ṣafī al-Dīn al-Ḥillī and Ibn Nubāta, two books of al-Ṣafadī (love verse and the letters he exchanged with other secretaries) and al-ʿUmarī’s chancery manual, from the OpenITI corpus. The reading scores −2.537 per letter. Keys of the same shape at random score −4.901 on average (the reading is 5.3 standard deviations above them), and keys fitted by the same solver to the signs shuffled score −2.881 at best on average and −2.745 at their best (3.4 standard deviations below the reading). That is a pass, but a narrow one, and the test is inconclusive at 89 signs: it shows that the text is Arabic-like under the key and does not carry the reading, which rests on the metre, the rhyme, the grammar and the red gloss. The text is short: run again with three other random seeds, the test gave one “weak” (2.7 standard deviations) and two passes, and in all four runs the reading scored above every fitted key. A fake reading fitted to the shuffled signs, run as a control, does not pass. The test was run on the text as first read, one letter different from the corrected text, and was not run again. Seven of the 22 signs rest on the sense of one word each (قد, مثوى, وخدّي, صحاح and two single signs, ة and لا), and the words are chosen by sense; none of the alternatives gives a verse that reads as well. The signs were not compared with the other alphabets of the volume (ff. 1r–106r), and al-Qalqashandī’s Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā (vol. 9) and Arabic Origins of Cryptology, where such verses might have been printed, were not searched. Two searches for the exact wording of the verses on 16 November found no other copy; the second verse has since been found in Ibn al-ʿAdīm (section 03), and the first has not been found.

A separate attempt on the same leaf, made from the images alone, did not read it and is kept in targets/ljs51/. Compared with this key on 16 November 2026, its sign labels, matched to this folder’s signs by best fit, agree at 77 of 85 aligned positions (eight of them labels used once), found the ṭawīl metre (its second verse scanned only with an added null alif) and a rhyme in -ā after ض, and had the two commonest signs (ا and و) right. Of its values for other signs, 11 of 37 positions agree; it gave the alif sign a second value as lam, which this key does not have, and its words are mostly not these. It is a failed search, run with a letter model whose normaliser split vowelled words (fixed since; not rerun, so it is not shown that the fixed model would fail): with the metre alone, more than 100,000 readings of the opening half-verse scanned, and it ended with a scanning candidate, not a reading. It read the second and fourth red letters over the first five signs as ta and ghayn, where this page read nun and ayn: it was right about the ta (Elbaum) and wrong about the ghayn.

Penn LJS 51, f. 106v: two verses

Two love verses in the tawil metre sent in cipher by Ibn al-Qabāqibī to the compiler of the volume: love has made the poet's heart the dwelling of a drowsy-eyed girl and his cheek the ground beneath her; he lies awake with sound eyelids while she sleeps with 'sick' ones. One damaged word of the first verse is not read. The second verse is quoted by Ibn al-ʿAdīm under an earlier poet. The compiler's own solution was on a leaf now lost.

# Reading: Penn LJS 51, f. 106v, the cipher note of Ibn al-Qabāqibī

Two verses in the ṭawīl metre, rhyme -ḍā. Twenty of the 21 words are read; the first word of the second hemistich is
damaged and not read. Corrected on 23 Nov 2026 after Alan Elbaum's eight corrections (NOTES.md).

## As enciphered (letter for letter, `python decode.py`)

وناعسة ^ا^لاجفان قد جعل الهوا [..ه..] مهجتي مثوا وخدي لها ارضا
سهرت واجفاني صحاح ولم انم ونامت ولم تسهر واجفانها مرضا

(^ا^ is a small plain alif written above the line before the lam-alif sign. `decode.py` prints لها for the damaged
group, from the three signs transcribed there; that is not a reading.)

## In normal spelling, vocalised

وناعسةِ الأجفانِ قد جعلَ الهوى / [..ه..] مهجتي مثوًى وخدّي لها أرضا
سهرتُ وأجفاني صِحاحٌ ولم أنمْ / ونامتْ ولم تسهرْ وأجفانُها مرضى

## Notes

- The cipher writes final alif maqṣūra as alif (الهوا، مثوا، مرضا), no hamza, no shadda, and "الأجفان" as a small
  alif above the line, one lam-alif sign, then جفان.
- Doubtful signs, read from sense and metre: the last sign of وناعسة (abraded; ة), the lam-alif sign (abraded), and the
  last sign of line 6 (broken in the middle; ر).

## Not read

- **[..ه..]** (line 2, before مهجتي): a damaged patch. The ه is whole; before it a short stroke and a small loop below
  the baseline survive, after it a sign and a tall stroke on a lifted flap. It was first read لها, from the parallel
  لها ارضا and the metre. Elbaum: at least two more letters stand there than لها accounts for; he has no reading
  either. لها is what the metre allows, not what the page shows.

## Withdrawn (23 Nov 2026)

- صحاح was marked as an emendation of صحاج. It is not: the last sign's foot turns to the right of the stem, as in the
  ح before it; the ج sign has its loop under or left of the stem. The word is صحاح as written.
- ولم تسهد is ولم تسهر: the last sign of line 6 is the ر sign, broken in the middle.

Seven of the 22 signs rest on the sense of one word each (ح, ص, ث, خ, ق, ة, لا).

## A parallel for the second verse

Ibn al-ʿAdīm's *Bughyat al-ṭalab fī taʾrīkh Ḥalab* preserves the second verse in a quotation attributed to al-Agharr ibn Sinān al-Ḥalabī. In the Suhayl Zakkār edition, the passage can be found by its opening سهرت وأجفاني صحاح ولم أنم. Ibn al-ʿAdīm has **صحاح** and **تسهر**, and so does the manuscript as corrected on 23 Nov 2026 (the cipher was first read تسهد here, wrongly), so the witness and the page agree letter for letter in this verse. The following verse in the history differs from the other verse on the Penn leaf, so the witness verifies only the shared verse. The attribution concerns the quoted poetry; it does not identify the correspondent named in Penn's prose. [Ibn al-ʿAdīm, text and transmission statement](https://www.islamarchive.cc/ketab_content/563/p-1881)

# Translation: Penn LJS 51, f. 106v (Ibn al-Qabāqibī's cipher verses)

Many a girl with drowsy eyelids: love has made my heart [her] dwelling, and my cheek the ground beneath her.
I lay awake, though my eyelids are sound, and did not sleep; she slept and did not lie awake, though her eyelids are sick.

("[her]" stands for a damaged word of line 2 that is not read; لها is what the metre allows.)

("Sick" eyelids are the languid eyes of the beloved, a standard figure of Arabic love poetry; the second verse plays
the sound-eyed sleepless lover against the sick-eyed sleeper.)
