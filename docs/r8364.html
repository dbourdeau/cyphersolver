Westminster → Paris · homophonic letter cipher and name codes in a clear letter · early 1573

Burghley to Walsingham, 29 January 1573: the ship sent to St Valery

→

BL Harley MS 260 f. 419r–v (DECODE R8364, catalogue 122), listed as “Unknown sender to …, Feb 1572”. It is a copy in Walsingham’s embassy letter-book of Burghley’s letter of 29 January 1573 answering Walsingham’s of 20 January. The letter is in clear English except for name codes and two short cipher runs. It is part of the plan to bring the Duke of Alençon to England.

The six-sign run is a place name that Digges printed in four letters of the same winter. Comparing the five copies, which use different homophones, with Walsingham’s file gives VALERY, the Channel port of Saint-Valery-sur-Somme. That word is the only cipher text recovered by a key (6 of 26 cipher tokens). The people behind the name codes come from a different source: a Folger chronology of 1573 that summarises this letter (below).

Summary. DECODE R8364 is two pages of a fair-copy letter-book, not an original. The ciphered letter is Burghley to Walsingham, written on 29 January 1573. Dudley Digges printed it in The Compleat Ambassador (1655, p. 327) with the cipher left as signs. The six-sign word is read VALERY, the Channel port of Saint-Valery-sur-Somme (grade M, probable): it is the same word in four letters of the winter, with the same first and third signs, and Walsingham’s own file holds “a French letter earnestly desiring that a ship might be kept in waiting at St. Valery” (11 Dec 1572). Added on 1 October 2026: a reader, Adam Wright, pointed out that a Folger Shakespeare Library chronology summarises this very letter under 29 January 1573 and names the people. The letter is about the attempt by the Duke of Alençon’s agents, Maisonfleur and La Mole, to bring the Duke to England on the understanding that Elizabeth might marry him. “Davison” is Maisonfleur, “Hall” is the Queen, “this last gentleman” is La Mole, and the cover-name “Glasgow” is the Duke, not the Archbishop of Glasgow as an earlier version of this page said. The boxed 9 reads as the Duke too. These are read from a summary, not from a key, so they are graded I. The nine-sign run is still unread, and the key of 1571 and the sent letter are still not online.

01 The record

Harley MS 260 is a later fair copy of Walsingham’s Paris correspondence of 1570–73, one letter after another with their addresses, under running heads by month. Folio 419r opens with the end of Walsingham’s own letter of 6 February 1572 (old style), in which he asks to be relieved. Then comes the address “To the right honorable Mr Fraunces Walsingham Esq, the Queenes Maiesties Amba[ssador] in Fraunce”, and the letter beginning “Sir I have receaved yor lres of the xxth of this month with the tickett after yor accustomed manner contayning [9]”. The copy breaks off at the foot of f. 419v with a struck-out “finis”. Digges printed it from another copy, with the rest of the letter (Worcester’s dishonour off Boulogne, the French libel against Burghley, Walsingham’s letters of the 24th), under the running head “The Lord Burleigh to Fr. Walsingham”.

DECODE’s sender (“unknown”), recipient (“…”) and date (February 1572) come from the letter-book’s old-style year and the address line. The letter is Burghley’s, to Walsingham, and is dated 29 January 1573 by the Folger chronology (after Conyers Read), which also says he wrote “partly in cipher”.

03 The place name: VALERY

Digges printed the cipher of Walsingham’s correspondence in cipher type; the earlier attempt had taken it for figures. A search of all 457 page images of his volume, first by the OCR’s word confidence and then by two page-by-page scans, found about 45 ciphered passages in the letters of 1570–73. Four of them are the same word, in letters about the one ship:

LetterContextDiggesHarley 260
Burghley, Westminster, 11 Nov 1572 (p. 301)“he hath required me to provide a passenger to attend at Harwich. Le the Port of …”2 0 ǂ 6 ɔ Δ
Walsingham, Paris, Nov 1572 (p. 303)“he desireth that the ship may stay at … 9 or 10 days”2 ɔ ǂ 6 r y
Walsingham, Paris, 20 Jan 1573 (p. 313; R8363)“a Gentleman of [9] departed hence to … with intention to imbarque there”2 ) ⌘ Ꭾ ) 42 1 ⌘ ε 9 4
Burghley, early 1573 (p. 327; R8364)“by Davisons writing of a ship sent to …”20 ǂ H Ꭾ )2 0 ‡ H 6 1

Every copy has six signs. The first is always 2 and the third always the same crossed sign (ǂ, ⌘ and ‡ are the founts’ and the copyist’s versions of it). The other four places change from copy to copy. That is what a homophonic alphabet gives when one word is written several times. The place is a French port where a ship waits for passengers bound for England, with Harwich on the English side. The Nov 1572 letter has Walsingham ask that the ship may stay there nine or ten days. Cotton MS Vespasian F VI, the volume of Walsingham’s letters and drafts of 1572–73, holds at f. 197 “A French letter; earnestly desiring that a ship might be kept in waiting at St. Valery. Dec. 11”. VALERY has six letters, and Digges’s copy of the Walsingham letter even ends in a plain “r y”:

V = 2 · A = 0, ɔ, ), 1 · L = ǂ, ⌘, ‡ · E = 6, Ꭾ, H, ε · R = ɔ, ), r, Ꭾ, 9, 6 · Y = Δ, y, 4, ), 1

A few signs turn up under two letters (ɔ/) under A and R, 1 under A and Y). They are the places where Digges’s type and the letter-book copyist blur distinct manuscript signs, which the Digges and Harley copies of the same letters show. The reading does not depend on them. Dieppe, the other six-letter port, needs a different sign for each of its two Ps and two Es in every copy, and nothing in the sources points to it. Harwich, Dover, Calais and Dieppe are all written in clear elsewhere in these letters.

Grade M (probable). No source gives the alphabet; the length, the fixed signs, the “ry” and the St Valery ship in Walsingham’s file all point to this word. With it the passage runs:

I see there was a great mistaking of our doings, for by Davison’s writing of a ship sent to [St] Valery, [tt] imagined that Hall had a meaning to provoke Glasgow to come hither; whereupon this last gentleman came to enquire of Hall some assurance, if [3] should so do, to enquire […]. This you must think must needs appear very strange.

Read for sense, the sentence is about an invitation. The people in it are identified in the next section. The port is the one in the French letter of 11 December 1572 in Walsingham’s file, and in Walsingham’s letter of 20 January (R8363) a gentleman “departed hence to [St] Valery with intention to imbarque there”.

04 Who is who: the Folger summary

On 1 October 2026 Adam Wright wrote that the Folger Shakespeare Library’s Elizabethan Court Day by Day (the 1573 volume) summarises this letter under 29 January, partly in cipher, and cites Conyers Read, Lord Burghley and Queen Elizabeth (1960), pp. 94–95. The summary names the people where our copy has cover-names and codes. Set side by side, clause by clause, it gives:

Harley 260 f. 419The summary saysSo
“the partie … sent from [9]”the party sent from Alençon[9] = Alençon
“hee spake with Glascoe 3 and Davison with him”he spoke with the Queen, and Maisonfleur with himDavison = Maisonfleur; “Glascoe 3” stands where the summary has the Queen
“tt ymagined”Alençon imaginingtt = Alençon
“Hall 3 had a meaninge to provoake Glascoe [9] to come hither”the Queen had a meaning to provoke him to come hitherHall = the Queen; “Glascoe [9]” = Alençon
“the laste gentleman came to enquier of Hall 3 some assurance yf 3 shoulde soe doe”this last gentleman came to enquire of the Queen for some assurance, if Alençon should do so, that she would marry himthis last gentleman = La Mole; the lone 3 = Alençon
“to enquier ^ 4 d H m 11 11 I J”(the marriage?)the nine signs may carry the marriage; unread

The people are the Duke of Alençon (François, the French king’s youngest brother), his agent Maisonfleur, who had a ship sent to bring him over, and the Sieur de la Mole, who was secretly at Whitehall on 23 January. The same chronology, under 22 January, says a ship sent for the Duke “waited in vain off the French coast for 23 days”. That is the 23 days of Burghley’s letter of 24 January (R8362), and the ship sent to St Valery of this one. The Archbishop of Glasgow is not in it: “Glasgow” is a cover-name, here for the Duke. An earlier version of this page, and of R8362, took it for James Beaton on the strength of the name alone.

What this does and does not give. These identifications are read from a modern summary, not from a key, so they are grade I. We do not know where Read had the plain text of the cipher. Two points do not fit one meaning: the digit 3 follows Glascoe and Hall where the summary has the Queen, but stands alone for the Duke, so it is left unread. And the clear name Glascoe is the Queen in one clause and the Duke in another, according to the digit that follows. The boxed 9 now looks like a code for the Duke rather than a bare marker: “the ticket concerning [9]” (R8363’s of 20 January), “sent from [9]”, “the ruin of [9]” and, in R8363, “a gentleman of [9]” all read well as Alençon. That is an inference from five contexts, not a proof.

05 What was tried for the rest

  • The key. Hsuan-Ying Tu’s thesis on Walsingham’s intelligence (York, 2012) cites the key itself: TNA SP 106/2 f. 141A, “Sir Francis Walsingham Cipher, 1571”. She gives the Harley 260 letters of 1570–73, among them f. 399 (R8363), as examples of its use. DECODE has eight keys from SP 106/2 but not f. 141A. The nearest in date, SP 106/2 f. 50 (R343), names Moray, Cardinal Châtillon, Dover, Dieppe and Calais and belongs to Norris’s embassy of 1568–70. Its homophones give neither VALERY nor sense in the Digges runs. SP 106 is online only in the subscription State Papers Online.
  • The sent letter. Cotton MS Vespasian F VI holds Burghley’s originals to Walsingham of 14 October 1572 (“with many cyphers”), 3 and 7 November 1572, and, at f. 245, a letter of 1572–3 from Somerset House that is probably this one. It also holds Walsingham’s drafts in clear of his letters of November 1572 to February 1573. The volume is catalogued “partly in cipher” and is not digitised.
  • The Calendar. CSP Foreign vols. ix and x were searched for letters “partly in cipher, deciphered” that could be paired with Digges’s cipher. Walsingham to Smith of 10 August 1572 (no. 527) is deciphered, but Digges prints that letter without its cipher sentence. Burghley’s letters to Walsingham are not in SP 70, and this one is not calendared.
  • The nine-sign run. Of its signs only H is known (= E, from the place name). Cribs such as passport, safe conduct, “the Queene”, “the Regent” and “Lord Seton” do not fit the shape. The Burghley–Walsingham runs of 1572–73 in Digges give only about 80 signs, loosely typeset, and too few overlap with this run to fix it.

06 What remains

  • The nine-sign run “^ 4 d H m 11 11 I J” (f. 419v l. 2): no key material (the key and the original are not online; one sign known).
  • Person codes 3 and II, and the exact value of tt and [9], which rest on a summary and not on a key. 3 is left unread because the summary does not give it one meaning. No key material.
  • The boxed [9] ×5 (read as Alençon, grade I) and the 3 after Glasgow and Hall (unread).

Of 26 cipher tokens, the six of the place name have a value (23%, grade M). SP 106/2 f. 141A, or a photograph of Cotton Vespasian F VI ff. 172–247, would probably give the rest of this letter and of its siblings R8356, R8358 and R8361, together with the 45 passages in Digges.
