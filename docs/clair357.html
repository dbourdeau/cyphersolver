An anonymous letter to a League leader, October 1586

BnF Clairambault 357 ff. 167–168 · a Catholic nobleman writes to a League leader during Catherine de Médicis’s talks with Navarre · listed as “two thirds of the first page unread”

A complete 1586 decipherment is bound with the letter: a clear text of the first part on the next leaf and plaintext between the lines of the rest. Both layers are transcribed, the interlinear glosses line by line at full resolution (October 2026), and both were checked against Tomokiyo’s partial key, which the check extends by a few values. Tomokiyo’s note does not mention the decipherment.

01 Three pages, two layers

Clairambault 357 is volume XLVII of the Bossuet-era collection of documents on Henri III’s reign (1583–1587). On Gallica it is btv1b9001053r. Folio 167r is canvas 331, the verso with the date and the sender’s signs is canvas 332, and folio 168r is canvas 333. The letter is not solid cipher. The writer drops into clear for a word or a phrase, and in clear he names Bordeaux and writes “tres fasché du bon terme en quoy sont voz affaires”.

03 What the letter says

From f. 168, with uncertain words marked [?] and passages not transcribed …:

Jay receu voz deux lettres en ung mesme temps, dont jay faict communication a Mr de la Forest et Mr de Corbet. … Indisposé d'une maladye qui ma travaillé pres de deux moys, qui a empesché vous escrire. … comme le voyage et negotiation qu'il entreprenoit [?] estoit contraire a celle de l'an passé, seroit trouvé estrange non seullement de ses amys, catholiques de France, mais des princes estrangers. Sur quoy il apportoit tout ce qu'il pouvoit de raisons. … qui luy promettoit l'asseurance [?] de ne desirer faire la paix qu'a condition qu'il n'y auroit que la Religion catholique en France … que chascun avoit faict son affaire, et l'avoit on laissé seul abandonné. … Je croy que faisant des stances [?] et autres places qui vous ont esté distraictes vous les pourrez avoir ou du moings faire congnoistre que vous n'y quictez rien.

From the interlinear decipherment of the rest, transcribed line by line at full resolution on 23 October 2026 with the writer’s clear stretches between the cipher lines, and the few unglossed groups decoded with the key:

Je vous diray que j'ay esté pressé, et la seule bonne volonté d'un que vous congnoissez s'y a mis de pratique, au moyen de reputation, qui s'appelle Fumée, vizadmiral de Guyenne, tres confident du Roy de Navarre, bien qu'il soit catholique et loyal a moy, a donné le retour de deça pour quelque negotiation … Il m'a plusieurs foys proposé cette reconciliation, qu'il dict luy estre aussy aysé et facille comme je la luy fais difficille et malaisée …

J'ay envoyé a Rendan les lettres pour la Royne de Navarre, et croy qu'elle les aura bien tost. [J'ay] receu hier une lettre de Villeroy, qui me manda que pour contenter Monsieur du Mayne … neantmoings on se servira de ceste occasion a conclure la paix, et si l'on donne quelque advantage aux Huguenotz, l'on se fondera [sur la] rupture de ceste armée, qui nous viendra passer sur le ventre et achever de ruiner ce pauvre pays.

“He has several times proposed this reconciliation to me, which he says is as easy and simple for him as I make it hard and awkward for him”: Fumée, on behalf of the King of Navarre, offers to carry the business through, with ships for an embarkation under cover of another voyage; the writer finds it “as thin as it is of little foundation”, like the Queen Mother sending the late Admiral to Florida. On the verso, Villeroy writes that the occasion will be used to conclude the peace, and if the Huguenots gain by it, the breaking up of the army “will come and pass over our bellies and finish ruining this poor country”.

The writer is a Catholic nobleman of the Angoumois or Saintonge. He mentions the abbey of La Couronne near Angoulême, Mrs de la Forest and de Corbet, and Bordeaux. He writes during the autumn of 1586, while Catherine de Médicis was negotiating with Henri of Navarre (the interviews of Saint-Brice took place that December). The addressee is set against Navarre as head of the other religious party, and Mayenne is named in the third person, so the addressee is most likely Henri, duke of Guise. The identification is inferred; the letter does not name him.

04 What remains uncertain

  • F. 168 is a fast secretary hand, and several phrases are transcribed only tentatively. Examples are the words after Charry, the passage on the abbot of La Couronne, and the last sentence. That sentence seems to pair protecteur with the Catholics, the reverse of what is expected, and needs the matching cipher words decoded.
  • Only sample runs above the cross were decrypted with the key and compared with f. 168. All of them agreed, but the page has not been checked word by word.
  • The interlinear part, transcribed in full on 23 October 2026: a few doubtful words (son [?edit], [?des lettres]), one cut gloss, and a few unglossed figure strings (under “ne pourroit procurer”, in the line “si porte bien”, and the last line before the date) whose strokes run together at full resolution. The interlinear part builds coherent sentences but reads at 94.2%, under the 95% bar; the clear text of f. 168 reads at 95.5%.
  • The sender’s signs at the foot of f. 167v (8 X X 8) are neither glossed nor in Tomokiyo’s table, and Fumée is not identified beyond his office.

# Clairambault 357 f. 168r, 28 Oct 1586: the clear copy of f. 167r up to the cross, English translation

I have received your two letters at the same time, which I have communicated to Monsieur de la Forest and Monsieur de
Corbet. Charry … being very vexed at the good state in which your affairs are, and that you will have got back in as
soon as possible [?]. Unwell with an illness that has troubled me for nearly two months, which has prevented my writing
to you. During that time, which was the reason that the clerk to the Abbot of La Couronne, by the means [by which] he was
to come to an understanding … of Monsieur de Nemours [?], and to hand over twenty [?] of their troops [?], who would take
advice from me, rather through you the marriage carried out, at which I showed much regret, … did not keep me from
telling him that the journey and negotiation he was undertaking [?], being contrary to that of last year, would be found
strange not only by his friends, the Catholics of France, but by foreign princes. Whereupon he brought all the reasons
he could. The best and most plausible [?] was … of the Queen Mother, from whom he gave support to whom he pleased, who
promised him the assurance [?] that she wished to make peace only on condition that there should be only the Catholic
religion in France; and he went further: that if he had been on the terms he had arranged with you, he would not have
undertaken the journey; that everyone had done his own business, and he had been left alone, abandoned. The rumour that
ran everywhere these last days [?] that you wished to come has indeed reached me. … the almoner, this [?], who was coming
to this country, … company and … will feel [?] richly … that gives me … thought … all this rumour that you recommended …
as it did not seem to me proper to do it. Meanwhile neither pressed nor included [?], which are the only causes that
should hasten you to it. I believe that by making stances [?] and other places that have been taken from you, you will
be able to have them, or at least make it known that you give up nothing there. The pursuit nor the labours [?] of the
quarrel between the King of Navarre and you, which I hold very difficult because of the character each of you has
taken, he of [protector?] … Catholics … Huguenots … which comes first. ✝

## Reading

Je vous diray que j'ay esté pressé, et la seule bonne volonté d'un que vous congnoissez s'y a mis de pratique, au
moyen de reputation, qui s'appelle Fumée, vizadmiral de Guyenne, tres confident du Roy de Navarre, bien qu'il soit
catholique et loyal a moy, a donné le retour de deça pour quelque negotiation, car je sçay comment de ce voyage a
Nerac et a la court, et il est bien adverty. Il m'a plusieurs foys proposé cette reconciliation, qu'il dict luy
estre aussy aysé et facille comme je la luy fais difficille et malaisée, et en est bien sy avant depuis dix jours
qu'il m'a quasy prié de la part du Roy de Navarre la bonne [?proposer], et que si vous y voulliez entendre, il se
promectoit mener avec moy se negoce a bonne fin, et par ce seul moyen se faisoit ouverture de la guerre [?sy] en
Angleterre, avec promesse et asseurance que la Royne d'Angleterre ne pourroit procurer des Huguenotz de la court, et
que luy Fumée, si luy voulliez commettre cette charge soubz couleur d'autre voyage, il vous fourniroit d'autant [de] vaisseaulx
qu'il seroit necessaire pour l'embarquement. Je ne me suis de bonne [?heure] [?avancé] de ces choses pour les trouver
aussy maigres que peu de fondement, et m'est [advis] quant la Royne mere envoyant le feu admiral a la Floride; mais
c'est pour vous […] juger que, vous fondant d'une part, l'on en faict autant d'ailleurs, [?a voz serviteurs].

J'ay envoyé a Rendan les lettres pour la Royne de Navarre, et croy qu'elle les aura bien tost. [J'ay] receu hier une
lettre de Villeroy, qui me manda que pour contenter Monsieur du Mayne, a l'instance, prieres et requestes qu'il a
faicte, son [?edit] a premier de son [?retour], comme aussy se retirent les reistres et Suisses et la reste. Car
[?moy] je croy qu'en deffault qu'il y a de toutes choses, et mesmes d'hommes, il ne pourroit faire au[…];
neantmoings on se servira de ceste occasion a conclure la paix, et si l'on donne quelque advantage aux Huguenotz,
l'on se fondera [sur la] rupture de ceste armée, qui nous viendra passer sur le ventre et achever de ruiner ce pauvre
pays. [Il] si porte bien, [?je suis vostre serviteur]. Je [vous] envoye [?des lettres] de M^r de la Forest.

Du 28^me octobre 1586.

## Notes

- The recto ends "et que luy" and the verso opens "Fumée, si luy voulliez": one sentence across the page.
- Glosses that correct earlier fragment readings: "Cambaronne" is "l'embarquement" (51 20 19 16 14 43 85 24 28 64 28
  19 26); "Coride" is "a la Floride" (20 70 20 35 70 37 88 34 25 28), with "le feu admiral" (Coligny) before it;
  "Saisonne" is "vaisseaulx"; the sign before "envoyant" is glossed "la Royne mere"; ✝o is glossed "la court" and R
  "Nerac"; the sign after "pour" in "les lettres pour …" is glossed "la Royne de Navarre"; ⊖ is glossed "Huguenotz"
  twice. 73 = d is confirmed ("45 31 73 D" glossed "d'Angleterre").
- "ne pourroit procurer" is the gloss; the figures under it read "ne s…roict" (sauroict?) and were not settled.
- The figure line at the end (45 31 73 70 28 47 88 28 …) is glossed only in part ("de M^r de la Forest"); the decode
  gives "lettre … de … rousse", so "[?des lettres]" is bracketed.

# Clairambault 357 f. 167r (from the cross mark) and f. 167v, 28 Oct 1586: English

Translation of `f167_interlinear_reading.md`, with the same holes: `[?word]` doubtful, `[word]` supplied, `[…]` unread.
Names modernised here only.

## Translation

I will tell you that I have been pressed, and the sole goodwill of a man you know has set things in motion, by means of
his reputation: his name is Fumée, vice-admiral of Guyenne, a close confidant of the King of Navarre, although he is
a Catholic and loyal to me. He has made the return journey here for some negotiation, for I know how [things went] on
this journey to Nérac and to the court, and he is well informed. He has several times proposed this reconciliation to
me, which he says is as easy and simple for him as I make it hard and awkward for him; and he has gone so far these
ten days that he has all but begged me, on behalf of the King of Navarre, to [?propose] it in good part, and that if
you would agree to it, he promised himself to bring this business to a good end with me; and by this one means an
opening would be made for the war [?if] in England, with a promise and assurance that the Queen of England could not
obtain [help] from the Huguenots at court; and that he, Fumée, if you would entrust him with this charge under cover
of another journey, would supply you with as many ships as would be needed for the embarkation.

I have not [?early] [?pressed on] with these matters, finding them as thin as they are of little foundation, and it
seems [to me] like when the Queen Mother sent the late Admiral to Florida; but it is for you […] to judge whether,
relying on one side, the same is done from elsewhere, [?to your servants].

I have sent to Randan the letters for the Queen of Navarre, and I believe she will have them soon. [I] received
yesterday a letter from Villeroy, who wrote to me that, to satisfy Monsieur de Mayenne at his urging, prayers and
requests, [the King] has made his [?edict] at the first [?return], as the reiters and Swiss and the rest are also
withdrawing. For I [?myself] believe that, for want of everything, and even of men, he could not do […]; nevertheless
this occasion will be used to conclude the peace, and if some advantage is given to the Huguenots, they will rely
[on the] breaking up of this army, which will come and pass over our bellies and finish ruining this poor country.
[He] is well, [?I am your servant]. I send [you] [?letters] from M. de la Forest.

28 October 1586.
