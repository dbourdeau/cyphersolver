Messina → Philip II · homophonic cipher in two-digit figures, alphabetical order · 16 July 1565

García de Toledo to Philip II, Messina, 16 July 1565

→

AGS Estado leg. 1394 no. 247, the duplicado of a ciphered despatch from the viceroy of Sicily two months into the Great Siege of Malta · four pages of Spanish with every sensitive word in two-digit figures · no decipherment on the leaf or in the catalogue

The key was recovered from two cribs in the cleartext around the figures. It is the alphabet in order on the figures 12–43, so once eleven letters were fixed the rest followed; most of the work was transcribing and decrypting the 1,930 figure groups, all of which give sense. Tomokiyo lists the Malta cipher letters of 1565 as having no contemporary decipherment; whether anyone held this key before is unknown. The covering letter written the same day, no. 249, deciphers with the key unchanged, every one of its 150 cipher tokens.

The letter
Don García de Toledo, viceroy of Sicily, to Philip II, Messina, 16 July 1565, with news from besieged Malta; plus a short covering letter of the same day (no. 249).
Where
Archivo General de Simancas, Estado, legajo 1394, no. 247, on PARES: the duplicado, in clear Spanish with names, numbers and intelligence replaced by runs of two-digit figures. Nothing on the leaf says what they mean.
The cipher
A homophonic substitution: figures 12 to 43 run through the alphabet in order, three figures for each vowel and one for each consonant, with no nulls and no code words.
How it was read
Two runs beside clear words, seiscientos soldados and en tiera, gave eleven letters; the order of the table predicted the rest. No. 249 was deciphered on 2 October 2026 with the key unchanged: all 150 cipher tokens give sense.
What it says
The 600 soldiers Don Juan de Cardona landed entraron en el burgo a saluamento, and a second run of galleys turned back at La Valette’s fire signals. The rest is money and fortification.
Still open
A handful of words are doubtful in sense, none in their figures. The original despatch, which could carry a contemporary decipherment, has not been found.

01 The document

The Archivo General de Simancas, Estado, legajo 1394, no. 247, is catalogued on PARES as Duplicado de carta cifrada de García de Toledo, capitán general del Mar, a Felipe II, rey de España, 16 July 1565. It is two leaves: four pages of text (ff. 1r–2v) and an address leaf, sealed and inscribed En manos del señor Franco de Erasso, for the King’s secretary Francisco de Eraso. The first page is docketed Dupp.do in the upper left. A duplicate was a second copy sent by another route in case the first was lost, and this one is a clerk’s copy: it carries copying slips that the original presumably does not (quesido for querido, puddo for puedo, lonze for onze).

PARES serves the six images (four text pages and the address leaf twice) as plain JPEGs about 915 pixels wide. That is the largest size the viewer gives; asking for any zoom other than the default returns a smaller image. At that size the figures are legible when a line is cropped and enlarged two and a half to five times, which is how every figure here was transcribed.

The letter is written in running Spanish, and the figures stand in the line wherever a word was thought sensitive: the relief, the Grand Master, the galleys, the signals, sums of money, place names, the enemy. Figure runs are written without word breaks, so a run such as 3618243616241829 is one or several words run together.

02 The cipher

A homophonic substitution on two-digit figures, in which the values 12 to 43 are assigned to the letters in alphabetical order. The five vowels and u/v have three figures each, every consonant one:

The alphabet has no j, k or w, and u and v are one letter, as in the clear hand. The table uses exactly the thirty-two values from 12 to 43, and no figure outside that range occurs in the letter: no nulls, and no code groups for names or words. Names are spelt out letter by letter, Caragoca (Syracuse), Augusta, Cabo Bono, Argel, Maltta, Mecina, Mosiur de Lenni.

The homophones are not used evenly. Of the three a figures, 13 occurs about 140 times, 14 about 95 and 12 about 50; of the e figures, 19 is used twice as often as 18 and nearly four times as often as 20; of the o figures, 31 about a hundred times against 40 for 30 and 13 for 32. The clerk had favourites. The overall letter frequencies are ordinary Spanish: a and e first, then s, r and o.

Four features of the hand affect the decryption. ñ is written nn (sennales, Espanna, campanna). Double letters are sometimes written single (en tiera) and sometimes doubled where the word has one (spantaado, quinta[a]les). The duplicado has its own copying slips, listed above. And the confusable pairs in this hand are 17/19, 35/36, 35/38 (a long-tailed 3 against a dotted 8) and 13/14.

03 How the key was recovered

Two cribs from the clear text. The second line of f. 1r has los followed by the run 36 18 24 36 16 24 18 29 37 30 36 and, on the next line, 36 30 27 17 13 17 31 36. Eleven figures with the first repeated at the fourth and eleventh places, standing after an article, fit seiscientos, and the next run then reads soldados. A few words on, the run after don Juan de Cardona puso is 19 29 37 25 19 35 14, en tiera, “put ashore”, spelt with one r. Together these fix a c d e i l n o r s t, with two figures each for a, e, i and o.

The alphabetical order gives the rest. Set out by value, the recovered figures were in alphabetical order: 13 14 a, 16 c, 17 d, 18 19 e, 24 25 i, 27 l, 29 n, 30 31 o, 35 r, 36 s, 37 t. The remaining values could each be predicted from its position and then checked on a word: 21 f, 41 x, 42 y and 43 z were filled that way and confirmed on 18212118373136 effetos, 15124119271836 baxeles, 42151329 yban and 332712431935 plazer.

Transcribe, decode, correct. The whole letter was then transcribed digit for digit with the clear text around the runs, and decoded by script. Nonsense in the output points to a misread figure, almost always one of the confusable pairs above; every correction to the transcription was found that way.

The audit. All 1,930 figure groups fall inside 12–43; all thirty-two values occur; no run has an odd number of digits. An out-of-range value or an odd run would be the mark of a transcription error, and none is left.

04 The check

A key built from the clear text could in principle only reproduce what the clear text already implies. The check is a name the clear text does not contain. On f. 1v, after the Grand Master’s signal not to enter, the clear hand writes y ansi lo dize and then the run 2830362539 35 1719 271929 2925, which decodes to Mosiur de Lenni: Monsieur de Leinì, that is Andrea Provana di Leinì, the Savoyard commander whose galleys sailed in the relief squadron, and exactly the man who would report whether the signals were made. Nothing in the clear text points to him.

The rest of the ciphered matter is consistent in the same way: Don Juan de Cardona’s 600 soldiers, the pilot Don Juan Çanoguera, the Conde de Módica serving at his own cost, Gian Andrea Doria’s claim on the viceroy’s funds, and the fortification of Syracuse and Augusta.

English

García writes from Messina on 16 July 1565, about two months into the siege and some days after the relief known as the Piccolo Soccorso reached the Borgo (Birgu) at the start of July. Everything below that is in italics in the Spanish was in figures.

  1. The relief got in. He had sent the King bad news by the last courier and writes now to soften it: the six hundred soldiers Don Juan de Cardona put ashore all entered the Borgo safely. A man just out of the Borgo says the Grand Master embraced them, unable to speak, and wept for joy; and that the next day the same men sortied against the Turks and killed more than two hundred, though García had written many times that there were to be no sorties. He has no letter from the Grand Master himself and expects one within two or three days.
  2. A second run turned back. The galleys meant to enter the harbour came within four miles of it, in good order. That night, an hour before dawn on Tuesday, the barqueta he had sent ahead to arrange the signals with the Grand Master got in. On Tuesday night the galleys came again to four miles off, and the Grand Master made the fire-signals, very clear, that they were not to enter, and repeated them within the appointed hour, as Monsieur de Leinì and all the others report. The galleys put back out to sea so as not to be seen, and reached Messina this morning. Çanoguera, who guided them, did it very well. Nobody knows why the Grand Master would not take the galleys in; perhaps the entry looked too hard, but García takes it for a good sign. As the signals ended the galleys heard a great volley of arquebus fire, which they judged a salute to show that the men were inside.
  3. The messengers. The man who left the Borgo that Monday has no news of the barqueta, which had not arrived when he left. The barqueta landed García’s messenger where it was ordered, and from there he was to swim to the Borgo; the signals show he arrived. La ciudad, Mdina, made the same fire, a sign that the man sent by that route with the duplicate had arrived too.
  4. Money. Even with money in hand he would not dare part with a real to pay debts. He will do what he can for Gian Andrea Doria, as ordered, and Doria will be the only one paid anything. He asks the King to reckon the Italians to be raised, the Spaniards to be paid, and the ships, which cost forty thousand escudos a month with two months’ pay already owed; biscuit to be baked; five hundred quintals of artillery by Spanish weight, cordage and timber for the jaretas; fitting out the eight galleys coming from Spain and eleven more being armed; casks, barrels, morions and cuirasses; and the infantry’s pay. The provision sent does not cover it: half the 48,000 ducados sent for the galleys is already spent and much of the rest is owed to the men. “Being without galleys ruins me.”
  5. Fortification. Work should begin at once on the landward front of Syracuse and on a cavalier facing the sea, where the ground would let it be stormed; and on the fortification of Augusta at the end of October, so that the occasion should not show the enemy “the second leap”. For this money is needed, and in this kingdom there is nothing but infinite debts.
  6. The besiegers. The man from the Borgo says all the Turks ashore, besides the bastions where they have their batteries, are ringed with trenches seven, eight and ten palms high, facing both the Christians and the open country; when he left, only fifteen hundred Turks at the water were without them. The Turks have lost many men, and sickness is killing men in their camp.
  7. At sea. The ships that a vessel carrying wine to La Goleta found off Cape Bon, García is sure, are the Algiers squadron coming to join the fleet. No ship had left the fleet at Malta when his man came away, and the governor of la ciudad says none either, as his original letter, sent with this one, will show.
  8. Postscript, in clear. The Conde de Módica serves well and at his own cost; summoned to Spain by his father the Admiral after his mother’s death, he will not leave Sicily until he sees how things end here.

The letter was written during the worst of the siege, while García was still gathering the force that landed on Malta as the Gran Soccorso on 7 September; the siege was raised days later. In July he had few galleys to spare, and this letter shows what they could do: put small parties into the Borgo by night and watch for the Grand Master’s fires.

06 The companion letter, no. 249

Estado leg. 1394 no. 249 (PARES record 3576586) is the duplicado of a one-page letter García wrote to the King the same day, 16 July 1565, docketed Dupp.do and addressed on its fourth image En manos del señor Fran.co de Erasso; images 2 and 3 are a blank verso and a blank leaf. The courier carrying no. 247 was already on board when La Valette’s latest letters reached Messina, and this is the note sent with them. It has 25 figure runs, 150 cipher tokens: 147 figures, two of a barred θ for ll, and one overlined M-like sign for r, neither of which occurs in no. 247. With no. 247’s table unchanged, all 150 tokens give sense; the word-divided decrypt scores −1.67 per character under the es-golden-age model, against −5.89 for the same letters shuffled.

English. As the courier was embarking, García received fresh letters from the Grand Master and forwards them, so that the King can see how much worse things are than he had thought, even with the relief got in. The bearer heard an artillery salute from the galleys of the Turkish fleet, which must mark the arrival of the ships sighted off Cape Bon, surely the Algiers squadron. Had he sent the galleys with the troops to La Goletta they would have arrived at a bad moment. He has held them back waiting for La Valette’s answer: had the Grand Master written that there was a way to put more men in by land, the galleys would not have been lacking; but he asks for more men than García can now give him, so the galleys leave tomorrow.

The figures carry exactly the military matter: the relief, the Algerian arrival, the galleys and troops for La Goletta, the wait for La Valette’s reply, the reinforcement and the departure. It is the same news as no. 247 in a short covering note, and it reads with the same key: a second text, not used to build the key, that the key deciphers in full.

07 What remains uncertain

  • yaretas (f. 2v, line 1): the figures are unambiguous, 42 12 35 20 37 14 36. It is taken as jaretas, the netting rigged over a galley’s deck; with the 41/42 confusion it could be xaretas, the same word.
  • secanos (f. 2r): “çierto es secanos donde podria ser assaltada” is taken as shoals or dry ground that would let the sea-facing cavalier be stormed. The figures are certain; the sense is a guess.
  • “salio del burgo el mismo lunes a los diez y nueue”: taken as the hour, not the day of the month, since 19 July is after the letter’s date.
  • Which duplicate went by way of Mdina (f. 2r, el que por aquella parte embie con el duplicado) is not said; it is presumably a second copy of García’s own message to the Grand Master.
  • No. 249: the last group of entrado is written 17·0, with a small mark between 7 and 0, and is taken as 17 30 (-do); PARES serves no image larger than about 970 × 1390 pixels, which does not resolve the mark, and any other reading turns the run odd. The overlined M-like sign (once, at the start of respuesta) is taken as r, and the barred θ (twice) ll; neither occurs in no. 247, and no key sheet or third letter in this key has been found to confirm them.
  • The originals of nos. 247 and 249, of which these are the duplicates, and the Mdina governor’s letter that went with them have not been found on PARES; a text search for the ciphered Toledo letters of 1565 returns only these two items. Estado leg. 1395 has not been searched. The original could carry a contemporary decipherment in the secretariat’s hand, and that would be the independent check on this decryption. Until it is found, the check is internal: the self-consistency of the table, the audit, and the names.
  • No published text of the letter was found in web searches for the document and for editions of the Simancas Malta correspondence (18 September 2026). That is not the same as a search of the printed literature on the siege, and an edition may exist that the searches missed.
  • The key table was rebuilt here from two cribs in the cleartext, not taken from a published source. The alphabetical key is easy to recover and the letter is on no list of open ciphers, so the key may have been known before, to Tomokiyo or from other material. The contribution is the transcription and decryption of this letter; no first break of the cipher is claimed.

# García de Toledo to Philip II, Messina, 16 July 1565 — deciphered

**AGS, Estado, leg. 1394, no. 247** — *"Duplicado de carta cifrada de García de Toledo, capitán general
del Mar, a Felipe II, rey de España"*, 16 July 1565. Six images on PARES (catalogue id 3576569, dbCode
27138924); ff. 1r–2v text, address leaf signed *En manos del señor Franco de Erasso* under the seal.

The letter is written in Spanish with every militarily sensitive word replaced by two-digit figures —
1,930 figure groups in all. *Words in italics below stand for figures in the manuscript*; roman text is
in the clear as the scribe wrote it. Spelling is the manuscript's own (*hauia*, *sennales* = señales,
*u*/*v* undifferentiated); word division inside a figure run is editorial, since the cipher writes none.

## What the letter says (English)

Written from Messina on 16 July 1565, about two months into the siege of Malta (begun 18 May) and about a week after the
*Piccolo Soccorso* reached Malta. Every proper name, number and item of intelligence is in cipher.

1. **The relief got in.** The 600 soldiers Don Juan de Cardona put ashore all entered Il Borgo safely.
   A man just out of the Borgo reports the Grand Master (La Valette) embraced the men and wept for joy,
   unable to speak; the garrison sortied next day and killed more than 200 Turks — sorties García had
   repeatedly written to forbid.
2. **A failed second run into the harbour.** The galleys came within four miles of the port, meaning to
   enter; the *barqueta* sent ahead to arrange fire-signals got in safely before dawn on Tuesday; but the
   Grand Master made the fire-signals unmistakably *not* to enter, repeated them within the appointed
   hour, and the galleys stood out to sea to avoid being spotted, reaching Messina that morning. Don Juan
   Çanoguera, who piloted them, "did it very well". The man the *barqueta* landed swam the last
   stretch to the Borgo, and the signals show he arrived; a second man, sent by the other side with a
   *duplicado* of García's message, evidently reached Mdina, which lit the same fire.
3. **Money and materiel.** Without galleys "me destruye". Forty thousand escudos a month for the ships,
   two months' pay owed, half of the 48,000 ducados already eaten; 500 quintals of artillery (by Spanish
   weight) bought, much cordage and timber for the netting, *botas, barriles, morriones y corazinas*; biscuit to be baked.
   He asks Philip II to count the Italians to be raised and the Spaniards to be paid, and says the
   provision does not suffice. Gian Andrea Doria will be paid what little can be managed.
4. **Fortification.** Begin at once on the landward front of Syracuse (*Çaragoça*) and a sea-facing
   cavalier where shoals would let it be assaulted; begin the fortification of Augusta at the end of
   October, so as not to show the enemy "the second jump". This kingdom has nothing but infinite debts.
5. **Order of battle inside Malta.** All the Turks ashore, besides their battery bastions, are ringed
   with trenches seven, eight and ten palms high, facing both the Christians and the open country; when
   the informant left, only 1,500 Turks were at the water. The besiegers have lost many men and sickness
   is running through their camp.
6. **At sea.** The ships found off Cape Bon by the wine ship bound for La Goleta are, he is sure, the
   Algiers squadron coming to join the armada — no ship had detached from the fleet at Malta when his
   informant left, nor does the governor of Mdina write of any.
7. **Postscript, in the clear.** The Conde de Modica serves at his own cost and, though his father the
   Admiral has written of his mother the Duchess's death and summoned him to Spain, he will not leave
   Sicily until he sees the outcome.

# AGS Estado leg. 1394 no. 247, García de Toledo to Philip II, Messina, 16 July 1565: English translation

## f. 1r

I brought Your Majesty such *bad news* by the last courier that it has not seemed right to me to hold this one back, so
that it may *partly* improve it. The *six hundred soldiers* that Don Juan de Cardona put *ashore*, God was pleased *to
guide them* so well that all *entered the Borgo safely*.

This man who has come out tells me that the *Grand Master's* joy was so great, *embracing* the men without *being able*
to speak, that *he wept for pleasure*; and he also says that the next day the same men went out to fall on the Turks and
*killed* more than two hundred, although *these sorties* I *wanted* him *by no means* to *make*, and I have *written* it
to him in *many letters* with great urgency.

From the *Grand Master* I have no *letter*, at which I am *astonished*, but I do not *take* it as a *bad* sign that he
forgets me, although I do not believe it will be *two or three days* before it comes; and according to what *he writes I
shall see to provide* whatever *can* be done for him.

Being without *galleys* *destroys* me, because some *effects* could be *achieved* against their *vessels* that come and
go; but with *those* that are here *occupied* in *gathering* the men, it is *not* possible *to provide for everything*. If
*those of Spain* came they would give me *much help*.

## f. 1v

The galleys *that were to enter by* the harbour *came within four* miles of it, in *very* good *order* and with great
*determination*; and that *night*, which will have been *Monday*, an *hour* before *dawn*, coming on *Tuesday*, the
*barqueta* that I *had* sent *to advise* the *Grand Master* of the *signals* *entered safely*, by which he had *word* of
what he *was to do*; and the *galleys* coming back on *Tuesday* night to within another *four* miles of the *harbour*,
the *Grand Master* made them *the* most *clear* *signals* by *fire* that they should not *enter* — and so says *Monsieur
de Lenni* and all the rest; and within the *appointed hour* he repeated *them* a *second* time, and the *galleys* *turned*
back out to *sea* so as *not* to be *discovered*, and have *arrived* here this *morning*.

Don Juan Çanoguera, who *was guiding them*, *did* it very *well*, and so did all the others. And this man does not know
*why* the *Grand Master* did not *want* to accept *these galleys*; although it might be because *the entry seemed* to him
very *difficult*, I do not fail to take it as a good *sign*.

All *our men* say that, as the *Grand Master* finished making the *signals*, they saw and *heard* a *great quantity* of
arquebus fire, and that *they* judged that it was a *salute* to *show us* that *our* men were *inside*.

This man who has come *left the Borgo* on the same Monday, the nineteenth, and *brings* no news of *the barqueta* that
*entered*, because it will not yet have *arrived*; which [barqueta] put *the man* I *sent* where I ordered him, and from
there

## f. 2r

he was to *swim* to *the Borgo*, and so he *arrived safe*, as is clearly seen from *the signals*; and the *city* made the
*same fire*, which is also an *indication* that the man I *sent* by that *side* with the *duplicate* *arrived*.

The *times* are such and the *needs* so great that even if *one had* much money I *would not dare to part* with a *real*
to pay *debts*; and although what Your Majesty orders concerning Juan Andrea is most just, little *can be done* with him,
but what *can* will not *fail* to be done as Your Majesty orders, for indeed I desire to give him every satisfaction, and
he alone will be the one at this time who *will collect from me* any quantity.

It is necessary to *set* hand at once *to begin to fortify*, against all that *could happen*, the *front* that *Syracuse*
has *on the mainland*, and to *raise* a cavalier *on the sea* side that *corresponds* — it is certainly *shoals* where it
*could* be *assaulted*. It is also necessary *to begin*, at the end of *October*, *the fortification of Augusta*, so that
the *occasion* should not show *the enemy* the second jump. For these things *money* is needed, and in *this* kingdom *there
is nothing* but *infinite debts*.

Let Your Majesty order a reckoning to be made of the *Italians* who are to be *raised*, and of the *Spaniards* who are to
be paid, and of the pay *of the ships, which every month comes to forty thousand escudos*, besides two *months' pay* owed
them; and let account be taken also of the *biscuit* that *must* be made anew, for although the galleys' share is later
deducted, still this *money* comes out of what Your Majesty has sent me. *Five hundred quintals* of artillery by the
weight

## f. 2v

*of Spain* have been bought, and much *cordage and timber* for the *netting*; and although this *cordage and timber* can
serve for *the galleys* that are to be built, and what is *metal is money* that is not *lost*, still it costs a good
deal. *Equipment* is also being made for the *eight galleys* that are coming from *Spain*, and other expenses for *casks,
barrels, morions and corselets* that cannot be avoided.

And if this is reckoned up, and the *pay* of the *infantry* is added, counting also the expense of the *eleven galleys*
that are being newly *armed*, Your Majesty will see that the provision made *is not enough* for *so many* things, nor *can*
I *sustain* with it the *expenses* that Your Majesty notes in *your letter*, all the more *if this* were drawn out at all.
And of the *forty-eight thousand ducats* that came for the *galleys*, *half* was already *eaten*, and another good part *is
owed to* the men. And *considering* this, I beg Your Majesty to order *provision* as shall seem best for your service; and
in *spending them* the care that Your Majesty orders will be taken.

This man who has *come out* says that all the *Turks* who are *ashore*, besides the *bastions* where they have their
*batteries*, are all *surrounded* by *trenches* seven, eight and ten *palms high*, both on the side of *our men* and on the
*side* of the *open country*; and that when *he left* there were no *others* without them except *one thousand five
hundred Turks* who were *at the water*. He confirms that they *had* lost *many men*, and that in the *camp sicknesses were
running* of which *they were dying*.

From those *reports* Your Majesty will see *the ships* that the *vessel* that *was going* with *wine* to the *Goleta*
found off *Cape Bon*. I hold it for certain that *they are those of Algiers*, who *are coming to join the armada*; and this
I believe because I do not know that any number *of them* has *left* *Malta*, for this man *says* that no *ship* had
*detached* *from the armada* when *he left*, nor does the *governor* of the *city* *write* it, as Your Majesty will see by
his original letter, which will go with this.

Whose life may Our Lord keep for as many years as his servants and vassals desire and Christendom needs. *From Messina*,
16 July 1565.

## Postscript (entirely in the clear)

I have already written to Your Majesty in other letters how well the Count of Modica serves you, and at his own cost;
and the Admiral his father having now written to him of the death of the Duchess his mother, and calling him to go to
Spain, although this matters much to him, he has not wanted to do it, nor to leave this kingdom until he sees the
outcome of things here. It has seemed to me very just to inform Your Majesty of it, so that you may understand his will
and the service he does Your Majesty in it.

## English summary

As the courier was already embarked with this despatch, García de Toledo received fresh letters from the Grand
Master, La Valette, and forwards them so that the king can see in detail how much worse the situation is than he
had thought, even knowing that the relief force (the *Piccolo Soccorso*) had got in. The bearer reports hearing an
artillery salute from the galleys of the Turkish fleet, which must have marked the arrival of the ships sighted off
Cape Bon, which Toledo is sure are the Algiers squadron. Had he sent the galleys with the troops to La Goletta they
would have arrived at a bad moment. He has held them back until now waiting for the Grand Master's answer: if La
Valette had written that there was a way to put more men into Malta overland, the galleys would not have been the
obstacle; but he asks for more men than Toledo can give him now, so the galleys will leave tomorrow.

The ciphered words are exactly the military content: the relief having entered, the Algerian arrival off Cape Bon,
the galleys and troops for La Goletta, the wait for La Valette's reply, the reinforcement question, and the
departure date. This is the same news as no. 247 (Cabo Bono, the Algiers squadron sighted by the wine ship bound for
La Goleta) in a short covering letter.

# AGS Estado leg. 1394 no. 249, García de Toledo to Philip II, Messina, 16 July 1565 (duplicado): English translation

S. C. R. M.

The courier being already embarked with this despatch, I have received these letters of the Grand Master, which I send
to Your Majesty so that you can see in great detail all that he writes, which is much more pressed than I thought,
knowing *the relief had entered*. This man who has brought these last letters says that he heard an artillery salute
from the galleys of the Armada, and it must have been *the arrival of the* ships that appeared off *Cape Bon*, which I
believe for certain are those of *Algiers*. And if I had sent *the galleys with the men to La Goleta*, they would have
arrived *at a bad time*. *I have* held *them* until now, waiting for *the Grand Master's answer*, because if he had written
to me that there was *a way to put more men into it by* land, it would not have stayed for *want* of them; but he *asks for
more than* I *can now give him*, and so the galleys *will leave tomorrow*. May Our Lord keep Your Majesty's life for as
many years as your servants desire and Christendom needs. From Messina, 16 July 1565.

Your Majesty's servant and vassal, who kisses your royal feet and hands, don García de Toledo.
