The letters
Four letters from Emperor Charles VI to Count Leopold Viktorin Windischgätz, his envoy at The Hague, dated 3 February, 13 April and 24 July 1720 and 11 April 1722; two survive in autograph and copy.
Where
Windischgätz family archive, State Regional Archives in Plzeň; DECODE R5019–R5024, catalogued with no decipherment.
The cipher
Two keys on DECODE: R5017, a two-part nomenclator with a syllabary of 1–400 and words from 401, and R5018, a private key of letter numbers and signs with about 300 codes.
How it was read
Both keys were transcribed in full from the DECODE images and applied as they stand; every cipher passage gives German that fits the clear text around it.
What it says
Alberoni’s fall and the fear of a separate French peace with Philip V, the Spanish marriage, and the Dutch Barrier.
Still open
Two nomenclator cells in the first letter are uncertain, and the clear text of the later letters is summarised rather than edited.
Credit
Jakub Mírka described the letters and keys in 2012 but printed no decipherment.

01 The letter and its key

The letters are autograph notes that Charles VI sent through his Oberststallmeister, outside the Chancellery’s ordinary despatches. The family later copied them into a register (book 5 of inv. 5), numbering them No. 1 to No. 7. No. 1, of 3 February 1720, survives as the autograph (DECODE R5019) and the copy (R5020, pp. 401–404); its cipher is 26 passages, about 190 groups.

R5017 is headed Ziffer Schlüssel mit der geheimen Hof-Canzley. It is the deciphering half of a two-part nomenclator. The numbers 1–400 are a scrambled syllabary of letters, bigrams and a few trigrams (schli, stre, spra), plus nulls (errans), punctuation and the controls annulla praecedentia / sequentia. The numbers 401–977 are words and names in alphabetical order, from Aachen to zu: 458 Cadagan, 513 Duc d’Anjou, 657 Mr. Moroille, 749 D. Orleans, 814 Robethon, 819 Stanhop. In this letter the Emperor writes that “den Ziffer, so ihr mit mir zu brauchen” would follow: the private key R5018, used for the later letters, a different cipher with its own page.

02 No. 1, 3 February 1720: Alberoni, France and the peace terms

The letter is two-thirds clear German, written in Charles’s own phonetic hand. The cipher sits inside the sentences, as in “absonderlich 89 312 251 65 14 312 20 48 96 genau berichten” = den Pentenrider. The autograph and the copy agree group for group. The 26 passages, in order:

So the secret instructions are these. Keep Pentenriedter fully informed, confide in the British envoy Cadogan, and show France and the Regent no mistrust. Watch them all the same: now that Alberoni has fallen, France may look for a separate peace with Philip V (whom Vienna still called only “the Duc d’Anjou”) through Morville, the French minister at The Hague. Find out whether Orléans means to make one. Win over the Portuguese envoy and bring his king into the Alliance. On the peace terms, nothing in the Quadruple Alliance treaty is to change: Sardinia for Lorraine is impracticable, the Montferrat equivalent is owed, and the costs of the coming peace must not fall on the Emperor.

03 Method

The records were fetched from DECODE with the project’s logged-in cookie. The R5017 syllabary (1–400) was transcribed column by column from the full-resolution scan. The nomenclator (401–999) was transcribed from tiled crops, with a confidence mark on each cell. The ciphertext of No. 1 was taken from the copy, checked against the autograph, and decoded by a short script (dec.py). Two slips in the key show up in use. Cell 31 is written “el”, but all three times it occurs it must mean zu, which is cell 3. Cell 268 is written “em” and must be ein.

04 What remains uncertain

  • No. 1, passage N: cell 446, whose key entry was transcribed as “Bischoff”, does not fit. The sense of the passage (measures and an accession obstructed by Spain) is C.
  • No. 1, passage X: cell 638 is uncertain (“Kommen” or “Kammern” in the key). Graded C.
  • No. 1, H: “Daroqui” as Count Tarouca, the Portuguese envoy, is M. The syllables are certain, and the next passage names Portugal.
  • All other passages are H.

## What the letter says

Charles VI writes in his own hand to Windischgrätz, his envoy at The Hague, six weeks after Philip V dismissed
Alberoni (5 December 1719) and while Spain was preparing to accede to the Quadruple Alliance. The cipher carries the
substance of the letter:

- Windischgrätz is to report everything precisely to **Pentenriedter**. He should speak confidentially with
  **Cadogan**, the British envoy, and keep Cadogan on side "in every way". Towards **France and the Regent** he must show
  no suspicion at all.
- The Emperor's fear: now that **Alberoni has been expelled from Spain**, **France may seek a separate peace with the
  Duc d'Anjou** (Philip V, whom Vienna did not recognise as King of Spain) **through Morville**, the French envoy at The Hague.
  At the end of the letter Windischgrätz is asked to find out whether (as is after all to be believed) **Orléans intends to make a
  particular peace with d'Anjou**.
- **Portugal**: Count Tarouca's good disposition is to be cultivated, with the aim of bringing **his king into the
  Alliance**.
- **The peace terms**: the Emperor will not see **the treaty made between us altered** in the slightest. Giving
  **Sardinia to the Duke of Lorraine** is "very ideal and hardly feasible". He insists on **the equivalent for Montferrat**
  and wants the burden of the future peace not to fall on him. Any alteration would wrong **all the allies, even the English themselves**.

# No. 1, Charles VI to Count Windischgrätz, Vienna, 3 February 1720: English translation

Dear Count Windischgrätz. Your letters, which you sent me privately through my Master of the Horse, I have all received
safely one after the other, as you will have learned through him, the Master of the Horse, as also my gracious
satisfaction; but I have not yet answered you particularly, because they consisted partly only of narratives, which I
have had approved for you, and partly you will have received the explanation and orders through the usual chancery
rescripts. Now I have nevertheless not wished to omit writing these lines to you myself, and to show you my satisfaction
with your zeal and with the conduct you have shown up to now both at Hanover and at The Hague, as also that your secret
letters are welcome to me, and that you are to continue with them, especially where there is something more secret, or
where you should find it necessary on some difficulty or query about the chancery rescripts, or otherwise in important
business. Now, to answer only some principal points: I send you herewith (if it is ready) or by the next opportunity the
cipher which you are to use with me, since the (?) of the Court Chancery could serve. Secondly, as I do not doubt, you
will with all diligence stay in good understanding with my ministers in France and England, and communicate to them all
you learn for my service, as they have also been ordered; especially report precisely to ⟦Pentenriedter⟧ everything that
you only will or the least ⟦suspicion make of France and the Regent⟧, which however set it apart with the necessary
caution; especially that you, graciously (?), where you believe you can well do it, also ⟦open yourself confidentially to
Cadogan⟧, but in no way show ⟦towards France the least suspicion either⟧. Concerning the Tories nothing has been
verified; but as for the ratification of the convention, I believe they will all long since have been exchanged, and you
can send them to me by a safe opportunity. That nevertheless on that account not (especially ⟦now that Alberoni has been
expelled from Spain⟧, ⟦[to] fear that France may perhaps seek a separate peace with the Duc d'Anjou through Monsieur
Morville⟧) is fit to be considered, and so it is also necessary to be watchful on one's guard, yet ⟦without the least
suspicion⟧ to look on. As for the Dutch aversion, you will learn the rest from the usual rescripts. The good understanding
and confidence ⟦with Count Tarouca⟧ is to be praised in every way; you will also continue and cultivate it further as far
as possible. We already find without that ⟦from Portugal⟧ his good inclination; thank him for it too; you will be able to
make a gracious expression; you can also say that 50 (?) it vexes ⟦his King me the Alliance⟧ and the ⟦publico no more
about it⟧ you could do than to bring his ⟦King into the Alliance⟧, on which also to proceed in every way because of his
own description (?), but you will be further mindful, for it is not yet the time; but on the above, ⟦think of Portugal⟧
for me; that will not well ⟦be, measures [?ere] it takes, and accession [446] one of Spain suppressed⟧ can be. The news (?)
gladly sent me in cipher are very dear to me, and so you will continue with all diligence and attention. As for the rest,
it is to be awaited and to be on one's guard; and you will seek in every way to keep ⟦Cadogan⟧ on side, and let him know
how I rely entirely on him and his zeal for the common cause, and thank him, and make known the injustice as well as the
harm ⟦to all the allies, yes, to the English themselves⟧, if one ⟦in the future peace⟧ in the least against ⟦the treaty
made between us should be altered⟧; that giving ⟦Sardinia to the Duke of Lorraine⟧ in the meantime is very ideal and not
well feasible; finally also ⟦as the [?] to the good faith of the treaty⟧, so it is not well to be thought of, that
otherwise ⟦the equivalent of Montferrat for me⟧ is most burdensome, not without, if also most just, to press for this
too, that in ⟦the future peace⟧ this burden should not fall on me, but, where possible, be cut off for the ⟦Duc d'Anjou⟧
or for him so ⟦great [to] [638] the benefit⟧. The rest you will learn otherwise, and I have nothing else to remind you of,
except that you continue with your diligence and application to give me all exact news, especially to see rightly
whether (as is after all to be believed) ⟦the Duc d'Orléans⟧ thinks ⟦of making a particular peace with the Duc
d'Anjou⟧; and that I am still most graciously well satisfied with your conduct, as I remain your most gracious lord.

Charles. Vienna, 3 February 1720.
