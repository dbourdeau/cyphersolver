Vienna → The Hague, Cambrai · two Imperial nomenclators · 1720–1722

Charles VI to Count Windischgrätz, 1720–1722

Státní oblastní archiv v Plzni, Rodinný archiv Windischgrätzů, inv. 694 (autographs) and inv. 5, book 5 pp. 401–416 (register copies) · DECODE R5019–R5024, none with a decipherment on record · the Emperor’s own letters to his envoy at The Hague, with the secret passages in cipher

Both keys are in the same family archive and on DECODE (R5017, R5018). They were transcribed and applied as they stand, with two key slips corrected, and every cipher passage in the four letters decrypts. Mírka (2012) described letters and keys but printed no decipherment.

Summary. DECODE catalogues six records of letters from the Emperor Charles VI to Count Leopold Viktorin Windischgrätz, his envoy at The Hague and later his plenipotentiary at the Congress of Cambrai, with no decipherment. They are four letters, two of them surviving both in the autograph and in the family’s fair copy. The records themselves name the keys: R5017, the old key with the secret Court Chancellery, and R5018, the private key that the first letter announces. Both keys were transcribed in full from the DECODE images and applied as they stand; every cipher passage decrypts to German that fits the cleartext around it. 3 February 1720: with Alberoni just expelled from Spain, the Emperor fears France may make a separate peace with “the Duc d’Anjou” (Philip V) through Morville. Windischgrätz is to confide in Cadogan, cultivate Portugal and bring it into the Alliance, and refuse any change to the treaty; Sardinia for the Duke of Lorraine is “sehr ideal”, and the Emperor insists on the equivalent for Montferrat. 13 April 1720: Spain and Orléans, and the burden of the Dutch Barrier. 24 July 1720: Beretti Landi proposes a marriage between one of the Emperor’s children and Philip V’s sons, but “they are both so small”. 11 April 1722: Beretti again, and the Spanish marriage. Jakub Mírka described the letters and keys in 2012 but printed no decipherment. Extent: all cipher passages decrypted. Two nomenclator cells in the first letter are uncertain, and the cleartext of the later letters is summarised rather than edited.

01 The letters and the two keys

The letters are autograph notes that Charles VI sent through his Oberststallmeister, outside the Chancellery’s ordinary despatches. The family later copied them into a register (book 5 of inv. 5), numbering them No. 1 to No. 7. DECODE has four of them:

6Vienna, 11 Apr 1722R5024 (copy, pp. 414–416)R50184 passages

R5017 is headed Ziffer Schlüssel mit der geheimen Hof-Canzley. It is the deciphering half of a two-part nomenclator. The numbers 1–400 are a scrambled syllabary of letters, bigrams and a few trigrams (schli, stre, spra), plus nulls (errans), punctuation and the controls annulla praecedentia / sequentia. The numbers 401–977 are words and names in alphabetical order, from Aachen to zu: 458 Cadagan, 513 Duc d’Anjou, 657 Mr. Moroille, 749 D. Orleans, 814 Robethon, 819 Stanhop. R5018 is the new private key, on a single sheet. Each of 24 letters has a number from 10 to 38 and a graphic sign, and the vowels have a second number. About 300 codes follow, as numbers 39–210 or two-letter groups (ab Anjou, fb Spanien, zi Hof, 69 Beretti Landi, 151 Oesterreich, 159 Orleans), and the nulls are 3 4 5 6 7 9. Several names were added later in paler ink, among them Fleury, Walpole and Windischgrätz himself. In the first letter the Emperor writes that “den Ziffer, so ihr mit mir zu brauchen” would follow; the later letters are all in R5018, as Mírka noted.

02 No. 1, 3 February 1720: Alberoni, France and the peace terms

The letter is two-thirds clear German, written in Charles’s own phonetic hand. The cipher sits inside the sentences, as in “absonderlich 89 312 251 65 14 312 20 48 96 genau berichten” = den Pentenrider. The autograph and the copy agree group for group. The 26 passages, in order:

So the secret instructions are these. Keep Pentenriedter fully informed, confide in the British envoy Cadogan, and show France and the Regent no mistrust. Watch them all the same: now that Alberoni has fallen, France may look for a separate peace with Philip V (whom Vienna still called only “the Duc d’Anjou”) through Morville, the French minister at The Hague. Find out whether Orléans means to make one. Win over the Portuguese envoy and bring his king into the Alliance. On the peace terms, nothing in the Quadruple Alliance treaty is to change: Sardinia for Lorraine is impracticable, the Montferrat equivalent is owed, and the costs of the coming peace must not fall on the Emperor.

03 Nos. 2, 3 and 6 in the private key

In R5018 the letters are spelt out one symbol at a time, mixing the number and the sign for the same letter freely, and names are given as codes. Decrypted from the register copy:

  • No. 2, 13 April 1720. “Das ob und Orleans sich endlich wider miteinander verstehen werden, ist schir zu besorgen”; and a project for how one might free oneself entirely “von dem onere der Barriere”, the Emperor’s cost of the Dutch garrisons in the Barrier fortresses.
  • No. 3, 24 July 1720. The Spanish envoy Beretti Landi, in the name of his court, has proposed “eine Heyrath zwischen ein oder andern meiner Kinder und Anjou Söhne”. Charles would stay “in guter Verstendnus mit seinen Hof”, but as for die Heyrath, “weren beede so klein” that nothing should yet be made known. His eldest daughter, Maria Theresa, was three.
  • No. 6, 11 April 1722, to Windischgrätz as plenipotentiary at Cambrai, opening with the Emperor’s grief at the death of his Oberststallmeister. The cipher covers what he writes “wegen des Beretti”, especially what he discussed “mit Beretti wegen einer Heyrath” between “Spanien und mir”. Beretti is “erbittert wider mich und Oesterreich”, yet it is not bad “sich des Beretti zu gebrauchen” and to show “ihm all Convenienz in utili et honorifico”.

The marriage theme runs from 1720 to 1722, well before the Ripperda negotiations of 1725 that promised the Emperor’s daughters to Philip V’s sons.

04 Method

The records were fetched from DECODE with the project’s logged-in cookie. The R5017 syllabary (1–400) was transcribed column by column from the full-resolution scan. The nomenclator (401–999) and the whole of R5018 were transcribed from tiled crops, with a confidence mark on each cell. The ciphertext of No. 1 was taken from the copy, checked against the autograph, and decoded by a short script (dec.py). The letters in R5018 were decoded by hand, because the copyist’s signs have to be matched to the key’s drawings. Two slips in the key show up in use. Cell 31 is written “el”, but all three times it occurs it must mean zu, which is cell 3. Cell 268 is written “em” and must be ein. In R5018 the copyist uses one sign for D and N, and one sign for Z and G; the numbered letters around each sign decide which is meant.

05 What remains uncertain

  • No. 1, passage N: cell 446, whose key entry was transcribed as “Bischoff”, does not fit. The sense of the passage (measures and an accession obstructed by Spain) is C.
  • No. 1, passage X: cell 638 is uncertain (“Kommen” or “Kammern” in the key). Graded C.
  • No. 1, H: “Daroqui” as Count Tarouca, the Portuguese envoy, is M. The syllables are certain, and the next passage names Portugal.
  • No. 2, first passage: the cipher opens with a V-sign, giving “und Orleans”. The subject, Spain, is inferred from the context. M.
  • No. 3, “ein Proposition” and “die Heyrath” each contain a copyist’s wrong letter; the words are H.
  • All other passages are H. Letters 4, 5 and 7 of the register (book pp. 409–414, 417 onward) are not on DECODE and were not seen. The cleartext of Nos. 2, 3 and 6 is summarised here, not edited.
