01 The letters

In the spring of 1620 the Bohemian war was spreading into the Empire. Spinola was gathering in the Netherlands the army that would invade the Palatinate at the end of the summer, and the Protestant Union was preparing its defence. Willem Lodewijk of Nassau (1560–1620) wrote from Leeuwarden to his brother Johann VII of Nassau-Siegen (1561–1623), the theorist of defence by militia, who had been commissioned to defend the Palatinate. Willem Lodewijk died at the end of May 1620.

The four pieces are almost wholly in cipher. A neighbouring letter in the same channel (fols. 22r–23v) has “1620” in clear and answers a letter of Johann of 23 February, which dates the group to February–May. The signature is in clear: “E. L. trewer bruder Wilhelm Ludwig graff zu Nassaw”. Two letters of the file (fols. 22–23 and 42–43) are in clear and not part of this reading.

02 The key and its test

The cover of the file says “Die Schlüssel sind zu C. 667 gelegt”. Image 55 of C 667 holds two 3×3 grids, a worked example dated 1620 and the nomenclator. The upper grid (AKT·BLV·CMW / DNX·EOY·FPZ / GQ·HR·IS) reads the 1620 letters; the lower one reads the letters of 1606 and 1609. The key was therefore not reconstructed here, and it is not claimed.

The grid was frozen by SHA-256 and tested before any letter was decoded, on eleven cipher words of fols. 25 and 29 that carry a contemporary gloss (“vnion”, “werbung”, “monatlich”, “künfftig”, “feindt”…). Mean agreement was 0.860, against 0.185 for the second grid and at best 0.442 for 20 permuted grids. Each transcription was frozen before decoding as well, so the key was never adjusted to a reading.

03 The reading

The reading is counted by script on the published text, with the statuses certain, probable, restored and unresolved. All four pieces together come to 1,388 cipher words: 1,184 certain, 154 probable, 25 restored and 25 unresolved, which is 96.4% certain or probable and 98.2% with the restored words. Letters 1 and 5 and the postscript pass the 95% bar; letter 3 does not without its restored words (92.6%), so by the repository’s rule the group counts as partial.

Willem Lodewijk writes “[A] and I” for himself and a person left unresolved. In letter 1 he calls a plan for the command of the relief forces “nicht practicabel vnd gantz immature”. Letter 3 answers Johann’s defence plan for the Lower [Q], very probably the Lower Palatinate: militia plus a regular corps, muster by Circles rather than one assembly of the Union, no invasion towards the Weser expected that year, provisions first. Letter 5 hopes Johann will continue in his charge and finds it dangerous to write such things “ohne chifer”. The postscript concerns officers’ pay fixed by the late Elector and table money in the field. The German text and an English translation of each piece are under Full texts.

04 What remains uncertain

  • The nomenclator capitals and symbols are printed as they stand, without a conjecture. From the sense only: [A] and [B] are persons or authorities, [Q] is feminine and very probably the Lower Palatinate, [O] and [⊙] look like rivers. The list of capitals in C 667 (images 20–54) has not been seen.
  • The dates are in cipher with number signs that are not resolved; the dating rests on context and on the neighbouring letter dated in clear.
  • The transcription of letter 5 does not record word spaces; the division was made from the sense. Letter 1, fol. 21v, lines 23–24, were not redone at high magnification.
  • The last line of the postscript runs to the edge of the leaf and its continuation has not been seen.
  • Most probable words come from a dot misread in the transcription. For letter 3 a blind re-reading of 22 shapes confirmed 21; the other pieces had none.
  • Prior work: a pencil slip on fol. 1, in an unknown modern hand, gives a partial alphabet and no decoded word. No edition of these letters was found (Groen van Prinsterer, Briefe und Akten, the finding aids). Three studies were not seen: Wolf 1937, Paul 1966 and Hahlweg 1973. If one of them holds an earlier reading it will be credited here.
  • The scans were used for research only and are not reproduced.

[A] and I have read Your Dilection’s letter with care and attention and weighed it thoroughly, [to see] what we might best advise you in such weighty matters. We hold, first, that no one is fitter or more capable for this service than Your Dilection, since you understand the work of territorial defence (Landrettungswerk) thoroughly, have put it into practice for many years in a praiseworthy and most profitable way, and know better than anyone the state of the country and the temper of the whole levy (Auszug) and of the other levies, on which the preservation of the Lower [Q] will partly depend; without your presence, direction and government little could be expected, and the common good, and the [B] above all, might suffer irreparable harm, which could be laid at Your Dilection’s own door, as if in the hour of need you had despaired of the common good and abandoned the [B] for private considerations — which we should be sorry to see. For [A] and I hope that the [B] has not the least thought of taking the general command from Your Dilection in the absence of the [A̅] and the [A·], after declaring you [∇] in your commission themselves; not only in view of equity and of the great difference in experience of war between Your Dilection and the others, who cannot be supposed to be preferred to you, but above all in view of necessity: the [B]’s own service and profit depend in the highest degree on Your Dilection being maintained in the command (“beim commendo maintenirt”). We also think that [A] and [A°], because of their particular interest, since they cannot be assisted by anyone as well as by Your Dilection, for the reasons given, must not be wronged. Therefore [As] and I hold that Your Dilection’s person and presence cannot be done without, without notable harm to the common good, and that the [B] will face the matter and give Your Dilection, for your own profit, a fair satisfaction; in that case we do not doubt that Your Dilection will weigh everything in good conscience and on your side know how to comply for the common good, since the other burdens are such that you can accommodate yourself to them; [As] and I are subject to the like ourselves, but with discretion and patience have overcome much and brought it to the best. Above all, if Your Dilection, in remonstrating your case, has done what the matter required, you cannot fairly be blamed in your person if the lord’s own service suffered and no hand was offered to you, or if it must be laid to the lord himself, who will not give better order, or to impossibility. Yet if, against all expectation, Your Dilection’s [∇] were not granted outright in the absence of the [A] and the [A°], neither [A] nor I could hold it against you that you keep to your resolution and resignation (“vfkündigung”), the more so as it would be not only most belittling for you but impossible to discharge your service properly, and you alone would remain answerable for what had been badly done, had turned out ill and might be another’s fault. If Your Dilection, because of age, from weakness of memory as well as infirmity of body — as I find in myself — felt too weak for so laborious and difficult a work, or noticed that the [B] or other directors of this work had doubts about it, I would advise you to do as I do myself: to count yourself an emeritus miles and withdraw in time from such danger and reproach, from which one must expect far more than thanks or reward, and leave the toil to those who want the thanks all the same. # English translation — Letter 3, fols. 41r-41v: Willem Lodewijk of Nassau to Johann VII, [spring 1620]

Working translation, close to the text; […] = unresolved word; nomenclator signs stay in brackets.

[A] and I find the proposal that Your Dilection made to the [B] about preparing for war in the Lower [Q] weighty and well founded, and hold it a necessity to rely in part on the country militia (Landvolk), since good foreign troops are hard to get and such costs are more safely spent to the profit of the levy (Auszug). We think, however, that a good corps [M] would be most necessary in so extraordinary an expedition and against the multitude of different nations on the other side; this has been thought of […] from afar. It would also be desirable that, as soon as the enemy begins to assemble, all members of the Union should likewise bring their assistance together in one place; but we hold it impossible to achieve, because no one will let his troops go unless assured that it will not fall on himself, and also because of the great distance between the quarters, and before one knows which course the enemy will take. Things should therefore be arranged so that the Circles hold their muster on their own ground and send one another the help they owe, once it is seen where the enemy is marching. In our opinion no invasion and no march towards the Weser is to be feared this year; so the Lower Saxon and Westphalian Circles will have only the defence of the country [2̲] and the Westerwald to provide for, and can send their help without any suspicion once the enemy has taken his route beyond the [O] or the [⊙]. If, when the enemy has joined at the general rendezvous, one could march against him instead of awaiting him on our own ground, that would be a thing to wish for, and Your Dilection is [master] […]; but whether it can be done will be decided by the state of our own affairs, the difference between our troops and the enemy’s, and other circumstances besides, as they turn out. In any case I think one must above all seek […] how to hamper the enemy most in his provisions, and that provisions can follow our camp conveniently for all its needs, and that this be always almost […]; […] can have; as Your Dilection has also prudently reminded, that provision be made, for a [∇], for the crossing of the [O], the artillery train and its order, the victuals and whatever else the camp needs, so that in the end one is neither taken by surprise nor cut short.

Working translation, close to the text; […] = unresolved word; nomenclator signs stay in brackets.

Now this has come to me almost against all expectation […], because in your last letter you made known quite a different opinion; and although, after [A] and I had read your letter and your reflections, one cannot blame you for seeking to free yourself from so doubtful an affair, yet since the common good, the [B] and your reputation are notably concerned, we should like satisfaction to be given you and you to continue in the charge entrusted to you, which we consider to be for the greatest service of the [B], and right too that they should meet you fairly in it and that you enjoy the fruits of the services faithfully rendered for [W] years now. We will nevertheless think further about the matter and send you our advice at the earliest. We find it very dangerous, however, to write such important and far-reaching matters over here without cipher (“ohne chifer”); otherwise everything here stays on the former terms; sure news that the [V] Portuguese mentioned before are coming into the [Q]; [that] the [B] also strengthens himself with troops in time, to be a match for the enemy.
