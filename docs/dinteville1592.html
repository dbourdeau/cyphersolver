01 The letters

f. 130 (no. 116) is a clear French letter signed by Dinteville, dated de Langres le iiije Juillet 1592. Its subject in clear is the enemy’s threat to return to Châteauvillain and the town’s request to the King and Nevers for two hundred horse and two hundred foot. The cipher breaks into it twice: about four lines near the top (ll. 4–8) and six full lines in the middle (ll. 11–16), 522 cipher symbols in all. After the date comes an autograph postscript in clear, cramped and partly struck out, on the Lorraine army; the verso carries only the address. Nothing on the leaf glosses the cipher, although DECODE describes R9451 as a letter “with cipher and decryption”. The day is iiije, as the 1882 summary in the Revue de Champagne et de Brie also gives it; the “3 July” of DECODE and of the first version of this page was a misreading.

f. 128 (no. 114), two leaves earlier, is Dinteville’s letter of 1 July 1592 to the same recipient, in the same symbols. Its three and a half lines of cipher (180 symbols) carry a contemporary decipherment written above each line, and the whole letter is printed in the Revue de Champagne et de Brie t. XII (1882), p. 340.

02 The key

The key was recovered by Alex Zarco (GitHub alejandrozarco), who sent it as pull request #24 on 6 October 2026, working with an LLM. Two things opened the crib that the first attempt here had failed on (section 05):

  • The marks belong to the symbols. The small crosses and strokes on some symbols, earlier taken for the decipherer’s marks, make separate cipher symbols: ∇ is a and ∇ with a rising stroke t; Δ with a tail below is a and plain Δ f; the o-shaped symbol with a cross above is s and with a plain ascender p; ψ is o and ψ with a cross below a null.
  • The gloss says Gennes. The interlinear reads a gennes (Genoa), not Geneve, and the cipher spells dessendre.

With both, Zarco made a new transcription of f. 128 and f. 130 that keeps every diacritic, and the alignment of f. 128’s second line against its gloss admits exactly one key, with only the leading double bar as a null. He then valued the symbols that occur only on f. 130 from context. The system is a homophonic substitution of 46 symbols: one to three symbols for each letter, four nulls (ψ with a cross, # with a long stroke, the double bar and a joined a-loop), and two symbols that the writer used for two letters each, # for c or d and 1 for e or i, so that context decides, as it did for the decipherer of 1592. No code words for names were seen. The full table is key.tsv in the repository.

NoAutopilot had published a partial key from the same gloss in their cipher-lab repository from 3 October 2026, with word fragments of f. 130, leaving the rows for h, Δ and a stemmed zero open. Zarco reports that he found their folder only after his decipherment and his blind test; every fragment they give agrees with his text, and his key settles their three open rows.

The checks made here on 7 October 2026:

  • The cipher under the f. 128 gloss and three lines of f. 130 (ll. 5, 6 and 14 at their openings) were compared with the transcription on the Gallica images, symbol for symbol. On f. 128, four tailed deltas had been written as plain Δ, so the final key gave f for a in auoir, Besançon, quarante and n’ayans; corrected, f. 128 decodes cleanly.
  • The decipherment of f. 128 agrees word for word with the 1882 print, including Gennes, doiuent partir where the gloss abbreviates, and d’escorte under the blot.
  • Scored on f. 130 with a French word model, the key gives −2.00 log-probability per letter; the best of 200 shuffled keys gives −4.29 (median −5.37). The key fixed on f. 128 alone, before any value was taken from f. 130, already gives 71% of f. 130 in words. A second transcription of f. 130, made blind from crops and a shape legend, agrees with the first on 95.6% of symbols and decodes to 97.3% of the same text. The statistical validation of 11 October (docs/_validate.py) passes: z = 7.6 against random keys and 25.2 against keys fitted to the shuffled ciphertext.

03 f. 130, 4 July

The two cipher passages in their clear context, in the writer’s spelling. Clear text is in ⟨ ⟩; […] marks the four open symbols of line 4, [?] an uncertain word, [?word] a doubtful one, and square brackets round letters mark letters supplied.

⟨…ce que J’ay eu auiourdhuy aduis de plusieurs endroitz que l’ennemy [?] faict estat dy retourner et⟩ l’emporter, […] il n’y estoit pourveu, qui me faict les y renvoier avec de la pouldre. ⟨Vous Iugerez aussi s’il vous plaist, Monseigneur, que⟩ je demeure seul ici avec les habitans et ⟨que la[rmée] … Lorrains [?] se fortiffie de ce qui estoit du coste de Strasbourg⟩ et, retournant a moi, il me seroit malaisé d’y conserver ceste place en l’obeissance qu’[?elle] y est. ⟨Messieurs de la Ville supplient tres humblement le Roy et vous d’y placer deux cens chevaulx et deux cens hommes de pied…⟩

⟨…Il y a plus de cinq cens chevaulx de garnison ennemye et le pais est […] soubz contributions sur ce qu[i …]⟩ si les forces n’y sont placées, oultre le hasart que court la ville, il ne se leuera un denier de tailles. D’aultre costé, l’en y voi[t] le dedans si bien basti qu’il ne soit plus a craindre que le dehors; les forces serviront tant a les contenir a leur devoir que a la deffence de la ville. Le Roi et vous m’excuserez si je [?pass] de mot, que [?] s’il ne plaist a Sa Maiesté d’y pourvoir, de m’en retirer, d’aultant qu’il y a a perdre la vie et l’honneur ⟨comme [?] luy. Je la doibs a sa Ma(jes)te…⟩

In English: the enemy means to come back and carry Châteauvillain unless it is provided for, which is why Dinteville is sending the men back there with powder; he is left alone in Langres with the townspeople, and if the Lorraine army turns on him he can hardly keep the town in its obedience. Without troops, besides the danger to the town, not a penny of taille will be raised; the troops would serve as much to keep the inhabitants to their duty as to defend the walls. If it does not please the King to provide, he asks leave to withdraw, since life and honour are at stake. The full translation is under Full texts below.

Each cipher run continues the grammar of the clear text on either side (retourner et | l’emporter, que | je demeure, l’honneur | comme luy), and the cipher gives the reasons behind the request the letter makes in clear. Of the 522 cipher symbols, 514 give sense (98.5%), none emended; counted by words of the reading, 95.5% read as sense. The Revue de Champagne of 1882 summarises this letter from its clear text only and marks the rest “En chiffres”; apart from NoAutopilot’s fragments, no earlier decipherment of these passages was found.

04 f. 128, 1 July

The cipher passage of the letter of 1 July, with the clear lead-in in ⟨ ⟩ as the print summarises it:

⟨…[un gentilhomme de M. de Gondy venant de Florence]⟩ m’a dit auoir veu dessendre a Gennes deus millions d’or d’Espaigne. Il y a laissé a Besançon quarante cinq mulets chargés qui doiuent partir dans trois jours et prendre le chemin de Vesou[l], n’ayans que cent chevaux d’escort[e] [blot]

A gentleman of M. de Gondi, coming from Florence, saw two million in Spanish gold unloaded at Genoa; forty-five laden mules left at Besançon are to set out in three days by the road to Vesoul, with only a hundred horse as escort. This is Spanish money on its way up through Franche-Comté. The text builds coherent sentences, but by words 93.2% read as sense, under the 95% that the project counts as complete: the opening words are the print’s summary of the clear lead-in, which has not been transcribed here, and the last letters of Vesoul and escorte lie under an ink blot.

05 First attempt

The first session here, on 21 September 2026, found the volume on Gallica, established that f. 130 carries no decipherment, and found the f. 128 crib two leaves earlier. It then transcribed 50 symbols of f. 128’s second cipher line by eye and searched (align.py) for a key of one symbol per letter, homophones allowed, with up to three null types, against the gloss read as avoir veu d’ascendre a Geneve deux millions d’or d’Espaigne. No key fit: auoir sat cleanly over the first symbols, but α then had to stand for both o and d. The session concluded that the cipher probably held code symbols for words and closed the target as not deciphered.

Both inputs were wrong. The crosses and strokes had been merged into their base symbols, which made pairs such as ψ and ψ+ look like one symbol with two values, and the gloss had been taken as Geneve and d’ascendre. Zarco’s bourdeau_test.py re-runs the same search: with the marks merged or the old gloss it finds no key; with the marks kept and the gloss read dessendre … gennes it finds exactly one. The search itself was sound; the transcription was not.

06 What remains

  • f. 130, line 4: the four symbols after l’emporter (the key gives ? i u s, perhaps ou s’il or puis). Blocker: the first symbol is unclear on the image.
  • f. 130, line 7: two symbols in qu’[?elle] y est. Blocker: the symbols are unclear on the image.
  • f. 130, line 14: the last symbol of pass[?] and the symbol after que. Blocker: the shapes are doubtful and no key value fits.
  • f. 128: the endings of Vesou[l] and escort[e], under an ink blot (the print supplies both), and the clear lead-in, given here from the print’s summary.
  • The clear text of f. 130 has uncertain words throughout, and the autograph postscript is legible only in fragments. These are clear-text questions; a better image or the original would settle them.

Checked for more material: ff. 127–131 of the volume; Tomokiyo’s catalogue of the Nevers ciphers, which has no Dinteville key; the 1882 print. The DECODE record is due a correction: R9451 has no decipherment on the leaf, the date is 4 July, and the key source is f. 128.

The cipher passages give the reasons he did not write in clear; 8 signs are open.

f. 128 (no. 114), Dinteville to Nevers, Langres, 1 July 1592 read in part

A gentleman of M. de Gondy coming from Florence reports two million in Spanish gold landed at Genoa, with forty-five laden mules left at Besançon to take the road to Vesoul under an escort of a hundred horse. The interlinear decipherment on this leaf is the key source; the plaintext is printed in Revue de Champagne et de Brie t. XII, p. 340.
