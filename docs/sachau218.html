Four lines of numerals, letters and invented signs in a Syriac-Karshuni miscellany from Midyat

Four underlined lines in a Berlin manuscript were transcribed as 59 signs of 26 shapes and one abjad key was tested and not supported, though the test was too weak to rule it out.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 19 November 2026

Method: not solved · Extent: text not obtained

At a glanceNot solved · text not obtained

The document
Four underlined lines at the top of one leaf of a Syriac and Karshuni miscellany compiled by a clergyman at Midyat in the Tur Abdin. The catalogue of 1899 calls them a cipher of numerals, letters and invented signs and gives no reading.
Where
Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, Sachau 218, f. 231a (catalogue no. 188 in Sachau 1899), digitised in the SBB collections. Catalogue item 2943, class C.
What was done
The 59 signs (26 shapes, about 13 groups) were transcribed twice and the transcription frozen. One key was written down in advance and scored once. A control then measured how likely that test was to pass.
Result
The key, abjad numerals, ranked 12th of 201 and gave no whole word: not supported. The control shows the test would almost never pass even with the right key, so the key is not ruled out. No reading is offered.
Still open
The language, whether the lines are a cipher or a table for the reckoning written below them, a check of the 26 shapes by a reader of Serto and Karshuni, and a second text in the same system.
Credit
Brice Dudout chose the lead and set the rule that the transcription and the test were frozen before any decoding; AI agents did the transcription, the test and the first write-up. The lead and its vetting are Daniel Bourdeau's (Arabic sweep 2, pull request 706).

01 The leaf

Sachau 218 is a miscellany “angelegt von einem Geistlichen zu Midjad im Tur Abdin”, Syriac and in smaller part Karshuni (Arabic in Syriac script); the SBB record dates it “17XX”. On f. 231a (image 467 of the SBB digitisation) four underlined lines stand at the top, written right to left, after the Karshuni Prayer of Solomon against the Red Wind on ff. 225a-230b. Below them, in large Karshuni script, lines 5-9 give a method for knowing whether a sick person will recover, with red dots used as Karshuni diacritics.

Sachau’s catalogue of 1899 describes the four lines as “vier Zeilen in einer Chiffreschrift, bestehend aus Ziffern, Buchstaben und willkürlich erfundenen Zeichen” and offers no reading. Nothing earlier was found: the catalogue vetting searched DECODE, Tomokiyo’s and Lasry’s key lists, the edition and the web, and the project’s own files hold nothing on the volume.

No image of the leaf is shown here. The SBB’s METS record names no licence, so the page links the image and does not copy it: SBB digital collections, PPN618589309, image 467. The SBB server answered HTTP 429 to rapid requests.

02 The 59 signs

The lines were transcribed twice, at two scales, by the same AI reader, and the result was frozen (checksum in the repository) before any decoding. They hold 59 signs of 26 shapes in about 13 groups, fewer than the 120-160 estimated at vetting. Groups are separated by clear blanks. The labels are neutral names for shapes. The commonest are I (8), F (6), M (5), D (5), and Q, R and C (4 each).

Some signs look like Eastern Arabic numerals: I (a vertical stroke, 1 or alaph), F (the Persian 4), E (the Arabic 4), N9 (9), O (a small loop, 5 or waw), L (an inverted V, 8), R (2 or rish), W (3). Some look like Serto letters and some like nothing in either script. The hooks at the right and the lassos at the left of each line belong to the underlining, and the strokes at the end of line 4 that look like ligatures are the ascenders of line 5.

Only the red dot under the first F of line 1 is surely a diacritic of a sign. The two passes agreed at 55 of 59 positions; all four differences were segmentation, not shape, and were settled on the image. The passes were by the same reader, so they are not independent, and no human has checked the transcription.

Line 1 of the transcription (a slash is a group blank):

F M / M2 I O F I M / Q E I R F W / D

03 One key, one run

The test was written down before any decoding and run once. The key, K1, treats each numeral-shaped sign as the letter with that abjad value: I = alif, R = ba, W = jim, E and F = dal, O = ha (ه, 5), L = ha (ح, 8), N9 = ta (ط, 9), and the red dot multiplies by ten (a dal under the dot is 40, mim). The suggestion came from Sachau’s word “Ziffern” and from the alphabet “of Simon”, in which letters are written with numbers. The other 18 shapes were left unknown and no value was adjusted.

The measure was the mean log-probability of the adjacent letter pairs that are both decoded inside a group, under an Arabic bigram model (the Tanzil Qur’an text, reduced to the 22-letter Karshuni alphabet), against 200 random keys that give the same 8 sign classes 8 random letters. The bar was a score at least equal to the best random key and at least 3 groups decoded entirely into attested Arabic or Syriac words.

Result: K1 is not supported. The score was −2.80 on 12 bigrams; the best random key scored −2.49 and the median −4.04, so K1 ranked 12th of 201. No group gave a whole word (fragments only). The text had no held-out portion, because there is no second document in the same system; the pre-registration declares this departure.

04 How much the test could tell

After the test, a matched control asked whether it could have recognised the true key at all. It took 200 runs of 13 consecutive Arabic words of 55-63 letters from the last 15% of the same corpus (the model retrained on the first 85%), enciphered each with K1’s design, and scored the true key exactly as K1 was.

  • The true key came first of 201 in only 16 of 200 trials. Its median rank was 12, the same as K1’s rank on the leaf, and 52% of true keys ranked 12th or worse.
  • The true key fully decoded 0.23 groups of two or more letters on average, and no trial reached the 3 whole words the bar asks for.

So the test could almost never have passed, even with the right key: K1 is neither supported nor ruled out. A fair test needs more text in the same system or values for more of the 26 shapes. The control uses Qur’anic Arabic while the plaintext of the leaf could be Karshuni or Syriac, which if anything makes the control kinder to the test than the leaf would be.

05 Other material looked at

  • Sachau 116 f. 1r has several alphabets (letters with red equivalents, alphabets in circles and strokes). None looks like the signs of 218.
  • Sachau 93 f. 75 has a Syriac atbash table and a ladder-shaped cipher in the margin. These were not tested: the signs of 218 do not read consistently as Serto letters, so no objective test could be frozen.
  • Sachau’s 1899 index of secret scripts (Bardesanes, Simon, Narsai and others) was seen only in the OCR text; the tables themselves were not all opened.

06 Language and nature

The language is not settled. Weak signs point to Karshuni: the Karshuni context before the lines and in lines 5-9, and the red dots used as in the copyist’s Karshuni. A second reading is kept open: the four lines may be a table of values (letters and numerals) for the sick-person reckoning that follows, a prognostic table and not a cipher. Nothing here settles it.

Sachau 221 f. 20b is a different system, a “tree” script, with its own work in progress; its check is not finished, and no reading or value of it is offered here.

07 What remains uncertain

  • The transcription: a reader of Serto and Karshuni should re-check it. Twenty-six shapes are too many for a clean simple substitution of a 22-letter alphabet, so some labels probably need merging. Not attempted.
  • A second text in the same system, to give a test with power and a held-out portion. Too short without it.
  • Whether the lines are a prognostic table rather than a cipher. Not attempted.
  • The Syriac atbash of Sachau 93 f. 75 and the alphabets of Bardesanes, Simon and Narsai. Not attempted.
