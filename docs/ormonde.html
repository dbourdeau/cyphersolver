Ireland / England · numerical cipher with nomenclator · 1634–35

Lord Maltravers to the Earl of Ormonde

→

Go to the full text ↓

Dorking, 13 September 1634 and Whitehall, 22 January 1634/5 · HMC, Calendar of the Manuscripts of the Marquess of Ormonde, i (1895), pp. 28–29

The letter alphabet, the nulls and the layout of the key were recovered from 59 printed figures and the cleartext around them; seven nomenclator codes were then identified from Wentworth’s own dispatches

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · partial

The letter
Two letters from Henry Frederick Howard, Lord Maltravers, to James Butler, Earl of Ormonde, reporting court news around the 1634 Irish Parliament.
Where
Printed by the Historical Manuscripts Commission in 1895 without a decipherment; S. Tomokiyo lists them among ciphers with no known solution.
The cipher
A numerical cipher, 59 figures. Consonants run in alphabetical order, three figures each from 7, vowels from 64; figures below 91 are letters, 91–111 nulls, and 112 and above a small nomenclator.
How it was read
The key was recovered from the printed cleartext around the figures. Wentworth’s dispatches, printed by Knowler in 1739, describe the same events in plaintext.
What it says
Kildare came to England “because he was angry” and the King refused to see him; in January 1635 Ormonde is to be a councellor, in exchange for Sir Piers Crosby.
Still open
Seven of the nine nomenclator codes are identified; the two parties in one clause about Crosby (185, 149) are not.

01 The documents

Maltravers (1608–1652), heir of the Earl of Arundel, sat in the Irish Parliament of 1634 and was a friend and correspondent of the young Earl of Ormonde. His letters from England report court news; the sensitive parts (the quarrel between the Earl of Kildare and the Lord Deputy, Wentworth, and what was being written about Ormonde himself) are in figures. The originals are in the Ormonde papers (now National Library of Ireland); the only witness online is the HMC calendar, whose figures were checked against the page scans.

13 Sept 1634:   ...he came over because he was 65, 35, 21, 26, 59, 98, 113, 186, 108, 143, refused to see him
                because he brought no 28, 69, 49, 50, 70, 44, 89, from 186, 99.
22 Jan 1634/5:  You desire to know what 93, 185, 95, hath written unto 99, 149, concerning 98, 10, 44, 79, 47, 8, 59,
                for whom 270 thus hereafter ... 95, 186, 98 hath written unto 98, 218, 97 much in praise of 100, 174,

                174 99 is to be 64, 99, 11, 79, 85, 35, 12, 69, 28, 29, 79, 44, in which courses 174 shall do well
                to continue.

02 Structure

  • Nulls, 91–111. Nine values (93 95 97 98 99 100 103 108 111); seventeen of their eighteen occurrences stand at the edge of a cipher run or beside a code number — 93 185 95, 99 149, 95 186 98, 98 218 97, 100 174 103, 111 95 221. Tomokiyo's guess is confirmed: they pad the names.
  • Nomenclator, 112 and above. 113 143 149 174 185 186 218 221 270, each standing alone; 270 is a code being assigned ("for whom 270 thus hereafter").
  • Letters, below 91. The spelled runs contain 49 50, 69 70, 28 29, 11 12 — doubled letters written with adjacent figures. Each letter therefore owns a block of consecutive figures, the commonest design of the period (Tomokiyo's survey of Wentworth-circle ciphers notes the same "three or six figures to each letter in regular arrangement").

03 The key

Three cribs fix the grid. "brought no 28 69 49 50 70 44 89" must be letters; "is to be 64 [null] 11 79 85 35 12 69 28 29 79 44" gives a councellor; "concerning 10 44 79 47 8 59, for whom 270 thus hereafter", the man whose fortunes were about to "go worser" in January 1635, is Crosby (Sir Piers Crosby, purged from the Irish Council by Wentworth that winter). All three fit one arrangement, and an exhaustive scan of 42 regular grids (start 1–11, block size 2–4, vowel blocks 2–6) ranks it first by English quadgram score:

04 Decipherment

Red = spelled in cipher · blue = nomenclator code, value from the documents in §05 · nulls dropped

13 September 1634. My lord of Kildare is long ago in England though I have not seen him; it is said he came over because he was angry [113 = with] [186 = the Lord Deputy]; [143 = the King] refused to see him because he brought no letters from [186 = the Lord Deputy].
22 January 1634/5. You desire to know what [185] hath written unto [149] concerning Crosby, for whom 270 thus hereafter; of which I can give you no account at all, only by what I hear it is like to go worser with him rather than better. [186 = the Lord Deputy] hath written unto [218 = Secretary Coke] much in praise of [174 = Ormonde] in general, and particularly for his good carriage in [221 = Parliament], and upon his motion [174 = Ormonde] is to be a councellor, in which courses [174 = Ormonde] shall do well to continue.

The history agrees with the decipherment. George FitzGerald, 16th Earl of Kildare, fell out with Wentworth over the Kildare estates during the 1634 Parliament and crossed to England that summer; Sir Piers Crosby, having opposed the Lord Deputy in the same Parliament, was removed from the Irish Council early in 1635; and Ormonde, who had backed Wentworth in the Commons debates, was sworn of the Irish Privy Council in January 1635, the month of the second letter. 186 as the Lord Deputy is used three times consistently; 174 as Ormonde ("your lordship") three times; 221 as Parliament follows "his good carriage in". The next section fixes 143, 218 and 113 from Wentworth’s own letters; 185 and 149 remain open.

05 The check against Knowler

Wentworth’s dispatches were printed in 1739 by William Knowler as The Earl of Strafforde’s Letters and Dispatches. Volume I covers 1634–35, and the events behind both cipher passages are in it, in Wentworth’s own words, with Secretary Coke’s answers in the margin.

Wentworth to Coke, October 1634 (i. 309). “The Earl of Kildare went away from hence without the privity of this State, and in seeming displeasure against me … within an hour after, he put to sea for England. Indeed I should not have mentioned thus much, but that I hear his Majesty refuseth to admit him to kiss his hand, which I acknowledge as a respect to me.” Margin: “The Earl of Kildare shall be sent back as is advised.”

That is Maltravers’s sentence of 13 September: Kildare angry [113] the Lord Deputy; the King refused to see him because he brought no letters from the Lord Deputy. So 143 = the King, and 113 is the connective between “angry” and the Lord Deputy (“with”; Wentworth’s word is “against”).

Wentworth to Coke, Dublin 22 December 1634 (i. 352–3). “In the Higher House, there is my Lord of Ormond, that hath as much advantage of the rest in judgment and parts, as he hath in estate and blood; and one, who upon my observation since I came hither, expresseth very good affections to the Crown and Government; so as I hold him a person of consequence, and fit to receive some mark of his Majesty’s favour, and humbly offer it to his Majesty’s wisdom, whether it were not seasonable to make him a Counsellor … so as I should think we had got much the better by the exchange of the Earl of Ormond for Sir Piers Crosby.” Margin: “for the Earl of Ormond, your Lordship shall receive herewith his Majesty’s warrant to swear him Counsellor, according to your advice.”

Maltravers, a month later: the Lord Deputy hath written unto [218] much in praise of [Ormonde] in general, and particularly for his good carriage in [Parliament], and upon his motion [Ormonde] is to be a councellor. The match is clause for clause: 186 = the Lord Deputy, 174 = Ormonde, 221 = Parliament (Wentworth’s “Higher House”), and 218 = Mr Secretary Coke, the addressee of the dispatch. The dispatch was for the King and Coke’s marginalia are the King’s answers, so “the King” is the alternative for 218, but the King already has 143. 270 is the code Maltravers assigns to Crosby after spelling the name once. James Howell, writing to Wentworth from Westminster on 1 January 1635, has Crosby in England and “in a bravery resolved never to see Ireland while your Lordship governs”: the court gossip behind “like to go worser with him”.

06 What remains uncertain

With the open codes and the doubtful figures below counted as holes, the written-out reading of the two letters builds coherent sentences but reads at 93.8% of its words (measured 24 October 2026), under the 95% a complete reading needs, so its extent is partial.

  • 185 and 149. Ormonde had asked what one party had written to another about Crosby, and Maltravers “can give you no account at all”. Since the Lord Deputy and the King have their own codes in the same letters, these are other parties unless the key carried homophones: the adjacent numbering of 185 and 186 suggests a grouped list of officers (Lord Treasurer beside Lord Deputy), and 149 beside 143 the royal or Council group. Only the key sheet, or Ormonde’s letter asking the question, would settle it.
  • The fourth figure of angry is printed 26, which the grid makes k; r is 43–45. The scan confirms the print, so either Maltravers slipped or the 1895 transcriber misread 44/45. "Angry" is the only word that fits a n g ? y.
  • 89 at the end of letter is either u (a slip for s, if the vowel blocks are six wide) or a null/plural sign (if they are five wide).
  • 31 (= m) before 174 in "upon his motion 31 174 is to be" is unexplained; perhaps "m[y lord]" begun and abandoned.
  • A key sheet, if one survives, would be among the Ormonde papers (NLI) or the Arundel papers; neither is online. The Bodleian’s online Carte Calendar begins at 1660, so the 1630s Carte letters to Ormonde cannot be checked for more of this cipher from here.

07 Method

  1. OCR of the HMC volume from the Internet Archive; both letters extracted and every figure checked against the page images.
  2. Regex scan of the whole volume (and the New Series volumes) for other figure runs: the only other ciphers (1644, 1650s) use different keys; no key or decipherment is printed.
  3. Positional test of 91–111 (edge-of-run statistic) → nulls. Consecutive-figure doublets → block alphabet.
  4. Cribs from the cleartext and the events of 1634–35; exhaustive scan of regular grids scored by English quadgrams; the crib-derived grid wins.
  5. For comparison, a simulated-annealing homophonic solver on the 28 letter-figures alone returns text with good quadgram scores and no sense. At this length the solution comes from the structure of the key; the statistics are too weak.
  6. Second session: the OCR of Knowler’s Strafforde’s Letters vol. I searched for Kildare, Crosby, Ormond, Maltravers and Arundel; each hit placed by letter heading, closing date and running head. The excerpts are in the repository.

Lord Maltravers to Ormonde, 13 Sept 1634 and 22 Jan 1634/5 (HMC Ormonde I pp. 28-29) read in part

Letter 1: the Earl of Kildare came to England because he was angry with the Lord Deputy (Wentworth), and the King refused to see him because he brought no letters from the Lord Deputy. Letter 2: Sir Piers Crosby's position is likely to go worse; the Lord Deputy has written to Secretary Coke in praise of Ormonde, particularly for his good carriage in the Parliament, and on his motion Ormonde is to be made a councillor. Both match Wentworth's dispatches in Knowler's Strafforde's Letters (1739).

## Letter 1.  Henry, Lord Maltravers to [James, Earl of] Ormond.  1634, September 13.  Dorking.
I can send your lordship little news from hence but only that all your friends are well, and for
my own part I was no sooner here but I soon wished myself in Ireland again, which makes me long
for the spring. I am now agoing to Arundel and shall return to London within a few days, when I
shall write to your lordship at large; and I beseech you to remember your promise that I may often
hear from you.
My lord of Kildare is long ago in England though I have not seen him, it is said he came over
because he was 65, 35, 21, 26, 59, 98, 113, 186, 108, 143, refused to see him because he brought
no 28, 69, 49, 50, 70, 44, 89, from 186, 99.

## Letter 2.  Henry, Lord Maltravers to Ormond.  1634-5, January 22.  Whitehall.
I have received several letters from your lordship and are by the hands of your father-in-law,
and as I told him I will do my best to do your service in that business concerning Sir Thomas
Butler, I have acquainted my lord likewise with it, who will move the judges in your lordship's
behalf, whenever it shall be. You desire to know what 93, 185, 95, hath written unto 99, 149,
concerning 98, 10, 44, 79, 47, 8, 59, for whom 270 thus hereafter of which I can give you no
account of at all, only by what I hear it is like to go worser with him, rather than better, 95,
186, 98 hath written unto 98, 218, 97 much in praise of 100, 174, 103 in general, and
particularly for his good carriage in 111, 95, 221, and upon his motion 93, 31, 174 99 is to be
64, 99, 11, 79, 85, 35, 12, 69, 28, 29, 79, 44, in which courses 174 shall do well to continue.
If your lordship know any that can do any good in discovering lands which were my lord's
ancestors, you shall do me a great deal of favour to set them on work; as for a purchase of land
I should desire it might be a very cheap one, and as great a scope of land as may be. I hear that
the plantations of Ormond and Connaught are like to go on, but of the particulars I know nothing,
I should be most happy to do your lordship service in any thing if I were able and shall desire to
be esteemed your lordship's faithful servant.

# Maltravers to Ormonde, 13 Sept 1634 and 22 Jan 1634/5: the reading

Written 23 Oct 2026 (verification 3). The clear text is HMC *Ormonde* I (1895) pp. 28-29 as quoted in NOTES.md §2;
the cipher figures printed there are read with the letter alphabet recovered from the clear context
(`key_alphabet.md`) and the nomenclator fixed against Wentworth's dispatches in Knowler 1739 (`key_nomenclator.md`,
NOTES §4a). Nulls are left out. Code groups are in capitals in brackets; `[?word]` = a value that is not certain
(*angry* rests on 26, which the alphabet gives as k; 113 is "with" or "against"; 218 is Coke or the King through
Coke); `letter[s]` = the last figure is not certainly s; `[185]`, `[149]`, `[31]` are unread codes. 270 is the code
Maltravers assigns to Crosby in the text itself. No manuscript, key sheet or contemporary decipherment is known.
The first paragraph is letter 1 (Dorking, 13 Sept 1634), the second letter 2 (Whitehall, 22 Jan 1634/5).

## Reading

My lord of Kildare is long ago in England though I have not seen him, it is said
he came over because he was [?angry] [?with] [THE LORD DEPUTY], [THE KING] refused to see him because he brought no
letter[s] from [THE LORD DEPUTY].

You desire to know what [185] hath written unto [149] concerning Crosby, for whom
270 thus hereafter of which I can give you no account of at all, only by what I hear it is like to go worser with
him, rather than better. [THE LORD DEPUTY] hath written unto [?Secretary Coke] much in praise of [ORMONDE] in
general, and particularly for his good carriage in [PARLIAMENT], and upon his motion [31] [ORMONDE] is to be a
councellor, in which courses [ORMONDE] shall do well to continue.
