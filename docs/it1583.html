A reply to Zorzo Maino, 4 May 1446

A 1446 slip from the Sforza chancery, clear Italian with runs of invented signs. It was identified and attacked with every Sforza key on DECODE, but the key was not found.

Daniel Bourdeau · attempted 21 September 2026 · updated 22 September 2026

At a glanceAttempted · not read

The letter
A reply from Francesco Sforza’s side to what his agent Giorgio del Maino had reported, dated in clear 4 May 1446. The clear clauses are general; the substance is in the cipher.
Where
BnF italien 1583 f. 68, part of the Archivio Sforzesco, on DECODE as R7898 with no sender, recipient or date. A second cipher of the same day, Vincenzo Amidani to Sforza (f. 70), is photographed on R7899.
The cipher
Invented signs between clear clauses: barred strokes, a phi, a long s, a cross-circle and others, some with dots or hooks. Related Sforza keys give several signs per letter, signs for syllables and words, and nulls.
How it was tried
Both slips were transcribed (404 and 390 signs) and attacked by computer against a Renaissance Italian model. Every run gave nonsense. None of the 208 Sforza key records on DECODE uses these signs, and no key names Maino.
What it says
Only the clear clauses are read: about not seeking something that “does not touch us”, and about seeking, “in the name of God”, what is due.
Still open
The whole cipher. It needs the 1446 key for Maino or Amidani, or a colour scan good enough to transcribe every sign and mark exactly.
Credit
Giuseppe Mazzatinti’s 1883 inventory of the Sforza papers in Paris identified the slip as a reply to Zorzo Maino, “In cifre”.

01 The document

The Paris volumes italien 1583–1593 are part of the Archivio Sforzesco that the Bibliothèque impériale bought in 1867. Mazzatinti’s entry for f. 68 sits under 1446 among letters to Sforza from Angelo Simonetta and Giovanni Stavoli in Venice, and from Foschino degli Attendolo in Cremona and Ancona. That spring Sforza was fighting in the Marche and bargaining with Filippo Maria Visconti, whose court at Milan Maino was reporting on. The slip carries an old number (21.), the folio 68, and the clear date line centred at the head.

The clear text, in Lombard spelling (nuy, havemo, refferemo), holds the frame of the reply:

… cetera. Che nuy no sapiamo [cipher] questi, ne cerchamo ne domandamo [cipher] tocha ad nuy cerchare questo, ma solamente cerchamo con lo nome de dio [cipher] nostra … [cipher] gli havemo rechiesto siano contenti fargli quello e dicto et ad quello se refferemo.

[cipher] voglia essere contento mettere per scripto [cipher] se tra loro no gli sera noma uno che no habia [cipher] debitamente lo debiamo havere [cipher].

The second paragraph asks someone to be content to put something in writing, and argues that if only one of them lacks it, “we ought rightly to have it”. Who and what are in the cipher.

02 The cipher

The cipher is written in invented signs: barred strokes, a barred b, a phi, a barred yogh, a long s, figure 8, a cross-circle and others, some with dots or hooks. Dotted separators (::, .:.) stand between runs, and three paragraphs open with the same three-sign group. With the diacritic variants merged, f. 68 has 404 signs of 26 kinds. Two signs are 12% each, two are 7%, and the rest trail off. Several long runs repeat: an eight-sign group twice, another eight-sign group twice, a four-sign group four times. The cross-circle stands directly before clear clauses several times. In the related key described below it is the word sign for de.

F. 70, Amidani’s letter of the same day, is all cipher: 390 signs of 31 kinds in another hand. It uses the same stock of signs, the phi, the barred b, the barred stroke and the cross-circle among them, but in different proportions. It is not certain that each sign means the same in both hands.

03 What was tried

  • Letter substitution by annealing. Both transcriptions were run against the it-cinquecento model with the clear text as context: free homophones, capped homophones, nulls allowed, and strict one-to-one swaps. The solver was first checked on a synthetic 400-letter Italian cipher, which it solved. On the real texts every run gave nonsense or collapsed into runs of one letter.
  • Word patterns. The repeated eight-sign group fits uisconti (Visconti), but the key that implies breaks the other repeats. It was set aside.
  • The Sforza keys on DECODE. All 208 key records from the Archivio di Stato di Milano (Carteggio Sforzesco, cartelle 1591, 1597, 1598) were downloaded and scanned. Cartelle 1591 and 1598 are sixteenth- and seventeenth-century. At the head of 1597 are eighteenth-century copies of fifteenth-century keys, each headed with the correspondent. Number 14, “Angelus cum cifra Vincentij”, was used with “Johannes de Stavolis, Augustinus, Baptista, Matheus, et Vincentius”. It gives two to five signs per letter, a sign for every syllable from ab to uz, word signs (che, per, quale, con, ma, quello, de, perché), names (la S. V., lo Duca de Milano, la Signoria Veneta) and nulls. That is why simple substitution cannot work here. But its letter signs are not the frequent signs of f. 68 or f. 70, so it is a relative, not the key. Number 17, the 1447 cipher of Sforza’s envoys to King Alfonso, does not match either. No key names Maino or Amidani.
  • Print. Mazzatinti gives only the heading. No edition or decipherment of either slip was found.

04 What would finish it

A system of this kind, 800 signs split across two hands and read from a binarized scan, cannot be broken from the ciphertext alone. Three things would change that. First, the key itself: the 1597 cartella should be searched at full size, and the fifteenth-century keys in Lydia Cerioni’s La diplomazia sforzesca nella seconda metà del Quattrocento e i suoi cifrari segreti (1970) should be checked. Second, a colour scan of italien 1583 ff. 68–70 from the BnF, so that every sign and diacritic can be transcribed exactly. Third, the seventeen other 1446 cipher letters from the same volume on DECODE (R7899–R7915), several from correspondents named on key 14. A reading of any of them in that key would show how far the family runs.
