The Marqués del Carpio to Baltasar de Fuenmayor, 1677

Ten ciphered letters from Spain’s ambassador in Rome to its envoy in Denmark, autumn 1677: the key was recovered from one letter’s marginal decipherment, and all ten now give Spanish sentences with English translations.

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · complete

The letters
Ten despatches of Gaspar Méndez de Haro, Marqués del Carpio, Spanish ambassador in Rome, to Baltasar de Fuenmayor, Spanish envoy at Copenhagen, 4 September to 11 December 1677. Each opens with clear compliments and turns to cipher.
Where
Archives générales du Royaume, Brussels, Secrétairerie d’État et de Guerre, inv. nr. 2559; DECODE R1002–R1011, 29 pages. DECODE lists eight as partly decrypted (the decipherment is in the margin) and two, R1004 and R1005, as not decrypted.
The cipher
A syllabic nomenclator: numbers 9–30 for letters, 31–60 for consonant-vowel syllables, struck numbers for a second syllable table, letter pairs with a disguised consonant (ge = de, ma = la), and about twenty three-letter code words for names and frequent words.
How it was deciphered
The key was recovered by lining up the 4 September letter with its contemporary marginal decipherment, then applied to all ten. On 22 October 2026 the eight margins were transcribed from the images and aligned word by word with the key’s output; each letter was written out as Spanish and translated into English. R1004 and R1005 carry a faint margin too, and it agrees with the key.
What it says
The French fleet off Messina and Catania; Orange raising the siege of Charleroi; riots against Spaniards in Rome, guns on Castel Sant’Angelo and French press-gangs at Civitavecchia; Innocent XI refusing Carpio an audience; the fall of Freiburg; d’Estrées at Turin threatening war in the Milanese.
Still open
Two place names at the end of R1006, illegible in the margin; one misread group in R1002 and one unwritten verb in R1003; struck 55 and a few struck numbers not placed in the key table.

01 The letters

The file is one of several in SEG 2559 holding letters to Fuenmayor from Spanish diplomats, 1674–79. DECODE describes Carpio’s ten as partly decrypted: “the solution is written in the margin, without revealing the key.” R1004 and R1005 are marked as not decrypted, meaning DECODE saw no decipherment on them. Each letter opens with a clear paragraph of compliments, then turns to cipher. The cipher is in one hand, in point-separated groups, with the null groups N, R, g and 4 struck through at the start and end of each block. The decipherment runs vertically in the left margin.

04 What the other eight say

These eight letters have the contemporary decipherment in the left margin. Each margin was transcribed from the images and set word by word against the key’s output; where the two differ, the sense decides and the choice is noted in the reading file. The Spanish of every letter and its English translation are under Full texts below.

  • 4 Sept (R1010). The French fleet has left Messina. It was said to be bound for Brindisi, but its design is to surprise Catania, and “given their great good fortune we must fear the success of whatever operation they undertake” (debemos temer el logro: the key gives temer, DECODE’s margin transcription tener). Carpio is dismayed by letters from Brussels that the Prince of Orange raised the siege of Charleroi intempestivamente.
  • 18 Sept (R1011). Balbases reports the effect of Charleroi at Nijmegen, and it is felt in Rome too. Those ill-disposed to Spain spread the rumour that Spaniards were raising men by force for Sicily; the populace has insulted and stoned Spaniards in the streets for more than fifteen days, and the Pope does nothing. Militia cavalry is brought in and more guns are mounted on Castel Sant’Angelo. Carpio contrasts this with the French: when their galleys were last at Civitavecchia they pressed more than a hundred men aboard, “without sparing the reapers on their way to work in the fields”, and Rome tolerated it.
  • 25 Sept (R1009). The rumours were false and fomented by Spain’s enemies, yet the Pope applied no remedy and would not even inquire. After conferring with the cardinals of the Spanish party, Carpio has reported to the viceroy of Naples and the governor of Milan and awaits their answers.
  • 2 Oct (R1006). The Pope and his ministers still give no satisfaction; Carpio awaits the governor of Milan’s advice before taking a final resolution with the cardinals of the party. The French fleet and galleys, after keeping station off Catania, are said to have turned back towards two places that are uncertain, probably Toulon and Hyères, the galleys badly knocked about.
  • 9 Oct (R1007). “This government” still withholds the satisfaction owed. Carpio waits for the viceroy of Naples’ answer and, on the cardinals’ advice, keeps away from the Pope’s audiences.
  • 16 Oct (R1008). Nothing new: the government stands firm, and Carpio will not move until the King’s orders arrive.
  • 4 Dec (R1002). On the King’s orders he asked Innocent XI for an audience on Tuesday 30 November and again on 1 December. Both times the Pope refused, without giving a reason, and said the King’s business should go through a cardinal. Carpio will not ask again nor treat the principal business until this point is settled, “a point as unbecoming to His Majesty’s service as the first”.
  • 11 Dec (R1003). The loss of Freiburg (taken by Créqui, 16 November) may change the plan of the winter quarters, the Duke of Lorraine having to recross the Rhine, and leaves Flanders in a miserable state. On the audiences he recalls the two refusals already reported; he has not asked again and waits for the advice of the Marqués de los Vélez, viceroy of Naples.

05 Method

DECODE’s ciphertext transcriptions (by RP and MEG, 2020) were parsed into tokens. The 4 September letter has a legible marginal decipherment, and lining its first lines up against the cipher showed the layout. The letter pairs looked like Spanish syllables with the wrong consonant. ca 9 [42] ca gi 24 against “va a imbadir” fixed a = 9 and r = 24, and 36 = na in both Mecina and encaminava. The rows of five followed from 33 mi, 36 na, 39 no and 48 ri. Every token of all ten letters was then tallied in context, which settled the consonant changes (gi-os Dios, lu-ar-de guarde, ne-lo-cios negocios). The decoder (targets/carpio1677/dec.py) applies the tables mechanically. The code words were first identified from context and from the historical events they fit: Charleroi, Freiburg, d’Estrées at Turin, Orange in London.

The decoder’s output is a stream of syllables, with stray letters where DECODE’s transcription has a slip. A reading audit on 22 October 2026 (George Lasry’s test: the text must form sentences that can be translated) found that only R1004 and R1005 had been written out as Spanish; the other eight had been summarised from the syllable stream. The same day the eight margins were transcribed from the DECODE images, DECODE’s partial margin transcriptions were checked against them, and each margin was aligned word by word with the decoder’s output. The two agree almost throughout. The alignment fixed seven code words (cen, ren/xen, xin, cun, tin, gen, 110) and struck 14 = ju, and corrected three of the earlier summaries: R1010’s “temer el logro”, the stonings and the Civitavecchia press-gangs in R1011, and the fleet’s return in R1006. Every letter was then written out as Spanish and translated into English; on 23 October 2026 the writer’s spelling was restored word by word from the margins (agrauios, recivido, governador, bolvio), without changing a word. All 3,504 cipher tokens are in letters that give continuous sentences; the holes are the few words listed below.

06 What remains uncertain

  • R1006, last sentence: two places the fleet returned to, the code word rin and the spelled vi ve re s. The margin is illegible there on the DECODE scan. Toulon fits the fleet’s return in autumn 1677 and the margin’s capital T (C); Hyères for Vieres is a guess (I). A better image of the original would settle both.
  • R1002: one misread group after “que Dios guarde”; the margin runs on without it, and the words supplied there are a guess at the construction. R1003: one verb (“[dejo]”) written neither in the cipher nor in the margin.
  • The cipher transcriptions are DECODE’s. The margins were transcribed from the images, but the cipher groups were not re-transcribed; a few misread numbers remain as untranscribed tokens ({..}) in the raw decoder files, and the margin covers them.
  • Key table: struck 55 (in Papa) and a few struck numbers ([25], [80]) are each read from context in their letter but not placed in the table. The three code words that were conjectures before the margins were aligned are now confirmed by them: tan = Flandes (R1003), cun iles = españoles (R1011) and lun = cartas (R1010).

# R1010, Carpio to Fuenmayor, Rome, 4 Sept 1677

The French fleet has left Messina. It was rumoured to be heading to attack Brindisi in the Kingdom of Naples, but I
have now come to understand that the design is to surprise Catania; and given their great good fortune we must fear
the success of whatever operation they undertake.

I am left so confused and downcast by learning, from letters I have had from Brussels this week, that the Prince of
Orange has raised the siege of Charleroi out of season, that I confess to Your Lordship I lack the heart to speak of
so irregular an event and one of such pernicious consequences; nor do I wish to discuss it with Your Lordship, so as
not to add to the grief it will justly cause you.

R1011, Rome 18 Sept 1677

Carpio to Fuenmayor: After Charleroi, rumours that Spaniards were raising men by force for Sicily turned the Roman populace against them; Spaniards have been insulted and stoned in the streets for over fifteen days with no action from the Pope, while militia cavalry was brought in and guns added on Castel Sant'Angelo. Carpio contrasts the tolerance shown to the French, whose galleys pressed more than a hundred men, reapers included, at Civitavecchia.

# R1011, Carpio to Fuenmayor, Rome, 18 Sept 1677

The Marqués de los Balbases writes to me that the effects of the siege of Charleroi are already felt at Nijmegen. But
that is no wonder, when at this court they make themselves felt with less reason, though to our greater surprise. As
soon as the news arrived, those ill-disposed to Spain spread the rumour that we were raising men here by force for
Sicily, and having [thereby] got the populace stirred up against our nation, they have insulted various Spaniards in
the streets these days. And although I have proved these rumours false, and the Pope and his ministers are not
unaware of it, I have not been able to obtain justice or satisfaction: not only is there [?no move] to punish the
seditious, there is [none] to find out the truth either. Such is my forbearance that for more than fifteen days I have
been putting up with their stoning the King's subjects, so as to put myself in the right and because I see that
their only aim has been to provoke me into a rash step. And they have brought in some cavalry of the militia of this
district, and mounted more guns on the Castel Sant'Angelo than there were before. This happens in plain view of the
irregularity and inequality with which they treat the French: the last time their galleys were at [?Civitavecchia] they
raised more than a hundred men and put them aboard, picking them by force, without sparing the reapers on their way
to work in the fields. This is proved, and it was tolerated, the Pope and everyone knowing it better than I. So Your
Lordship can infer the just displeasure I am left in, not knowing where these disorders will end, when the use of
all the gentlest means is not enough, and I must see to my own safety, to the dignity of my post and to that of the
King's subjects.

# R1004, Carpio to Fuenmayor, Rome, 13 Nov 1677

… [and at the same time I must] lament with Your Lordship how our ill fortune pursues us everywhere: with the arrival
of the Cardinal d'Estrées at Turin some great upheaval is feared in Italy, and the affairs of [Flanders] are in the
state Your Lordship knows. As for the business of the Congress, it seems we must suspend judgement until we see what
comes of the meeting of the Prince of Orange with the King of England. On the difficulties at this court I have
nothing to add to what I told Your Lordship in my previous letters, except that, although I expected His Majesty's
orders by this post, I have not received them; but I do not doubt the next will bring them, for they write to me
from Madrid that the letters in which I gave the first reports on these affairs had arrived there.

R1005, Rome 20 Nov 1677

Carpio to Fuenmayor: The signs that the next campaign will bring war in Milan cause great concern, the Cardinal d'Estrées at Turin not being idle. DECODE lists it as not decrypted; a faint margin decipherment agrees with the key.

# R1003, Carpio to Fuenmayor, Rome, 11 Dec 1677

The news of the siege of Freiburg is now very painful, for its pernicious consequences, since this mishap must bring a
change in the plan of the winter quarters, the Duke of Lorraine having to recross the Rhine; but I [leave] to Your
Lordship's consideration the miserable state in which Flanders is left.

⟨By the previous post I informed Your Lordship⟩ how His Majesty's resolution on the affairs pending at this court
had reached me, and that, having asked His Holiness twice for an audience, he had refused it both times in the
manner I reported to Your Lordship. As I have not asked for it again since then, there is no news to pass on to Your
Lordship: I am waiting for the answer to the advice I asked of the Marqués de los Vélez, which has not yet arrived,
and I have taken no step in the matter.
