The decipherments, on film

Short narrated films of ciphers worked on here: the manuscript, each stage of the decipherment and the reading, with captions. Each film links to its write-up, where the evidence is.

The Emperor’s Cipher: Maximilian I to Siena, 1496 3:10

Maximilian I, camped at Pisa in November 1496, puts his terms to Siena in a cipher of sixty invented signs. The key was recovered from the cipher alone twice, a month apart, by this project and by George Lasry with Claude; the film shows both routes on one word, compares the two keys sign by sign, and reads the terms.

Reading: George Lasry, with Claude. Manuscript: Archivio di Stato di Siena, via DECODE. Portrait: Dürer, public domain. Synthetic narration (Kokoro); score synthesised.

The Lost Codebooks: Jefferson’s Code No. 11 and No. 16 3:23

No codebook survives for two of Jefferson’s diplomatic codes. Starting from Thomas Barclay’s arrest at Bordeaux in 1787, the film rebuilds them from the meanings written over the numbers, tests the editors’ guess “sea”, finds Pinckney’s London code of 1793 at Tunis in 1801, and reads a passage of 1807 that no one had decoded.

Code No. 16 key: Andrew Beling. Images: Library of Congress; US National Archives; portraits from Wikimedia Commons, public domain. Synthetic narration (Kokoro); score synthesised.

Aaron Burr’s two ciphers, 1804 and 1808 3:35

The Burr–Hamilton duel in cut paper, then Burr’s cipher letter to Joseph Alston a week later, deciphered sign by sign: whether to stand trial or go to Europe. Then Baltimore in 1808, where Burr in hiding writes to his daughter Theodosia in a six-alphabet number cipher, and sails for England.

A Mamluk cipher poem deciphered, step by step 3:15

Ibn al-Qabāqibī’s cipher verses in a Mamluk secretary’s book (Penn LJS 51, f. 106v): transcribed sign by sign, deciphered from five red letters, a repeated word and the metre, corrected by Alan Elbaum, and read aloud in Arabic and in English.
