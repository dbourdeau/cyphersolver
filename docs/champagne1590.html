Champagne news letters to the duc de Nevers, June 1590

Go to the full text ↓

Three news-letters of June 1590 hide names and phrases in a short two-digit alphabet whose values rise with the alphabet; a few letters glossed above the figures and that order recovered it. Michael Brackx then found the key itself among the Duke of Nevers’s cipher tables and deciphered the letter of 5 June in part. Andrew Beling transcribed and deciphered the letter of 15 June and the slip of 7 June, and checked the key on glosses nobody had used.

The letters
Unsigned news-letters of June 1590 to Louis de Gonzague, duc de Nevers, from the royal army before Paris (BnF fr. 3623 nos. 24, 25, 60), in clear French with short cipher runs.
The cipher
A two-digit homophonic alphabet whose values rise with the alphabet, with nulls and barred figures for names.
The key
Nevers key no. 25 of October 1589 (BnF fr. 3995 f. 51r), matched by Michael Brackx from Tomokiyo’s catalogue. Its letters agree with the alphabet rebuilt here from the contemporary glosses.
Deciphered
No. 25 of 5 June (Brackx), 63 of its 64 cipher groups: 0.925 of words read as sense. No. 24 of 15 June and the slip no. 60 (Beling): 0.957 and 0.938. Together 0.940, by the same count; nos. 24 and 60 rest on one reader.
The check
Glosses over no. 24’s runs, not used before: 13 of 14 glossed figures agree with key no. 25; the best of 200 shuffled keys gives 5 of 14.
Still open
Whether one run of no. 25 ends “du M. de La Noue” (Brackx) or “du Roy” (Beling); doubtful words in all three letters; who Ludouic and Launay were; no. 78’s single group in another code.

The catalogue item grouped six letters to Nevers as one Champagne correspondence; since 14 October 2026 each cipher has its own page. The other two: no. 23, Bienvenut’s letter from Paris of February 1590 with its own name key (Bienvenut to Nevers, 1590), and Laurière’s letters of 1593 in Nevers key no. 57 (Laurière to Nevers, 1593).

01 The documents

The BnF notice and the DECODE records give the folios: no. 23 f. 35 (key slip f. 36r), no. 24 f. 37, no. 25 f. 38, no. 60 f. 88, no. 78 f. 111 in fr. 3623; on Gallica canvas = 2 × folio + 9. The two letters bound between them, no. 26 (f. 39, from Sy, 11 January) and no. 27 (f. 41), are entirely in figures with a contemporary decipherment between the lines, but in another key. No. 23 (f. 35, with its key slip on f. 36r) is on its own page, a different cipher.

02 Nos. 24, 25, 60: an alphabetical two-digit key

The glosses used at this stage give d u n i, a, n and, in no. 60, L u d o v i c (no. 24 carries glosses too, found later; see section 04). Set against the context they place a = 34/35, c = 37, d = 38, i = 53/54, l = 56/58, n = 63/64, o = 67, u = 93. The values rise with the alphabet, so every other figure has a predicted slot: e 42–45, f 46, g 47, h 48, r 74–75, s 82–86, t 92. Decryptions were accepted only when each value fell in its slot.

03 The key found: Nevers key no. 25

Satoshi Tomokiyo’s catalogue of the Duke of Nevers’s cipher tables (BnF fr. 3995) describes key no. 25, endorsed October 1589, as a substitution by two-digit figures with some homophones, nulls, and figures with an overbar for names and words. Michael Brackx compared the Nevers keys on their photographs and found that no. 25 (f. 51r; f. 50r is its wrapper) carries this alphabet: c 37, d 38, g 47, i 53 54, l 56, n 63 64, o 67, u 93, every value the glosses had given here. Keys 28, 30 and 38 do not. The table also settles what our transcription had flattened: a plain 39 is a null, a barred 39 is M. de La Noue, a barred 17 the comte de Soissons, and the plain 12, 19, 89 and 99 are nulls. His transcription of the alphabet, the nulls and six barred entries is in texts/key25.md. Andrew Beling has since transcribed the sheet’s names 1 to 51, its word list, cities and provinces (beling/transcription/), which confirms those six entries; the blocks headed “Mots” and “Dames” are not transcribed, and his transcription of the sheet has had no second reader.

No. 25, 5 June 1590. Brackx transcribed the whole recto, clear text and cipher. With key no. 25, 63 of its 64 cipher groups decode; one isolated figure 3 stays unread:

  • Nous treuvons bon la rupture de l’edict [de] l’union
  • la venue d’un qui faict les affaires de chaseron broille quelque chose v[ost]re seruice
  • vos troupes disent qu’elles ne combatroient point contre la ligue
  • Joynez a cela qu’on dict: comte de Soissons [?] il se defie du M[onsieu]r de la noue plus que de l’autre. [?Il] a aussi de la defiance du comte de Soissons, lequel n’ose veoir ny caresser personne

One run is disputed. Brackx reads the end of the Soissons run as 38 93 and a barred 39: il se defie du M[onsieu]r de la noue. Andrew Beling, in a second transcription of the page made before he opened Brackx’s (by his record; he had seen this page’s table of runs, so not fully blind), reads 38 93 and a barred 9: il se defie du [Roy], the comte de Soissons distrusting the King. He counts five digits after the 43, with the bar over the last 9 only and the letters d u written over 38 93. The independent verification of his contribution looked at the same place on the Gallica image and agrees with him. Brackx has not yet been asked to look again, so both readings stand here, and the texts below keep his as he wrote it. In l’union both readers see 58 on the page; the sheet gives l as 56 only, so 56 is a correction of the encipherer’s figure.

The letter around the runs is a newsletter from the royal army before Paris: Paris held by famine and division, the legate and the Spanish ambassador “at the end of their roll”, the Pope waiting for the Holy Spirit, Mayenne gathering weak forces, Villeroy working for peace in vain, Chasteaudun taken by the League. Measured on Brackx’s reading, 0.925 of words read as sense; most holes are doubtful words in the clear text. That is a partial result, under the 95% bar. The second reader agrees on 14 of the 37 words Brackx marks as doubtful, reads 13 of them otherwise and leaves 10 doubtful for both; he differs in about 18 other words and accepts Brackx’s reading in 7 of those. Three of his alternatives change a name or a sense, and each rests on him alone: elle demeura a Gonnesse for d’ille derechef a Compiegne, la bataille d’Auvergne for d’Arques, and a la puissance for a la misericorde.

04 Nos. 24 and 60, and a check on unused glosses

Andrew Beling (pull request 422, 11 November 2026, working with AI agents) transcribed the clear text of no. 24 and of the slip no. 60 and deciphered their runs with key no. 25.

The check. Both runs of no. 24 carry small letters written over the figures by the decipherer of the time. Nothing on this page had used them. Beling wrote down the test before transcribing them (by his record; the pull request is a single commit): key no. 25 unchanged, figures and glosses transcribed into separate files, a pass at 0.85 with a margin of 0.30 over the best of 200 shuffled keys. Of the 14 figures with a legible gloss, 13 agree with the key (0.929); the shuffled keys average 0.053 and the best reaches 0.357. The one miss is a y written over a null or over the barred 9, which the key cannot give as a letter. The verification reproduced the figures and ran 6,000 more shuffled keys (best 0.429). Two limits: the sample is 14 figures, and two of the 14 are not independent, because their glosses l and u sit over the very figures Beling re-read (95 93 to a barred 9, 56, 93); the image supports that re-reading. The check adds to Brackx’s match; it would not carry the key alone.

No. 24, 15 June 1590. A newsletter from the royal camp before Paris for 11 to 15 June. In cipher:

  • du nom du secretaire de [duc de Guise], par celuy de [M. la Guiche] and L’on dict que [M. de la Chastre] se practique: the barred 22, 51 and 44 of the key’s name table.
  • la verité du faict de Ludouic, the name that no. 60 glosses.
  • Les intelligences qui y sont: the Roman numeral xliij, “Intelligence” in the key’s word list, followed by a plain 84 for s. The 84 is not barred.
  • il demeura a Senlis mal disposé: le Roy lu[y] au[o]it refuzé ceste commission comme onereuse.

The clear text reports little hope of peace, the King’s night march of the 11th to stop Saint-Paul at Meaux, the bread given to the poor in Paris, four soldiers who had seized the keep of Vincennes hanged and thrown from the battlements, the army’s quarters around Paris on the 14th, the death of Richelieu, the grand provost, Brigard refused a passport to Mayenne, and Mayenne’s siege of Crécy-en-Laonnois raised. The run figures were compared with the Gallica image at the verification and agree, 25 of 25; the clear text beyond the run lines was not checked. One figure was added when the contribution was taken in: in line 20 the 17 carries an overbar on the image, which makes it the comte de Soissons (ayant envoyé devant [comte de Soissons]), where Beling has the number 17. That is a single look and needs a second.

No. 60. Five lines: Ce que je vous ay escript du s[ieu]r Ludouic est vray, and someone dira ce qu’il en est aussi de Launan. The key gives Launan; the decipherer of the time wrote a dotted y over the last figure, Launay. At the verification the words before that run looked like au s[ieu]r, which would give au s[ieu]r de Launay; that too is one look and is not adopted. Neither Ludouic nor Launay is identified (four web searches by Beling, a weak negative).

How much reads. By the count used for no. 25, in which doubtful words, supplied words and bracketed expansions of abbreviations are all holes, no. 24 stands at 0.957 (761 words, 34 holes) and no. 60 at 0.938 (45 words, 3 holes). Leaving the expansions out, the verifier counted about 0.98 and 0.956. With no. 25 the three letters stand at 0.940 (1,698 words, 109 holes). Nos. 24 and 60 have one reader each, and the English translations in the full texts were written here from Beling’s French.

05 What stays open

  • In no. 25, the end of the Soissons run: du M. de La Noue (Brackx) or du [Roy] (Beling, and the verifier’s look). Brackx is to be asked.
  • In no. 25, doubtful clear words (poix and doigt, Lamour, balliages de flandre, the besieged town in Picardy; ten words are doubtful for both readers) and the isolated figure 3, which has no value.
  • In no. 24, 15 doubtful places (among them Sire, soufflement, Chauny, Roissy, Esperon), an unread passage in line 48, and the barred 17 of line 20; in no. 60, two doubtful words. A second reader of the clear text is the next step.
  • Who Ludouic and Launay were.
  • The “Mots” and “Dames” blocks of the key sheet are not transcribed; no run of these letters uses them.
  • No. 78 (f. 111r, 1591): one group, “traictast de 96 □ 3 9̄ f 3”, too short to decrypt, in another code; Laurière’s key no. 57 gives no sense on it.
  • On 3 October 2026 key no. 57 and its word list were tried on the two-digit runs and give no sense (a different system), and the five letter values still unknown were bracketed by the alphabetical order and searched exhaustively with a French model (240 combinations). The best (52 = i, 62 = n, 92 = u/v, 95 = y) gives “de Launan” and “…audovic”, but no value is confirmed by a second occurrence, so none is counted. The code numbers are words or names that no model can fill. (Nevers key no. 25 later showed the plain 12, 19, 89 and 99 to be nulls and the barred 39 and 17 to be names; see section 03.)
  • Before the key was found (to 23 October 2026): 63 of 104 figures of nos. 24, 25 and 60 had been deciphered from the glosses and the alphabetical order.
