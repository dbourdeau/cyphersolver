Unknown sender → unknown recipient · alphabet of invented symbols · 1728

A cipher page of 1728 in invented symbols, with a codebreaker’s worksheets

British Library, Add MS 32287 ff. 37–40 (DECODE R7707). One page of 205 cursive symbols, filed with a codebreaker’s frequency tables and a docket dated 4 August 1728. Neither he nor this attempt recovered the key.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 22 September 2026 · updated 26 October 2026

Method: not solved · Extent: text not obtained

At a glanceNot solved · text not obtained

The document
One cipher page, f. 37r: ten lines of invented cursive signs, 205 in all, each followed by a dot. The other leaves are a codebreaker’s worksheets, a household bill and a 1728 docket.
Where
British Library, Add MS 32287 ff. 37–40. DECODE R7707 listed it as a ciphered letter between unknown correspondents, dated by the volume span 1716–1836.
Why it resists
The index of coincidence is 1.07, which is flat and rules out a simple substitution in any language. Two hundred symbols are too few to recover a polyalphabetic key alone.
How it was tried
The signs were transcribed and the counts agree with the codebreaker’s tally on f. 40r. Monoalphabetic annealing in seven languages failed, as did Vigenère and Beaufort at periods 5 and 10 and a Quagmire-type search.
Still open
The text was not deciphered. Only outside material would help: the key, or the other letters named on the docket (31 August and 5 October 1728).

01 The documents

  • f. 37r: the cipher, ten lines, 205 signs, no word division. About 27 distinct signs.
  • f. 38r–v: a first tally, with some signs struck out; a list headed “Duplicates”; tables of the signs before and after the commonest sign and after l.
  • f. 39r: an unrelated household account in English (bread, flour, milk, meat, sugar: 18s 11d).
  • f. 39v: the codebreaker’s guesses: l a vowel, e; three other signs consonants.
  • f. 40r: a fair count of 26 signs, with a second “Duplicates” list.
  • f. 40v: docket S.n 4 Aout 1728 / L.s 31 Aout / L.s 5 8bre. It is in French and lists three letters.

02 The cipher

The transcription (ct.txt) uses arbitrary labels for the signs. Its counts match the 1728 tally within a few units for every sign: the commonest sign has 14 against his 14, and l has 13 against his 16. The “Duplicates” are simply the signs that occur doubled in the text.

The index of coincidence is 1.07, where 1.0 is random. A monoalphabetic substitution of French or English would give 1.7–2.0. There is no repeated trigram in 205 signs. The periodic index rises slightly at periods 5, 10 and 15 (1.25, 1.34, 1.29). That hints at a five-alphabet system, but columns of about 41 signs are too short to prove it.

03 What was tried

  • Simple substitution by simulated annealing with the lang/ 4-gram models: English, French, German, Dutch, Italian, Spanish, Latin. The best was about −5.1 log-probability per letter; real text scores about −2.5. There are no words.
  • Vigenère, Beaufort and variant Beaufort at periods 5 and 10. These took the order of the 1728 tally as the symbol alphabet. None gives plaintext.
  • An unknown symbol alphabet combined with periodic shifts (Quagmire type), periods 5 and 10, French and English. None gives plaintext.

04 What is needed

Only outside material. That could be the key, or the other letters on the docket (31 August and 5 October 1728). More text in the same system would make a periodic attack feasible. The other DECODE records from Add MS 32287 (R7746–R7751) are Swedish keys of the 1830s, not this alphabet.
