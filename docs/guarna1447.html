Nicolò Guarna to Francesco Sforza, Milan, 21 May 1447

Sforza’s envoy reports an audience with the Duke of Milan on money, the plague and Sforza’s coming; the key was rebuilt from another Guarna letter and about 72% of the cipher words are read, so it is read in part.

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · partial (72% of the cipher)

The letter
Nicolò Guarna, writing from Milan as Count Francesco Sforza’s envoy at the ducal court, to Sforza, on Sunday 21 May 1447. It is signed “Servitor N. Guarna”. The opening, the connectives and the close are in clear; most of the body, about 3,960 cipher signs over three pages, is in cipher.
Where
Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, italien 1584 (the Archivio Sforzesco, 1447), ff. 153r–154r, on Gallica (views 146–147). No DECODE record and no edition found. The letter is not in the catalogue; the lead for Guarna’s other letters in this volume (ff. 163–164 and others) does not list ff. 153–154.
The cipher
Homophonic substitution with nulls (79 distinct signs), clear decoy words written inside the cipher (quia, adviso, mo, primum …) and a small code (cer = Signore, mea = non, tuy = gente d’arme, tri = Re de Ragona, dn = dinari). Several signs carry more than one value in our table, which may partly reflect our own confusion of look-alike signs.
How it was read
Brice Dudout rebuilt the key from Guarna’s letter of 27 May 1447 (ff. 163r–163v), aligned with its 18th-century clear copy (ff. 161r–162v, 165r), and applied it to this letter, which has no copy. The tests do not validate it: both held-out measurements are partial, and there is no fresh held-out block left.
What it says
An audience with Duke Filippo Maria Visconti about Sforza’s coming to Lombardy: enemies in the field, troops poorly provided, the plague, nine thousand ducats and then “not another florin for a long while”. The Duke answered “more openly than the other times” and shows a fervent wish for Sforza to come quickly.
Still open
About 236 words are unresolved (an estimate: each unread stretch counted as its length divided by five), mostly on f. 153r and the first half of f. 153v. 72.3% is below the 95% read bar. The key is partial, and the end of the letter (f. 153v from about l. 46 to f. 154r) was already in clear on f. 156, so it is not new.
Credit
Brice Dudout (Europe chiffrée), working with Claude Opus 5.5 agents: transcription, key, tests and reading, contributed by pull request #280. The clear text of f. 156 is the contemporary reading of the end. The training pair came from Daniel Bourdeau’s survey of the late copies in this volume.

01 The letter

Guarna writes to the Count from the court of Duke Filippo Maria Visconti, the last year of the Duke’s life, when Sforza, his son-in-law and condottiere, was far off in the Marche and the Duchy was short of money and men. The letter has about fifty lines on f. 153r, fifty-two on f. 153v and three on f. 154r. The salutation, many connectives and the close are in clear; the substance is in cipher, with clear decoy words scattered through it.

The letter belongs to the Sforza archive volume that also holds the Duke’s own letters to Sforza and those of Barbavara and Pusterla, each in a different cipher; the Duke’s second key and the Barbavara and Pusterla letters have their own pages, and nothing here is read with them. f. 156, a 15th-century clear text, gives the end of a letter of the same day, which matches the end of this cipher.

02 The cipher

The signs are letter-like and geometric, written without word division. Most stand for a single letter, and several letters have more than one sign (homophones). Some signs are nulls, and a few clear words (quia, adviso, tanto, tame, mo, cio, oto, si, ot, primum, tua, spes) are written inside the runs and dropped when reading. A short code gives whole words: A = Papa, cer = Signore, mea = non, con = qua, tus = que, tuy = gente d’arme, ba = per, ra = perché, qt = quantunque, tri = Re de Ragona, SF = Signore de Faenza, dn = dinari.

In the table as we have it, many signs list two or three values, most frequent first. That may be the cipher’s own polyphony or the contributor’s confusion of look-alike signs on a microfilm (a four-like sign and hk, a sigma and an o with a hook, t, T and z, hs and h). A blind shape sheet on those pairs was prepared and is not yet answered, so it was not applied. The key is published as targets/guarna1447/key.tsv (CC BY 4.0).

03 The key and its tests

Where the key comes from. No Guarna key was known: DECODE lists none among the keys examined, and nothing in print was found. Brice Dudout rebuilt it from the cipher of Guarna’s letter of 27 May 1447 (ff. 163r–163v), aligned with its late clear copy (ff. 161r–162v and 165r), the pair listed in the survey of late copies of this volume. The key was frozen in three stages (K, K2, K3, each fixed by SHA-256 before its test) and applied to this letter.

The tests. The thresholds were fixed in advance: validated at 0.60 (words of five letters or more in Italian) or 0.85 (characters against a clear), and at least 0.30 above the best of 200 shuffled keys; partial when the gap holds without the absolute figure.

What that means. The key is partial by our protocol, twice, and not validated. Both held-out blocks are now used, so no fresh block is left to test it on. Declared slips: line 20 of the first block (clear only) was seen before the block was frozen, and the copy f. 165r was seen at low resolution while the second block was chosen, though it was not used to build the key. The end of the cipher agrees word for word with the clear text of f. 156, which the key never used, but that is not a pre-registered test.

04 The reading

The reading covers the cipher segments only (the clear frame of the letter is not counted): 852 words, of which 583 are certain and 33 probable, none restored and 236 unresolved. Certain and probable make 616 words, 72.3%. The unresolved count is an estimate, because a stretch that does not read cannot be cut into words: each is counted as its decoded length divided by five. By part: the held-out block 71%, the rest of f. 153r 71%, f. 153v 73%, f. 154r 91%. That is below the 95% bar, so the letter is read in part. The reader is one AI agent working on a microfilm, and no palaeographer has checked the signs.

The sentences are built from stretches that construe as 15th-century Lombard Italian (de li quali, per respecto del spaccio, senza comparatione) with many holes, most of them on f. 153r and the early lines of f. 153v. The part where the reading is steadiest is the end, f. 153v from about line 46 to f. 154r, and that part is also the part that was already in clear on f. 156. The passage below is the end of the letter in the normalised text (a bare word is certain, {x} probable, [?n] about n unresolved words).

ne {recevessimo} [?1] per le genti de Cremona restano [?1] milia li quali spero havere fra otto o diece di o non prima … ma per {dirve} quello che io credo pareme esser certo voi non haveriti piu uno fiorino per uno grande spacio se la S.V. aspecta de moverse. … vole bene voria che facesti ultimum de potentia de venire presto et Dio volese che potesti farla
of which we received […] for the troops of Cremona remain […] thousand, which I hope to have within eight or ten days, not before … but to tell you what I believe, I seem certain you will not have another florin for a long while, if Your Lordship waits to move … [one who] wishes you well would want you to do your utmost to come quickly, and God grant you may do it.
  • The danger of the state. Guarna reports that the enemies are in the countryside and that the state stands in great danger; the plague adds to it. What he wants from Sforza is not clear from the opening lines, which are mostly holes: they speak of remedying “these cursed troubles”, of how Guarna stands in the world on account of the dispatch, and of what was hoped from Sforza.
  • Money. The main thing to remedy is to find money. Besides nine thousand ducats there is what is expected from the gabelle, and money for the troops at Cremona, to be had in eight or ten days; after that, not another florin for a long while.
  • The Duke. He answered Guarna “more openly than he had done the other times” and shows a fervent wish for Sforza’s coming, though in effect he has no money to give. Sforza should come quickly, with all or part of his company.

The gist (an audience with the Duke, money short, the Duke wanting Sforza to come) holds across the parts that read; the details of the first half are not secure.

05 What remains uncertain

  • About 236 unresolved words (estimated), mostly on f. 153r and f. 153v ll. 4–19. Blocker: illegible; the look-alike signs are not separated on the microfilm. The next step is the pending blind shape sheet and a finer re-transcription.
  • The key is not validated (section 03), and there is no fresh held-out block. Another Guarna leaf with a complete late copy (the lead for Guarna’s letters in this volume) would serve as a new test.
  • Not new at the end. f. 153v from about line 46 to f. 154r agrees with the 15th-century clear of f. 156, so the contribution of the reading is the rest, where no decipherment is known.
  • Multi-valued signs. Most signs list more than one value in the key. In the reveal above the orange signs are the ones where the reading takes a value other than the first, or corrects one from the sense.
  • One reader. An AI agent transcribed and read; no palaeographer checked the signs.
  • Not seen. Cerioni, La diplomazia sforzesca (1970), and the printed calendars of BnF italien 1584. Four web searches (the last on 5 November 2026) found no edition of this letter; if one prints it, we will credit it.

# guarna1447 - reading of Nicolò Guarna to Francesco Sforza, Milan, 21 May 1447 (BnF ital. 1584, ff. 153r-154r)

Read in part (5 Nov 2026). Counted by script on the lines below (cipher segments only; the clear frame of the letter is not counted): **852 words: 583 certain, 33 probable, 0 restored, 236 unresolved** (the unresolved count is an estimate, see below), so 616 = **72.3%** certain or probable. Under 95%: read in part. Our working notes gave 862 words and 72.6%; the recount by script on this file, counting each word split at a line end once, gives the figures above.

Conventions (spelling of the cipher kept; 15th-century Lombard Italian):
- a bare word is **certain** (given by key K3, clear sense);
- `{word}` is **probable** (one doubtful sign, or one of the sign's other values);
- `<word>` would be **restored** (none here);
- `[?n]` is a stretch of **about n unresolved words**: n is estimated as the length of the decoded stretch divided by 5, because a stretch that does not read cannot be cut into words. The total of 236 unresolved words is therefore an estimate.

The end of the letter (f. 153v from about V47b, and f. 154r) agrees word for word with the 15th-century clear text on f. 156 of the same day ("le gente de Cremona restano … fra otto o diece di, o no prima … piu uno fiorino per uno gran spacio … ultimum de potentia de venire presto"). That part is therefore **not new**: it was in clear in the volume. The rest (f. 153r and f. 153v to about l. 46) has no known decipherment.
