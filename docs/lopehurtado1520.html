Lope Hurtado de Mendoza to Charles V, Tordesillas, 10 December 1520

Written five days after the royal army retook Tordesillas from the Comuneros, a letter wholly in cipher whose key Brice Dudout rebuilt from the letter itself; about 98% of its 920 words give sense.

Method: key recovered from ciphertext-only · Extent: complete

The letter
Lope Hurtado de Mendoza, a gentleman of Charles V’s household sent to the governors of Castile, to Charles V, then at Worms, from Tordesillas, 10 December 1520. Two pages wholly in cipher, sent with a clear letter of the same day.
Where
Archivo General de Simancas, Patronato Real, PTR,LEG,2,36,8, ff. 1r–1v (PARES 12988891). The clear letter is 36,6, printed by Danvila in the 1890s. Catalogue item 626 here.
The cipher
A homophonic substitution of about 37 signs with the words kept apart, some signs standing for two or three letters, dotted sigla for persons, and a last paragraph that mixes cipher with words in clear letters.
How the key was found
An archivist’s slip points to the key in a letter of 23 September 1520; it does not fit. Brice Dudout rebuilt the key from this ciphertext alone and tested it on lines transcribed after the key was fixed: 0.827 of their words are Spanish, against at most 0.596 for shuffled keys.
What it says
The ·fre·, probably the Admiral of Castile, wants a settlement with the Junta at any price; the Marquis of Denia is hated; the army is “muy mal mandada”; and the see of Jaén and every vacancy should be kept unfilled so that the lords go on hoping.

01 The two letters

In the autumn of 1520 the Revolt of the Comunidades held much of Castile. The Junta of the rebel cities sat at Tordesillas, where Queen Joanna lived, and wanted her to reign in place of her absent son. On 5 December the governors’ army, under the Count of Haro, took the town back; it was sacked and several of the Junta’s procurators were captured. Charles V was in Germany, at Worms for his first Diet.

Lope Hurtado de Mendoza, a gentleman of Charles’s household, had been sent to the governors as the Emperor’s confidant. On 10 December he wrote twice from Tordesillas. One letter, in clear (PTR,LEG,2,36,6), tells of the sack and lists the prisoners; Antonio Danvila printed it in the 1890s in his documentary history of the Comunidades. The other, 36,8, is entirely in cipher, and says what could not be written openly about the governors and the grandees. Its dorse reads “Carta de Lope Hurtado de x de dez.”. It fills both sides of one leaf, 44 lines on the recto and 42 on the verso; the two pages can be seen on PARES.

Hurtado’s later letters from Rome, of 1522 to 1524, use a different cipher, a code of three-letter groups with an alphabet of cursive signs, and have their own pages: Rome, 1522 and Rome, 1523–24.

02 The cipher

A substitution of single letters by about 37 signs: Latin letters, figures and invented shapes, among them a barred o for t, a cross for s, a 9 for a, a long ſ for g and a ⊤ for z. The words are kept apart, as in the clear hand. Vowels and a few consonants have more than one sign; the encipherer used them unevenly. Some signs stand for two or three letters: one gives a, l or h, one i, j or y, another e, h or p, so the reading of those signs is settled by the word.

Persons are written as dotted sigla, ·fre·, ·lis·, ·los·, ·lus·, ·ym·, short groups between points whose meaning is not in the letter. The last lines of the verso mix cipher with words in ordinary letters (“fynes”, “lo primero”, “vra altza”, “el bien del rreyno”, “creo q no”), and cipher and clear read on as one text.

03 How the key was found

The key that was supposed to fit. A slip by the archivist J. Paz in the bundle says the key is to be found in Hurtado’s letter of 23 September 1520 (PTR,LEG,2,36,2), which Danvila printed deciphered. Its values are incompatible with this letter, so the key had to be recovered from the ciphertext.

Breaking in. The signs were transcribed from the PARES images in the working notation. The repeated groups that had to be vuestra magestad opened a homophonic letter substitution. A first key, K1, was built from lines 1–16 of the recto only and frozen, with a SHA-256 hash recorded.

Held-out lines. Two stretches of the verso were kept out of the work. Lines 1–11, transcribed after K1 was frozen, gave 0.747 of their words in a Spanish lexicon under K1, against 0.326 at the 99th percentile of 200 control keys with the values permuted. Lines 11–17 were transcribed and frozen before any decoding and used to fix no value; the later keys K2 and K4 both read 0.827 of their words, against a median of 0.288, a 99th percentile of 0.519 and a maximum of 0.596 for the controls.

The full reading. The rest was deciphered word by word, deciding between near-identical signs against a Spanish lexicon (the Quijote and Danvila’s documents). K3 became K4 when one value, ſ as s, was withdrawn after Dudout’s own look at twelve doubtful signs on a sheet of sign shapes, made without any reading offered; that look also settled holgado, cardenal, necesidad and oficio.

The statistical check here. The project’s validator (docs/_validate.py) scores the reading of 3,432 signs (35 sign types) with a four-gram model of Golden Age Spanish, as mean log-probability per letter: the reading gives −2.139, random keys of the same shape −5.439 on average, and a key annealed onto the shuffled ciphertext at best −3.118. Verdict: pass.

04 The decipherment

Brice Dudout’s text, spelling of the letter kept, punctuation and accents added. {x} probable reading; ‹…› unresolved; [x] restored from the sense; ·x· a dotted siglum, not identified.

The ·fre· and the Queen

El ·fre· está ganoso de asiento con los de la Junta como quiera que fuese; dize que lo haze por el mal aparejo que {ay} de sostener la gente que aquí está. Hizo a la reina que, delante del ·y· de muchos cavalleros, dixiese que mandava que toda la gente de la Junta se derramase, e mandólo tomar por testimonio para enviar a requerir con él a don Pedro Girón ·y· a las ciudades. […] E aun estando así, dixo que a la reina tenía por cuerda.
The ·fre· wants a settlement with the men of the Junta at any price; he says he does so because of the poor means {there are} of maintaining the troops here. He had the Queen say, before the ·y· and many knights, that she ordered all the Junta’s troops to disperse, and had this recorded as testimony to summon don Pedro Girón ·y· the cities with it. […] And even so, he said that he held the Queen to be of sound mind.

The Marquis of Denia

El marqués de Denia viene aquí con más pasión de la que era menester […]; está muy mal quisto a muchos: les ha pesado tanto de su venida como de averlo saqueado. […] Dizen que trae determinación de revolver todo.
The Marquis of Denia comes here with more passion than was needed; he is much disliked by many: his coming has grieved them as much as having sacked the town. […] They say he comes resolved to overturn everything.

The vacant sees

El obispado de Jaén e otra qualquier vacante que oviere deve vuestra alteza detener […], porque acá an muchos que esperan que vuestra magestad les hará merced, y esta esperança los obliga a servir; quando se dé a uno, se estragará la voluntad de los otros. El Rey Católico, quando estuvo en Nápoles, tuvo muchos obispados y encomiendas y tenencias sin proveer hasta su venida.
The see of Jaén, and any other vacancy that may occur, Your Highness should hold back […], for there are many here who hope Your Majesty will grant them favour, and this hope binds them to serve; when it is given to one, the goodwill of the others will be spoiled. The Catholic King, when he was in Naples, kept many sees, commanderies and keeperships unfilled until his return.

The army

Como ay aquí muchos señores, son mal de concertar para lo que es menester, especial ·fre· e el conde de Benavente […]; e deste e{x}ército y la gente muy mal mandada, como es de muchos, que es el mayor temor que tenemos.
Since there are many lords here, they agree badly on what must be done, especially ·fre· and the Count of Benavente […]; and in this army the troops are very badly commanded, as any force with many heads: that is the greatest of our fears.

The letter ends with the grandees’ “tres fynes”, three aims, written half in clear letters: first what suits them, then standing with the Emperor, then the good of the kingdom, provided they are not left in need. The complete Spanish text, the translation, the transcription and the line-by-line decryption are under Full texts below.

05 Who is who

  • The ·fre· negotiates with the Junta and hesitates to accept “la governación”. Dudout takes him to be the Admiral of Castile, Fadrique Enríquez, named co-governor in September 1520 and known to favour a settlement with the Comuneros; fre would abbreviate his first name. That is a hypothesis: nothing in the letter proves it.
  • The other sigla (·lis·, ·los·, ·lus·, ·ym·, ·y·) are not identified. ·lus· and ·los· seem to refer to the Queen and her household at Tordesillas.
  • The Queen “por cuerda”. Whether Joanna was of sound mind was the political stake of the Junta, which wanted her to reign. That the ·fre· called her sane was, for Hurtado, a sign that he might give way on the agreements.
  • The Marquis of Denia, Bernardo de Sandoval y Rojas, governor of the Queen’s household, had been expelled by the Comuneros in September and came back after the town was taken. “Having sent away the women” probably refers to the Queen’s attendants.
  • The Naples precedent. Ferdinand the Catholic, in Naples in 1506–07, left sees, commanderies and keeperships unfilled until his return; Hurtado recommends the same to keep hopes of favour alive.
  • The lords named in the clear: the Count of Benavente, the Marquis of Astorga, the Count of Miranda, the comendador mayor, don Pedro Girón (the Comuneros’ captain) and the Count of Haro, “who holds the office of captain”. The Cardinal and the Constable of the later proposals are presumably the two other governors, Adrian of Utrecht and Íñigo Fernández de Velasco, whose seat was Burgos.

06 What remains uncertain

Of about 920 cipher words (each unresolved passage counted as two), 95.4% are certain, 2.3% are probable readings marked {x}, and 2.3% are unresolved. By the project’s grades, the certain words rest on the recovered key and the held-out check, the probable ones are M (uncertain) and the restorations [x] are I (inferred from context).

  • Recto l. 26, “porque de ‹…› dezía”: the third sign, a circle with a downward stroke from its middle on Dudout’s re-reading, matches nothing in the table.
  • Isolated words on the recto, ll. 11, 21, 36, 42, and on the verso, ll. 2, 21, 25, 31, 37, 38; the end of a verso line first missed in transcription and then restored, “no se acertara, por‹…›”.
  • The siglum ‹·tar·’, and every siglum but ·fre· (above).
  • Weak sentences, where the words are deciphered but the sense is strained: “e no y ase cada día mill vezes” (recto l. 12), “{segund él} tiene” (recto l. 32), and “Pues pero está agora todo, que estava entonces” (verso l. 8). The cipher there has pero; a slip for peor, “everything is worse now than it was then”, would give the clearest sense before “más necesidad tiene vuestra magestad”. That is a suggestion made here, not part of the contributed text.
  • Slips of the encipherer: on the recto “que lo solían hazer porque dizen” is written twice (end of l. 36, start of l. 37) and translated once; one word on the verso is struck through.

No earlier decipherment was found. Danvila printed the letter of 23 September (deciphered) and the clear letter of 10 December, not this one; PARES and a web search on 27 October 2026 gave nothing, and the item was open in this catalogue.

Lope Hurtado de Mendoza to Charles V, Tordesillas, 10 December 1520 (AGS PTR,LEG,2,36,8)

Five days after Tordesillas was retaken from the Comuneros, Lope Hurtado de Mendoza, Charles V’s confidant with the governors of Castile, wrote two letters on the same day. One, in plain text, tells of the sack of the town and the capture of the Junta’s procurators; Danvila printed it in the late nineteenth century. The other, entirely in cipher, says what could not be written openly: the Admiral hesitates to govern, the Marquis of Denia is hated, the army is “very badly commanded”, and the see of Jaén and every vacancy should be held back to keep loyalties. An archivist’s slip in the bundle said the key was to be found in a letter of 23 September; it was not. We rebuilt it from the letter itself.

On this point let Your Majesty send the post at once, for it is needed.

The Marquis of Denia comes here with more passion than was needed, {as he} has; he is much disliked by many: his coming has grieved them as much as having sacked the town. Your Highness should order him to restrain himself greatly, to work with good will to satisfy the servants of ·los· and to serve the lady ·lus· and the ‹…›, better than was done before, for they say she was kept discontented, and that now his coming has grieved her; and yet ·los· has not been pleased with him. And since she is content ·hs· to have sent away the women, let him not think of making changes before Your Highness comes, but keep things as they are; for they say he comes resolved to overturn everything, and given the ‹…› they have and the ill will with which they are received, I believe what he would do would not be good.

The see of Jaén, and any other vacancy that may occur, Your Highness should hold back and not fill, for there are many here who hope Your Majesty will grant them favour, and this hope binds them to serve; when it is given to one, the goodwill of the others will be spoiled. The Catholic King, when he was in Naples, kept many sees, commanderies and keeperships unfilled until his return. But as everything stands now, as it stood then, Your Majesty has even more need to do ‹…› what you {can} for those who wish to serve Your Highness.

The ·fre· is now about to accept the government, on condition, he says, of holding it {until} a courier is sent to Your Majesty for you to send him the powers he asks for; I would wish ·ni· that he accepted it ·ni· that Your Majesty sent him the powers, for he lacks the steadiness for such a charge: every day he changes his mind about a thousand things, ·y· yet he means to do well by the kingdom. If he accepts, let Your Majesty provide as is fitting.

The Count of Haro holds the office of captain, as in everything else. Since there are many lords here, they agree badly on what must be done, especially ·fre· and the Count of Benavente, who have ‹…›, whence very great disorder; and in this army the troops are very badly commanded, as any force with many heads: that is the greatest of our fears. And at every hour it is thought great luck to take this ‹…›; for, the way things go, I believe everything will be {lost} if this is not got right. ‹…›

Some think it would be good for the Cardinal and some members of the Council and the contadores to come here, and justice and finance be administered from here; and for the Constable to see to Burgos and those mountains, and others to this place, ‹…› provided and with the troops needed to hold it; and for the governors, Council and contadores to meet at Burgos. There is a great difficulty in this: the presence of the Marquis of Denia, so disliked here by the servants of ·lis· and by all the {people}; and if [it] is given to another, it would be an affront to him. Let Your Highness have all this examined and order provision for it, as is most to your ‹…› service.

These grandees, if they ‹…›, have three aims: first, what suits them; then, standing with Your Highness; and then the good of the realm, provided they are not left in need. I do not think they would mind Your Majesty keeping them [in your service].
