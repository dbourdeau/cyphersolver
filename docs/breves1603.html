Henri IV to Savary de Brèves, ambassador at Constantinople, 1603

Go to the full text ↓

Bibliothèque nationale de France, Français 3541 (anc. Béthune 9021) · twelve royal letters “avec chiffre”, ff. 60–104 · the year of the Celali revolt, the spahi rising at the Porte and Mehmed III’s death

Desenclos (2018) lists these letters with no known decipherment; Tomokiyo published a partial decipherment from a key he rebuilt from Brèves’s own letters. The text here is Berger de Xivrey’s 1853 print of a contemporary copy in clear, and the original key survives in BnF fr. 3462. The cipher leaves are not online, so the letters have not been decrypted with the key.

01 The letters

François Savary de Brèves was Henri IV’s ambassador at the Porte from 1591 to 1605. In 1604 he negotiated the renewal of the Capitulations. The BnF notice for fr. 3541 lists the King’s letters to him, several marked “avec chiffre”. For 1603 there are twelve. Berger de Xivrey’s source lines give the folios.

The letter usually cited as f. 63 of “1 March” is the Metz letter of 31 March, le dernier jour de mars. The same volume also has a fully ciphered letter of 5 January 1610 (f. 8) and a Puisieux letter with cipher (f. 39). Both date from Brèves’s later embassy in Rome and probably use another key. They were not examined.

02 The key

Tomokiyo rebuilt this cipher from Brèves’s letters to the King in BnF fr. 16144, and then noticed that the original is in fr. 3462, a volume of keys and jargons. The leaf is f. 103 (Gallica canvas 109). The next leaf, f. 104, is not the same key. It is the “Chiffre reformé pour Levant”, sent to Brèves in April 1604, after these letters.

The table has five parts:

  • An alphabet of homophones, figures and signs.
  • A nomenclator in initial-letter blocks, numbered 10–99 for words in a–g (aussi 11, avec 12, capitullations 57, Constantinople 59, corsaires 61, faire 92, fault 93, guerre 98), with signs for the rest (le, que, qui, pour, vous, Turcs, galleres, vaisseaux, la mer).
  • A column of persons, each a single sign: the Pope, the King, the Emperor, the kings of Spain, Poland, Persia, Fez and Tartary, the Grand Seigneur, the Sultana his mother, the grand bassa, the viziers, the general of the sea, the aga of the janissaries, the beglerbey, the nissangi, the tesquitar and the spahis.
  • Doubles, the figures 13, 49, 57, 30, 97, 38 and 70 marked with a caret.
  • Two lists of nulls.

The figures agree with the values Tomokiyo recovered from the ciphertext (aussi 11, avec 12, faire 92, fault 93, a = 7/3/5), which confirms that this is the key of the letters. A transcription is in targets/breves1603/key.md. Its numeric entries are firm. Its names for the signs are provisional until they can be checked against the ciphertext.

03 The text in print

Berger de Xivrey’s sixth volume of the Recueil des lettres missives de Henri IV (1853) cites each of these letters as “Orig. — B. N. Fonds Béthune, Ms. 9021, fol. …”, with a second source: “Cop. — Biblioth. de M. Monmerqué, Ms. intitulé Lettres à l’ambassadeur du Levant”. That copy was in clear, and Berger printed from it. He gives the February, March and April letters whole. He lists the other nine in the table of unprinted letters at the end of the volume, each marked “(En chiffres)” and summarised. The Monmerqué manuscript has not been traced. It was probably dispersed with his library.

The OCR of the 17 April letter garbles exactly those passages Berger seems to have set in italic, which may be how he marked what was in cipher. This is not checked against the printed page.

05 What the letters say

Berger’s text of 18 February, from “que les desordres”, the stretch Tomokiyo’s overlay covers

… les desordres de l’empire de ce Seigneur vont continuant et augmentant, à sa honte et au grand prejudice d’iceluy, et ay bien consideré et pris le conseil que vous me donnés sur cela, duquel je me serviray le mieux qu’il me sera possible; mais la juste jalousie que j’ay de mes voisins ne me permet de faire pour ce regard tout ce que je desirerois et seroit necessaire. Je ne me puis persuader que cest empire soit pour tomber ou faire naufrage si tost que vous l’apprehendés, joinct que j’ay l’opinion, s’il y avoit du changement, que ce sera sur la personne du prince plustost qu’en autre chose; car les ennemys du dict empire ne sont mieux conseillez et servys que luy, et ont des incommoditez qui les combattent, qui ne sont de petite importance; mais ils doivent plustost craindre les armes du revolteur d’Asie que celles des autres.

February. Brèves had written that the Ottoman empire might collapse. The King does not believe it: if anything changes, it will be the person of the sultan, not the empire. Its Christian enemies are no better led than it is, and the real danger is the rebel in Asia, the Celali rising. France is at peace. Bouillon has had to withdraw to Germany, finding no Protestant willing to rise for him. The King is leaving for Metz, where war threatens between the Protestants and Catholics of Germany over the bishopric of Strasbourg, disputed between Brandenburg and Lorraine.

March, from Metz. He wants to know how the remuement at the Porte has ended, “car il en succedera un grand bien ou un grand mal” to the sultan and his empire. The Emperor is recruiting Frenchmen: Nevers, wounded at the assault on Buda, has vowed to go back. The King will not be drawn into mediating a peace between the Emperor and the sultan. He has ordered his subjects in the Levant to sail armed against English pirates. He reports that Elizabeth is gravely ill.

April. The revolt of Mamoucte Bassa and the spahis against the sultan and his first vizier has been put down. The King regrets the fall of the mufti and his brother. Brèves is to congratulate the sultan, but not to ask for an Ottoman fleet, and to get out of the peace negotiation gently. The Emperor will not hear of peace, and the King of Spain is urging him against it so as to have a free hand at Algiers. Elizabeth is dead, and James has been proclaimed without opposition.

May to December, from Berger’s summaries only; the letters themselves are not in the repository:

  • the war in Hungary and Transylvania, with Moses Székely driving out Basta;
  • the King’s wish that the Frenchmen serving the sultan withdraw, quietly and in Brèves’s own name;
  • Spain’s enterprise on Algiers, taken up, dropped and taken up again;
  • a treaty with James I disowning his subjects’ piracy, and Marseille ships to be armed against Barbary and English corsairs;
  • the sultan’s chiaoux waiting at Marseille for a royal journey to Provence that did not happen that year;
  • in August, surprise at the execution of the sultan’s son on a mere suspicion;
  • in December, unease at “certain changement survenu à la Porte”, which the King fears favours the house of Austria.

Mehmed III died on 22 December 1603, the day before the last of these letters was written. The renewal of the Capitulations in May 1604 falls after the series.

06 What remains

  • The ciphertext. Fr. 3541 is not on Gallica: there are no records under its shelfmark or its old Béthune number, and the BnF notice has no link to a digital copy. Desenclos 2018 has no plates, and Tomokiyo does not say where his images came from. A reproduction of ff. 60–104 would let the nine summarised letters be decrypted in full with the key. The three printed letters give about two pages of known plaintext to settle the key’s signs first.
  • The full texts are at the BnF. Fr. 4029 (anc. 8962(3), olim Supplément français 500), a 17th-century “Recueil de copies de lettres”, holds 158 letters of Henri IV to Brèves from 1592 to 1608. Its nos. 131–142 are this series, from 18 February to 23 December 1603, one folio each on ff. 118–128. No. 130 is a letter of 6 January 1603 that has no ciphered original in fr. 3541. The volume is not on Gallica. Eleven folios of it would give the full text of the nine summarised letters, and a reproduction order for them is the cheapest way to finish this series. The Monmerqué copy that Berger used, Lettres à l’ambassadeur du Levant, is not needed. Guadet’s supplement (t. VIII–IX, 1872–76) does not print the nine in full.
  • Checking the key’s signs. Brèves’s own letters to the King in the same cipher are online. Fr. 15578 f. 185 (Gallica canvas 196) is fully ciphered, with the recipient’s decipherment written between the lines (“Je n’adjousteray a ma derniere … que la mort du grand …”). That makes it known plaintext for settling the provisional sign names in key.md before any images of fr. 3541 are obtained. It has not been transcribed yet.
  • A loose end in Berger. His table (p. 667) also lists a “dépêche entièrement chiffrée” to Brèves of 17 April, “fol. 60”, with no copy cited. It is either a second April dispatch or a misprint. Only the manuscript can say which.

# Henri IV to Brèves, Paris, 18 February 1603: English translation

To Monsieur de Brèves, gentleman in ordinary of my chamber and my ambassador in the Levant.

Monsieur de Brèves, since my last, written on the 4th of this month, I have received yours of the 3rd, 15th and 17th of
December, from which I have learned that the disorders of this Lord's [the Sultan's] empire go on continuing and
increasing, to his shame and to its great harm; and I have well considered and taken the advice you give me on this,
which I shall use as best I can; but the just jealousy I have of my neighbours does not allow me to do in this regard
all that I would wish and that would be necessary. I cannot persuade myself that this empire is about to fall or be
wrecked as soon as you fear, besides which I am of the opinion that, if there were a change, it would fall on the person
of the prince rather than on anything else; for the enemies of the said empire are no better advised and served than
he is, and they have difficulties fighting them that are of no small importance; but they should fear the arms of the
rebel of Asia more than those of the others. So I shall be glad to know what progress it makes, and you will know that
my kingdom is at present very peaceful. The Duke of Bouillon has been forced to withdraw to Germany, having found my
subjects of the Religion not disposed to follow his counsels and rise for him. I shall leave in two days to go to Metz,
to settle a certain dispute that troubles the peace of that town and to be ready on that frontier, where it seems that
matters are on the point of a war beginning between the Protestants and Catholics of Germany, founded on the dispute
over the bishopric of Strasbourg, which is contested between the houses of Brandenburg and Lorraine — the latter
favoured by the Emperor and the other by the said Protestants, arms being already ready on both sides. You will be
informed of what comes of it, as I also wish to be of what happens over there. I pray God, Monsieur de Brèves, to keep
you in His holy and worthy care. Written at Paris, the 18th day of February 1603.

HENRY. [DE NEUFVILLE.]

# Henri IV to Brèves, Metz, 31 March 1603: English translation

To Monsieur de Brèves, gentleman in ordinary of my chamber and my ambassador in the Levant.

Monsieur de Brèves, I greatly wish to know what the outcome has been of the commotion begun at this Lord's Porte, of
which you informed me by your letter of 3 January, which I received with that of 29 December on the 19th of this
month; for great good or great harm will come of it to the person of this Lord and to his empire, which, being attacked
and harried on all sides, must succumb if he suffers harm and his principal officers fall into disorder and take sides.
The Emperor is preparing to attack him more powerfully than ever, even to the point of enlisting my own subjects for
that purpose. Last year the Duke of Nevers was in his army, and was wounded at the assault on Buda by a great arquebus
shot that he received in the body, of which he nearly died. He now says that during his wound he vowed to God to go
back there if he recovered from the said shot, so that, being again solicited by the said Emperor, I foresee that it
will be difficult for me to prevent him from going back. Nevertheless I have resolved to do all I can in this regard.
The first time he was accompanied by only five or six gentlemen, as adventurers; but I have learned that the said
Emperor wishes to give him a command, so as to oblige him to go this time with a larger following; which will be cut
down on my part as far as possible. I am sorry for the disgrace of the Mufti and of his brother, given the confidence you
have in them, and I wish to hope that this change will soon bring about others which may perhaps be the cause of their
restoration; but know that I do not wish to be engaged in the negotiation of peace with the said Emperor, as they had
proposed to you, for I could not bring it to an end, and besides I would displease the Pope and others by undertaking
it. I also know that the said Emperor will have no wish to hear of it, so that it would be shameful for me and for this
said Lord to speak of it.

I shall have the Grand Duke spoken to about the prisoner, this Lord's porter, whom he recommended to me, and shall send
his answer. Meanwhile do not promise them his release, for the said Grand Duke might make difficulties about it. I will
not promise what I do not keep. I have ordered my subjects in those ports of the Levant to sail there armed, for
protection against the English pirates; but it will be necessary to resolve to summon this Lord to give me satisfaction
for it, because of the refuge they find in that country; and if he will not or cannot do so, my said subjects will
abstain from trading there. For the rest, I have finished in this town what I came here for, for I have changed its
governors as I wished, both for the good of my service and for the relief of the citizens, so that I shall leave within
two days to return to Paris. I have had word that the Queen of England has fallen gravely ill; nevertheless I hope she
will not die this time. I pray God, Monsieur de Brèves, to keep you in His holy care. Written at Metz, the last day of
March 1603.

HENRY.

Fontainebleau, 17 Apr 1603 (f. 66)

Monsieur de Brèves, I have learned, from your letter of the [?29th] of January and from those of the [?11th] and [?20th]

understand that I commanded you to do so, as one who wishes this prince to keep in his empire his authority and power,
as his predecessors of glorious memory did. But since no certain reliance is to be placed on their assistance and
promises, as you have written to me in your letters, refrain from soliciting and pressing for an armament at sea, and
from letting them see that I need their assistance, or that I expect [it] in any way, so that this does not oblige me to
engage myself with them; for I recognise well that there is neither gain nor honour in it. Nor commit me either to the
negotiation of peace with the Emperor; for I know for certain that the said Emperor will hear nothing of it, both
because of the advantages he has gained and hopes God will continue to give him hereafter against this Lord, and
because the King of Spain dissuades him from it, so as to facilitate the enterprise he has always had against Algiers,
to which he invites the kings of [?Kuku] and their like. Therefore withdraw gently from this negotiation, as I wrote to
you in my last, and continue to inform me of all occurrences.

You will know that the Queen of England having died on the [?3rd] of this month, the King of Scotland has been proclaimed
and declared her legitimate successor and king of the said kingdom and of that of Ireland, with the common consent of
all the English, without any opposition up to this hour, and he was expected in London with much devotion to be crowned
king; at which I have been very glad, for I have always had a particular and brotherly friendship with the said king,

HENRY.
