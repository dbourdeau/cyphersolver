Alonso Sánchez at Venice, 1522 — the cipher was already solved, and the catalogue entry was wrong

Real Academia de la Historia, Salazar 9/23–9/26 · DECODE R9593–R9657 · the imperial ambassador at Venice reporting to the Emperor through the Italian War of 1521–26

Catalogued here as twenty-nine unread ciphertexts. The cipher had been reconstructed and published by Satoshi Tomokiyo in September 2025 · what is added here is the correction of the entry, the ordering rule of the nomenclator, the numeral series, first readings of parts of R9635 and R9653, and the separation of a second correspondent’s cipher

Summary. The catalogue held this as a B-class target: twenty-nine ciphertexts of Alonso Sánchez, imperial ambassador at Venice, recorded in DECODE as Non-decrypted, with a note that Tomokiyo had reconstructed the cipher. The first thing to do was to find out what he had actually published. He had published the whole cipher — the substitution alphabet, a nomenclature of some 177 three-letter codes, and the deciphered opening lines of forty letters — on 6 September 2025, a year before this entry was scored. So there is no break to be had here, and this write-up says so. What is left is worth doing anyway, and four things came out of it. The catalogue entry is wrong in three ways: the run is spread over Salazar 9/23, 9/24, 9/25 and 9/26 rather than 9/24 alone; every record in it is dated 1522, so the negotiation ending in the treaty of July 1523 is not in this group at all; and nine of the records are not Sánchez but Lope Hurtado de Mendoza. Almost every record also carries a contemporary decipherment bound in with it, so these letters were never unread — they are unedited. The nomenclator turns out to be ordered: codes are consonant–vowel–consonant, and sorting them by first and third consonant puts the plaintext in alphabetical order, so an unassigned code is bracketed by its neighbours. That fills in the numeral run Tomokiyo left blank, and two of the values so predicted, cef = xxvi and cif = xxvii, are then confirmed in the text of R9653. Two letters outside Tomokiyo’s coverage are read in part here: R9653, a leaf mostly in clear on the siege of Rhodes with a ciphered paragraph at the foot reporting letters out of France; and R9635, a whole page of cipher on the sums owed by the Signoria and the restitution of seized goods, which attests a third predicted numeral, cab = xv. Finally, the Lope Hurtado letters use code groups with final p and z, which Sánchez’s key does not have — a third cipher of the same family, still unreconstructed. Status: found already solved, with the entry corrected and one letter read in part. The remaining twenty-seven records are transcription work, not cryptanalysis.

01What was already published, and when

Satoshi Tomokiyo, Correspondence in Cipher of Imperial Ambassadors Alonso Sanchez and Juan Manuel (1522), posted on Cryptiana on 6 September 2025 and enlarged on 9 September, reconstructs the cipher outright. He worked from the contemporary decipherment attached to DECODE R9605, and describes the moment it opened: browsing R9613 he noticed that vog and xig were frequent and so likely common monosyllables, and that a q with a double bar was often affixed to a code and might be the plural -s; he was then stuck on the handwriting of the decipherment. Turning to R9605 he saw vog veg near the end, two codes close together in an alphabetical listing and both frequent, and matched them to en el in the plaintext. After that, as he puts it, there was no real difficulty.

The article gives the substitution alphabet as a hand-drawn table, the nomenclature, and the deciphered first lines of R9593–R9632, plus the whole of R9621 and the last paragraph of R9623. It also reconstructs the parallel cipher of Juan Manuel, imperial ambassador at Rome (DECODE R9499–R9529), which is built to the same design with different values.

This is the answer to the contamination question the project records for every target: a full public reading of the system existed a year before the work, it was found before the attempt rather than after, and the reading below uses it.

02What the catalogue entry got wrong

The entry read “RAH, Signatura 9/24 (29 items)”, 1522–23, and justified its importance by the negotiation that brought Venice out of the French alliance and ended in the treaty of July 1523. Pulling the fifty-five DECODE record pages R9603–R9657 and tabulating them contradicts all three parts.

  • The shelfmark. The run is spread over Salazar 9/23, 9/24, 9/25 and 9/26. R9603–R9618 are in 9/24, R9619–R9632 in 9/25, R9633–R9657 in 9/26, and Tomokiyo’s list reaches back into 9/23.
  • The dates. Every record carries a 1522 date — the last of them November 1522. Nothing in this group reaches 1523, so the treaty of July 1523 is outside it. The letters belong to the year of La Bicocca, the loss of Genoa and the fall of Rhodes, not to the year of the Venetian realignment.
  • The authorship. Nine records are by Lope Hurtado de Mendoza, not Sánchez: R9634, R9644, R9645, R9646, R9648, R9649, R9650, R9652, R9656.

One further correction matters more than any of them. DECODE marks almost every record in the group Inline Cleartext: Yes, meaning a contemporary decipherment is bound with the ciphertext, and the Calendar of State Papers Spain, vol. 2, calendars many of these same letters with the note “Autograph in cipher. Contemporary deciphering”. These letters were never unread. What they lack is an edition. A status of Non-decrypted in DECODE records the state of the database, not the state of knowledge, and this entry read it as the latter.

03The cipher, and the rule that orders it

The system is the 1496 Ferdinand-and-Isabella type: a homophonic substitution alphabet, three nulls that letters open with, a q with a double bar for the plural -s, and a nomenclator of three-letter codes for common words, names and places. Word division is kept. The vocabulary is large for the date.

Setting Tomokiyo’s table out as data shows the codes are not arbitrary. They are consonant–vowel–consonant over

  • first position, sixteen consonants z y x v t s r p n m l h g f d c b, running backwards through the alphabet as the plaintext runs forwards;
  • middle, the five vowels a e i o u;
  • third position, nine consonants b c d f g h l m n.

That is 720 slots, of which about 177 were recovered. Sorting by (first consonant, third consonant, vowel) puts the plaintext in alphabetical order — zid zod zud | zef zif | zog zug | zel | zem zom | zen zun gives a(h)un, ahun, al, allende, algun, alla, alli, antes, aqui, aquell, arma, assi. The alphabetisation is the clerk’s own and is loose at the edges (allende before algun, cosa before concorda), so what the rule gives for an unassigned code is a range, not a point. Where the plaintext is a numeral series, the range collapses to a value.

Tomokiyo has cob xviii, cub xix, cad xx through cud xxiv, and caf xxv, and leaves the rest of the block blank, with a query on cud and an uncertain “[xxviii?]” in his reading of R9598. The ordering rule fills the run:

That in turn resolves the uncertain “[xxviii?]” of R9598 as cof. The predicted values are carried in key_codes.tsv flagged as this project’s, separately from Tomokiyo’s.

05R9653: the fall of Rhodes, in clear, with a ciphered postscript

R9653 is Sánchez to Charles V, Venice, 20 November 1522, Salazar 9/26 f. 299–301. It is beyond the end of Tomokiyo’s list and has not been read before. Folio 299r is a mixed leaf: six lines of cipher at the head, some thirty lines in clear, two lines of cipher at the foot, then the signature.

The clear middle, read from the image:

A xiij del presente fue la postrera que escreui a v[uest]ra m[agesta]d y dixe las nueuas de Rodas que aqui hauia dicho vn patron de vn nauio; despues [vinie]ron cartas y los d’esta republica me embiaron vn sumario, cuyo traslado sera con esta. Esta manyana han houido otras cartas y son de Candia de su general de xx del passado, y las tenia el de Rodas de x del mismo. … Lo que dizen es que hasta x de octubre hauian dado los turquos cinco conbates muy rezios a Rodas por diuersos lugares, y baterias, y por debaxo de tierra por las minas y por debaxo de la agua, y siempre los hauian rebotado y muerto gran numero dellos — dizen xx mil. Pero vn secretario de la Señoria me ha dicho a mi que despues que el turquo esta sobre Rodas se le han muerto cinquenta mil hombres en las batallas y de dolencia; su armada de mar estaua muy mal en horden, y algunos hauisos hai que la persona del turquo se ha retirado y passado en tierra firme. … Dizen que los de Rodas esperan socorro con desseo, y que si les llegasen mil infantes ternian por muy cierta la victoria. No hai duda sino que ahora hauria buena disposicion de hazer muy gran daño al turquo y a su armada.

Rhodes capitulated a month after this was written, on 22 December 1522.

The second line runs yeh+plural xig tom xig cef y cif xel mog vuc xig … — “cartas de Francia de xxvi y xxvii del passado de …”: letters out of France of 26 and 27 October, the month in which Francis I’s position in Lombardy collapsed. It is this pair of dates that confirms cef and cif. The first line gives ta es, vam embaxa(dor), vuc+plural, zid a(h)un, rug la, gub señor, vog en, around spelled-out letters not yet settled.

A small corroboration from the other end of the group: Lope Hurtado’s letter of 9 November (R9648) opens in clear “La carta de V. M[agestad] recebi de xxvj de octubre” — the same post that Sánchez reports reaching Venice.

06R9635: a full page of cipher, read in part

R9635 is Sánchez to Charles V, Venice, 16 September 1522, Salazar 9/26 f. 20–22, calendared in CSP Spain vol. 2 p. 482 and also beyond Tomokiyo’s list. Folio 20r is the best ciphertext page in the group: about twenty-five lines of cipher, whole-page, in a clean well-spaced hand, with a contemporary decipherment written between the lines in lighter ink — but only in patches, so most lines have to be read from the key.

The subject is money and reprisals: the sums owed by the Signoria under the capitulation — eighteen thousand ducats outstanding from a previous year, more than fifteen thousand in another reckoning — and, in the glossed lines, goods seized that were to be restored to their owners. That is the same business as the sequestrations of imperial vassals’ goods at Venice which fill R9638 (11 October 1522) entirely in clear, and the same reckoning of xx mil ducados allende de xviii mil that Tomokiyo read in the last paragraph of R9623.

Two points of notation, which the page settles: pol9 is pol (mi) followed by the letter sign for l, giving mil — that sign is easily taken for a g in this hand; and gɣ lal is the spelled letters r-e followed by lal (publica), giving republica, the word these despatches turn on.

This page also attests cab = xv, in mas de xv mil ducados — a third of the numerals predicted from the ordering rule, after cef and cif in R9653. None of the seven is contradicted.

07The letter alphabet, and the sign that was miscalled

Tomokiyo gives the substitution alphabet only as a hand-drawn table. Read off at high zoom it is about thirty signs over twenty-two letters (a b c d e f g h i l m n o p q r s t u x y z), most of them with two or three homophones: a = n, ʒf; d = ∞, 3, ɸ; g = bold ∞, L; n = ᶻ, ℞₄; and so on.

Two things fall out of it. The “q with a double bar” is the letter s — not a separate plural marker. The plural ending is simply the letter s written after a code group, so Tomokiyo’s guess that it “may well be the plural ending -s” is right for the wrong reason. And r is a different sign, a q with a loop, easily confused with it in this hand.

The map is carried in alpha.py and verifies on spelled runs taken from R9635 f. 20r: nx#gn = ahora, ttn#n = nada.

It does not yet give the spelled runs continuously, and the reason is worth stating because it is the whole remaining obstacle. Tomokiyo transliterates the code groups with latin labels drawn from a set that includes v, which is not one of the twenty-two plaintext letters; the code labels and the alphabet therefore do not share one transliteration, and every sign has to be matched to both by eye. At the resolution DECODE serves, telling the homophone pairs apart — Ɋ (r) from ǂ (s), 9 (l) from Ɋ (r), ∞ (d) from bold ∞ (g) — is a judgement call at each occurrence, and getting it wrong silently produces plausible-looking Spanish that is not what the page says. So the runs are left marked rather than filled in.

The way through is the one the glosses offer: collate the patchy interlinear decipherment on R9635 f. 20r word by word against the signs beneath it, which pins each homophone in this scribe’s hand; the unglossed lines and the rest of the group then follow. That is edition work against a published key, not cryptanalysis.

08R9605 gives ground truth, and it changes two things

R9605 (9/24 f. 39–44, 7 May 1522) is the record Tomokiyo broke the cipher from, and its images show why: the cipher and the clerk’s full contemporary decipherment sit on facing leaves. Aligned at the end of the letter, where the clear reads Plegue a Dios no lo yerre en el camino. De Venecia a vij de mayo 1522:

zn·β·aɤ + nufcami + no = caminospelled, then a code group used for the last syllable

Two things follow that the key alone could not give. Code groups stand for syllables as well as whole words — nuf is no the adverb in one breath and the final syllable of cami-no in the next, so a reading that assumes one group is one word will mis-segment. And the lookalike signs separate: camino needs β = m, ahora (nx#Ɋn, R9635 f. 20r) needs Ɋ = r, and the bold ∞ is g. All three are a “g”-shape at the resolution DECODE serves, and they are the single largest source of error in the spelled runs.

Continuing the same passage against the clear “la venida del visorey con todas las postas, que despacho y escrivo” gives three more results. xel def = del visorey and ded+xof = veni+da = venida, verbatim. The spelled run for escrivo is seven signs for seven letters, fixing z = c, ɡ = r, aɤ = i, m = v, # = o with ground truth beneath them — the method working. And va = despacho, not “desponho”: Tomokiyo’s table gives a word that is not Spanish, where the clear reads que despacho y escrivo with ho = que and va in that slot. It also explains his puzzled “me desponho x-e-r-o-n que” in R9623. A correction to the published key.

One check came out inconclusive and is recorded as such. The clear at “que fue a …” stands over the group cud, which Tomokiyo reads “xxiv?”; had the clear read xxv, the whole c-block series would shift by one and the values predicted here would be wrong. It does not settle it — the date is written over a correction and reads as something like xxv y xxvij, two dates rather than one, so it cannot be matched to a single group. cab, cef and cif do not depend on it: each was confirmed in its own context, independently of where the run starts.

The same alignment runs from the very first word of the letter. Clear (f. 42): “Ya dixe a V. Mag. que el embaxador de Hungria fue respondido de los desta republica con buenas palabras…”; cipher (f. 39r ll. 1–2): cig = ya; vu·o·ɣ = di+x+e = dixe; dom pud ho veg = vuestra magestad que el; vam vuc·ɡ = embaxa+do+r = embaxador; n·℞·∞ ɡ·aɤ = Hun-gria, spelled across the line break; sa = fue; ɡ·ɣ·q·ao·#·& vu vuc = r-e-s-p-o-n + di + do = respondido; xig vum ɡ·ɣ lal = desta republica; xa yuc·n+s = con buenas.

Nothing in that is a guess — the clerk’s plaintext stands over every group on the facing leaf. It fixes the sign table against ground truth rather than by eye: n=a, ɣ=e, ɡ=r, aɤ=i, o=x, #=o, q=s, ao=p, &=n, m=v, z=c, β=m, and the q-with-double-bar = s. (q=s here against Tomokiyo’s alphabet column q=t: two similar glyphs, and the pair to watch next.)

Carried to the unread page, though, the table does not by itself unlock it, and that corrects the diagnosis offered above. On R9635 f. 20r the first line is ‖ como ‖ quien ‖ deg ho feb ʒnɡ xof+s yeh+s xig dom pud. The tail is secure — das cartas de vuestra magestad — and the null padding matches R9605 exactly. But deg, feb and ʒnɡ resolve to nothing: none is in the nomenclator, and the last spells y-a-r, which is not a word. Sense wants recebi in that slot, whose code is hug — nothing like what is written.

So the bottleneck is not the sign table but the step before it: turning the cursive shapes into labels. R9605’s hand and R9635’s differ enough that a shape read confidently as d in one is something else in the other, and a wrong label yields a code group that either does not exist or exists with the wrong value. The alignment has to be done on the image, glyph against the clerk’s word above it, building a picture-book of each sign as this scribe forms it — not by transcribing to labels first and resolving afterwards. That is slower, and it is the honest route.

This is also why R9605 and R9613 are not additions to the reading: they were deciphered by the Emperor’s own clerk in 1522, on the facing leaves. Transcribing that clear text gives a continuous plaintext of those letters, but it is an edition of a 1522 decipherment, not a decipherment. The records that are genuinely unread have no such facing plaintext.

The route through is now open and is the next session’s work: align the whole of R9605’s cipher against its decipherment — several hundred signs of ground truth — to fix each homophone in this scribe’s hand, then carry it to the unglossed pages.

09The edition pass: the model of the writing was wrong

The failure reported above came from a wrong model of the page, and correcting it is what opened it. Everything here is written in ordinary latin-looking cursive. The two systems are told apart by shape-length and context: three-letter latin trigrams are nomenclator codes (xig, vog, dom, pud, lig, nuc), read straight off Tomokiyo’s table; single letters and short runs between them are the substitution alphabet, where the letterform and the value differ — n=a, y=e, g=r, o/✗=x, #=o, ao=p, m=u/v, z=c, β=m, 9=l, q-with-double-bar = s. Three nulls open each paragraph, padded with the clear words como and quien.

Verified on R9605 f. 39r l. 1 against the clerk’s plaintext on the facing leaf: cig vu·o·y n dom pud ho veg = “Ya dixe a vuestra magestad que el”, with vu(di)+o(x)+y(e) = dixe.

With that, the unread page reads. R9635 f. 20r ll. 2–3 run continuously:

lig vog vom nuf gad lel ao-y-g-xig-g nuc … xa pac+s xig vum g-y-lal
= “pues en esto no se puede perder nada … con los de esta republica”

Two things in that sentence are new. lel = puede, a code Tomokiyo never recovered: it brackets between lal publica and lol pudi, and the sense fixes it. And perder is written p-e-r + xig (de) + r — a code group used mid-word for a syllable, the same pattern R9605 showed with cami + nuf(no) = camino.

Line 9 then gives an independent check on the whole approach: nul (obliga) + vuc (do) + s = obligados — and obligados is precisely the word the 1522 decipherer wrote in the gloss above that stretch. Key, alphabet and gloss agree without being fitted to each other.

Further down the page: pef pac ho zel xig a-h-o | r-a = me lo que antes de ahora; yig (capitula) + c-i-o-n = capitulacion; and ho hob hec ho pac+s = que quien Inglaterra que los, with hec = Inglaterra — the English ambassador at Venice being the running subject of these months.

Lower on the page: vom+s cad pol-9 xig a-h-o-r-a = estos xx mil de ahora; ao-# xig g pac suf-z-e-r lu-ho = poderlo hazer, porque; and rug+s lul ta = las quales. Those last show the mirror of the stem-plus-ending pattern — codes run together to make one word: lu(por)+ho(que) = porque, lul(qual)+ta(es) = quales, suf(ha)+z-e-r = hazer.

The page closes with darian todos los …, no pude …, si no se … en …, and then opens a fresh paragraph with the same three nulls and pac+s xig vum r-e-lal = los de esta republica. One code there, cag, is unassigned in Tomokiyo’s table and sits at the end of the numeral run immediately before cig (ya); cag = xxx is the natural continuation and suits the sense, but it is one slot past where the run is attested and is left probable, not certain.

Line 5 gives xof veg + p-r-i-m-e-r-o + xig ta = da el primero de es[te], which fixes β = m on this page independently of camino in R9605; and at high zoom the ending of capitulacion is the curly second form of c that Tomokiyo draws as a backwards 3, so that word is settled rather than inferred.

Every line of f. 20r now carries a reading. Continuous or substantial: ll. 2–6, 9, 10, 11, 13, 15, 17, 18, 20, 22, 23, 25, with the passages at ll. 7 and 14; partial, with runs still unresolved: ll. 1, 8, 12, 16, 19, 21, 24. The page is read throughout in substance — subject, sums, parties and the drift of the argument — while a scatter of spelled runs inside partially-read lines stays open. It is not a word-perfect diplomatic edition and is not offered as one. (The crops were cut by dividing the written area into twenty-five equal bands, so the line numbers are approximate; the readings are anchored to their own words.) The remaining lines are the same work at the same zoom, cryptanalytically unremarkable, and are left untranscribed rather than filled in by inference. One token resists: gta, a dozen occurrences, cannot be a code (its middle position is not a vowel) and is most likely g (y) + ta (es), but that does not sit well everywhere and is left unresolved. The edition is in sanchez1522/edition/.

Folio 21 is two further full pages of cipher, denser than f. 20r, with the decipherer’s gloss obligados … fuessen restituydas sus tierras continuing across it. One paragraph on the right-hand leaf, marked A in the margin, is written entirely in the clear, and it names the English ambassador:

M[icer] Ricardo Paceo, embax[ad]or de Inglaterra, me ha dado el incluso memorial y rogado que escriva a v[uest]ra m[agesta]d suplicandole por las cartas que dize para el Papa y para don Joan. Sup[li]co a v[uest]ra m[agesta]d muy humilmente, con primero las mande enbiar, pues es persona que merece toda buena obra de v[uest]ra m[agesta]d, por ser muy servidor suyo y aficionado &c.

Richard Pace, Henry VIII’s envoy at Venice, has given Sánchez an enclosed memorial and asked him to press the Emperor for the letters he wants for the Pope and for don Juan — Juan Manuel, the imperial ambassador at Rome, whose own 1522 cipher Tomokiyo reconstructed in the same article. Sánchez seconds the request warmly. This fixes the identification behind hec = Inglaterra met on f. 20r, and it is the same Pace who opens R9631 in Tomokiyo’s list. The two leaves of f. 21 are not yet transcribed line by line.

10Lope Hurtado de Mendoza writes in a third cipher

The nine Lope Hurtado records bound in the same volumes look at first like more of the same: the same period, the same recipient, cipher mixed with clear inside the sentence, code groups of the same shape. They are not in Sánchez’s cipher.

The test is the third position. Sánchez’s codes end strictly in b c d f g h l m n. Folio 260v of R9648 gives tep, tap, and groups ending in z — finals Sánchez’s key does not contain. Juan Manuel’s Rome cipher, by contrast, does use p r s t z as finals (jap papa, jir, jus, boz, qat). So Lope Hurtado’s key is a third member of the family.

Whether it is Juan Manuel’s own key is not settled here. None of the groups legible on that leaf — ton, tep, tap, xul, xild, zar, xurzun — has a value in Tomokiyo’s Juan Manuel table either, but that table is itself a partial recovery, so their absence decides nothing. Several of these records carry contemporary decipherments, so the key is recoverable by the same route Tomokiyo took; they deserve a target of their own.

11What is open, and how good the reading is

  • The six-line head paragraph of R9653 f. 299r is not transcribed. Ink has bled through from the facing leaf and at the resolution DECODE serves the two hands cannot be separated reliably. Transcription, not cryptanalysis, is the obstacle.
  • R9653 f. 300–301 and R9635 f. 21–22 have not been examined.
  • On R9635 f. 20r the spelled-out letter runs between the code groups are only partly transcribed, so the page has continuous sense but is not yet a continuous text. Collating the patchy interlinear glosses line by line against the cipher would settle the letter alphabet in this hand and close it.
  • The other twenty-seven records are untouched here. Most have a contemporary decipherment bound in, so the work is transcription and edition against the published key.
  • Lope Hurtado’s key is unreconstructed.
  • Grades. The resolved run of R9653’s foot paragraph is graded C — certain on the code groups, which are unambiguous against the published key and make continuous sense. The remainder of that paragraph and the whole head paragraph are I, incomplete. The clear middle is a reading of ordinary Spanish secretary hand and carries no cipher grade; two words in it are conjectural and marked with brackets above.
  • cof xxviii, cuf xxix, cab xv, ceb xvi, cib xvii are predicted, not observed, and are flagged as such in the key file.
