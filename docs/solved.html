A catalogue · September 2026

What has been read, and how

Thirty-four ciphers solved, read or partly read, from an unaddressed Spanish cipher of January 1497 to Sun Yat-sen’s telegrams of 1917, each with the thing that actually broke it, and an account of what a frontier AI did well on the way and where it stopped

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 17 September 2026 · updated 18 September 2026 · compiled with Claude Fable 5.1 · companion to the ranking and the source file

On this pageSolved or read in fullPartly readExplained, verified or found already solvedWhere the AI was strongestWhere it was weakestSources and caveats
The short version. Twenty-nine items are read in full or in substance and eight more in long stretches. Eight of them fell to a solver written for the occasion and run ciphertext-only; seventeen fell to a printed or manuscript sibling, or a key in the same archive, that nobody had connected to the ciphertext; six fell to looking at the leaf and seeing what it was; two are verifications of keys other people found. Every reading and every negative on this site was run against a matched control of the same length and design. The last two sections say where the AI was strongest and where it was weakest, and they are a judgement, not a measurement. Status: catalogue. Percentages and counts are copied from the write-ups, not re-derived here.

01 Solved or read in full

Ordered as in the ranking, hardest and most consequential first. The fourth column is the assistant’s own account of its contribution and is the part of this page most open to correction.

Henry of Navarre → Ségur
BnF 500 Colbert 401 ff. 233, 239, 288v; then ff. 366, 333		1585–86 461 figures transcribed from the Gallica images. The upper figures pile into one slot in five (mod 5), the signature of a syllable table in alphabetical order; a structured annealer built on that constraint, with the letters’ own clear French as context, returned a key alphabetical on its own (letters 4–47, syllables 54–123, doubles) and read a matched control at 97 %. The volume was then swept: f. 366 in the same key, f. 333 read with Tomokiyo’s sibling key, which is this key shifted by eight. Hypothesis first, solver second. The mod-5 observation came before any annealing and the solver was designed around it rather than run blind. The sender was corrected from Henry III to Navarre from the content. The one item solved from the leaf with no transcription and no key family known in advance.
Swatow telegram to Sun Yat-sen
JACAR B03050738800		1916 A code condenser laid over the standard Chinese telegraph code was hypothesised from the letter pattern; 57,600 candidate keys were brute-forced and scored with a Chinese character model; 41 of about 44 characters read, the rest operator garbles. Reading the JACAR frames and the setting (the 1916 campaign against Yuan Shikai in Chaozhou) narrowed the system to a family a laptop could exhaust. The candidates were judged for sense in Chinese inside the session.
Huang Xing → Lin Hu and Li Genyuan
JACAR B03050731500		1916 Recognised by inspection: three kana per character, the consonant row carries the digit, the vowel is free. Read straight from the frames. Pattern recognition, then reading the telegraph-code result. No solver. A by-product of the Swatow item, on no list.
Fra Giovanni di Lucca → Ferdinand III
DECODE R2159		1644 Tomokiyo’s crib was self-contradictory under any substitution. The eight consistent crib letters seeded an annealer that found a 24-figure Italian alphabet with two polyphonic figures (17 = i/n, 19 = t/s); every seed agreed, shuffled controls failed; a Viterbi pass over the alternatives read the 231 groups end to end. Seeing that the published crib could not all be right, and modelling polyphony instead of forcing a one-to-one alphabet. Seventeenth-century diplomatic Italian read to judge the outputs.
Warsaw, 24 Dec 1627, to the Bishop of Olmütz?
DECODE R1408		1627 A 5-gram plus dictionary annealer from random and seeded starts. The alphabet turned out to be in plain order (odd figures a–m, even n–z) with consonant pairs, a dozen syllables, a m as nulls and thirteen word codes; shuffled controls failed. Recognising the plain-order alphabet after the fact and glossing the word codes from context (Nikolsburg, the canonry, the Queen of Poland’s sons). The addressee is an inference from the content.
Maltravers → Ormonde1634–35 A regular block alphabet and nulls recovered from only 59 figures; every spelled word reads. The nomenclator was then confirmed clause by clause against Wentworth’s dispatches in Knowler’s Strafforde’s Letters (1739) on archive.org. Knowing Knowler 1739 existed and would carry the same news, then matching clauses in its OCR. The cryptanalysis was small; the archival match is what made the reading certain.
Richelieu → M. de Rancé
BnF fr. 3829 ff. 87, 89		1629 Homophonic alphabet and nomenclature recovered ciphertext-only; then found word for word in Avenel’s edition (1858), which the catalogues and the lists had missed. A clean ciphertext-only solve, then the bibliographic check that turned “solved” into “verified against print”. Cryptanalytically among the best pieces here; historically a confirmation.
Armstrong → Madison, coded postscript
the “THE = 972” code		1808 A 580-group code table rebuilt from the State Department’s pencil decodes on NARA microfilm roll 13; 48 of 49 groups determined, the 49th a probable slip for man. A shortcut that scored frames for faint pencil was later retested over all 393 frames, found not to locate the annotated pages, and withdrawn. Working through the frames and reconstructing a table from scattered decodes; also retesting and withdrawing the frame-finding claim. The separate 20 Feb 1808 letter stays unsolved and the AFIO claim on it was rejected.
Louvois and Louis XIV → Catinat
seven despatches, Mémoires de Catinat 1819, t. II		1691 No cryptanalysis. Bazeries’ 1893 Grand Chiffre table applied to 12,362 groups from the MDZ OCR of the volume, checked against the page images (137 doubtful stretches, 7 OCR fixes); 98.1 and 99.1 % agreement with the two letters Bazeries printed in clear; the other five read for the first time in print. Realising that a printed table and a printed ciphertext had never been put together, then twelve thousand groups of careful decoding with OCR repair. The King’s decision of 14 September to abandon Piedmont is the most consequential text on this site.
Feuquières → Catinat
Pignerol, 25 Jan 1691, petit chiffre		1691 Identified as the petit chiffre of the Pignerol governors. A second letter in the same code, Louvois to d’Herleville of 6 Sept 1690, was found in the 1819 Mémoires t. I printed with its contemporary translation; the two share 72 groups, and hand alignment carried between them reads 586 of 601 tokens. Finding the sibling by searching the OCR of three volumes for the right code family, then aligning by hand. First reading since Bazeries’ unpublished one of 1893.
Urquhart’s Cyphral Octastich
The Jewel (1652), last leaf		1652 A book cipher on the book itself: number k gives the first word on physical page k, and Urquhart took the initial he needed. Verified on EEBO-TCP A95749 with a new 285-number transcription from the 1983 edition’s photographs on HCPortal (the public 272 were thirteen short): 238 of 284 first-occurrence hits against 0.43 for shuffled and random-page controls, without the plaintext Vals AI published in August 2026. Designing a check that did not lean on the rival plaintext, and fetching a better transcription from an API nobody had used for this. Ten letters of line 5 unread; the distich claim was tested and does not reproduce.
Sormano and de Vaulx → François I
Ferrara, 23 Feb 1529 (nos. 65, 66) and the sequel no. 63, BnF fr. 3096 ff. 115–122		1529 Lasry’s key, verified on the glossed sibling no. 62, applied at glyph level: 18,273 signs segmented from the Gallica scans, clustered and hand-labelled, classified by k-NN, then every line read by eye on review sheets. The leaves add a null, the crossbarred long-s as e, a double-circle nomenclator for duca, and the d, ll and cc forms. No. 66, docketed duplicata, enciphers different stretches from no. 65, so the pair checks itself. Nos. 65 and 66 read end to end; no. 63 in stretches, its last leaf missing. Building the glyph pipeline that made three long symbol-cipher letters readable in one sitting, and seeing the null and the nomenclator that the published table omits. Content: the duke of Ferrara declines the crown of Naples and the captaincy of the French army before Cambrai.
Gramont, Mâcon and Langeac → Montmorency
Venice and Rome, 1529–1537, BnF fr. 3083 no. 8, fr. 3091 no. 23, fr. 3071 nos. 4 and 7		1529–37 Four letters in four keys, all reconstructed by Lasry in 2023 from siblings and published as tables with no plaintext. Applied at glyph level: Langeac and Gramont’s 171-line letter of October 1529 in full, Gramont 1530 about 85 %, Mâcon about 90 % of the cipher. Six code signs the tables leave blank identified from context (the heart = the King, 8Δ = the Pope, 20/30/40 = Emperor/Pope/King). The Mâcon slip f. 9bis is a contemporary decipherment and agrees with the reading. Seeing that the keys existed, checking each on the leaf, and organising the reading of about 9,000 signs as parallel page-by-page transcriptions into ASCII aliases with a decoder, then joining and dating the letters. Content: Clement VII on the council and his feigned illness before Bologna; the Farnese marriage and Paul III’s threat in 1537.
Lanssac → Charles IX
Warsaw, 26 Apr 1573, BnF fr. 4735 f. 124, and ff. 160, 164, 174		1573 A homophonic letter cipher with word signs. The key was fixed from the glossed sibling f. 154v, the gutter-cut marginal fragments, and a pinned annealer with shuffled and blind controls; 96 of 100 signs on f. 124. The same key then reads three election letters of 1 and 9 May whose only “déchiffrement” is a cut gloss. Tomokiyo’s table corrected. Reading the sibling glosses on the leaf and using them as pins rather than trusting the published table; then carrying the key across the volume.
Philip II → Mendoza
7 Sept 1589, BnF fr. 3641 ff. 10/14 and 12/76		1589 Resolved rather than solved: the “second undeciphered letter” (f. 14) is the 1589 decipherer’s fair copy of the Cg.13 original on f. 10, laid out like the original with the unread groups underlined; f. 76 is the same for f. 12. Group-by-group alignment rebuilt a partial Cg.13 table, consistent with Tomokiyo’s fragment at every overlap, filled groups the decipherers left blank and corrected two of their readings. Seeing from the layout alone that the second letter was a copy, not a cipher, then squeezing more out of the pair than the 1589 office did. Fourteen groups open; the Simancas table (Devos 1950) would close them.
Charles I and Nicholas → Boswell
TNA SP 84/157 ff. 217, 219		1643 Alphabet solved by R. Pitt (Sept 2026); verified here against a 20,000-row permutation control (z = 9.6). Added: the four graphic signs as inline word-signs (good, Cousin, Master, us), the King’s letter identified as re-credentials to the Duke of Courland’s envoy via Simpson 1893, Nicholas’s letter as the Dutch embassy of 1644. Verification design and the historical identification of addressee and occasion. The key is Pitt’s.
Sir Richard Forster
13 May 1644, Val-d’Oise 68.H.8		1644 Found already read by Lasry, Biermann and Pitt. Here: permutation control z = 8.8, and a blind recovery of 31 of 34 symbols from 207 tokens once word boundaries are used and the French model writes u for v and i for j; six matched controls read. Methodological only: how far a short homophonic can be read blind when the language model is made period-correct.
Sun Yat-sen’s circle: the intercepted telegrams
JACAR B03050088300–B03050090200		1916–17 The Swatow family of systematic condensers (57,600 tables) run on each telegram and scored with a Chinese character model. A key was accepted at z ≥ 6 on one long telegram only if it then read others it had not been fitted to. That gave five 1916 keys and one for 1917, plus two tentative. About sixty telegrams were read in whole or in part, the clerks’ one-letter slips were emended, and rhyme-day dates were checked against the forms. The Hankow digits read as standard code +111. Transcribing some sixty handwritten forms from six JACAR files, and working out the clerks’ consistent confusions (g/q, x/d/s, h/k/n) so that emendations stayed single-letter and checkable. The readings were judged for sense in Chinese and set against the 1916 chronology. Where a key failed, as with 文密 and the June 1917 wires, that was recorded rather than forced.
Conti → Laigue and Noirmoutier
Paris, 5, 26 and 27 March 1649, BnF fr. 3854 nos. 41–43		1649 Nos. 41–42 were already read by Lasry (2023); here they are re-read from a fresh transcription with his key, with a quadgram beam and word rescoring picking among the polyphones. No. 43 is a syllabic number code whose first twelve lines were never glossed; its key was rebuilt from about 3,300 of the clerk’s own number/gloss pairs, and the opening was decoded. Nine values fixed by structure or context were later confirmed by glosses elsewhere in the mémoire. Checking prior art first (Lasry’s paper, miscited as fr. 3584); seeing that no. 43 was a different system and that the underline reverses a syllable; parallel agents transcribing glossed pages, finished by hand when they hit the usage limit; editing the whole mémoire.
García de Toledo → Philip II
Messina, 16 July 1565, AGS Estado leg. 1394 no. 247 (duplicado)		1565 1,930 two-digit figure groups transcribed digit for digit from the PARES JPEGs (about 915 px, the largest the viewer serves). Two cribs from the clear text, seiscientos soldados after los and en tiera after puso, fixed eleven letters. The recovered figures were in alphabetical order, so the rest of the 12–43 table was predicted and confirmed on effetos, baxeles, yban, plazer. Audit: every group in range, all 32 values used, no odd runs. Checked on Mosiur de Lenni (Provana di Leinì), a name absent from the clear text. Finding the letter in a PARES sweep and working out the image and search routes; seeing that the table was alphabetical and predicting the gaps instead of solving them; correcting misread digits from the nonsense they produced in the decode. The original despatch, which could carry a contemporary decipherment, is still to be found.
Robert Yard → the Earl of Manchester
Whitehall, 12 and 16 Oct 1699, Beinecke OSB MSS fc37, box 2, folders 49 and 51		1699 No cryptanalysis. The key was in the same Manchester papers at Yale: a printed one-part code sheet (“Diplomatic cipher, contemporary copy”, box 18/47, the = 454) with handwritten numbers skipping 5s and 9s. It was transcribed (1,456 slots) and checked on the 5 October sibling’s contemporary decipherment, then both letters were read straight off, 368 groups. Yard’s copy differs in three values (42/48 = e, 21 = d, 483 = and). Looking for the key in the recipient’s own papers instead of accepting the HMC’s “no key is known”, and transcribing the sheet from the full-resolution image. Tomokiyo had named the code and decoded the first groups. Content: the watch at Dover for Mills and Lord Drummond’s priest, and reports of a design managed from Saint-Germain.
Adrian of Utrecht, the Admiral and the Constable → Charles V
Vitoria, 30 Dec 1521, AGS Estado leg. 8 no. 150		1521 Found in print: Claudio Pérez Gredilla’s nineteenth-century decipherment, printed by Danvila (Memorial Histórico Español 38, 1899, pp. 703–705), found by OCR search once PARES gave date and place. The ciphertext was transcribed from the PARES images and aligned with it chunk by chunk (19 xif = V. M. anchors). This rebuilds a mixed nomenclator whose code initials run in plaintext-alphabet blocks. It corrects the dead king from England to Manuel I of Portugal, and reads or corrects about seventeen of Gredilla’s c. 25 unread groups. Finding the edition that neither PARES nor the lists mention, then using it as a crib rather than an answer. The Inglaterra/Portugal error came from the content, and the blank groups were read from places in the same letter where the group is spelled out or already read. About ten groups open; a sibling in the same code would close them.
Anonymous → a League leader
28 Oct 1586, BnF Clairambault 357 ff. 167–168		1586 No cryptanalysis. The letter’s 1586 decipherment is bound with it: f. 168 is a clear text of f. 167r down to a cross mark, and from the cross to the end the decipherer wrote the plaintext between the lines. Tomokiyo’s partial key checks against the glosses (Guienne, tres confident, Fumé), which add 87 = e, 22 as a null and the King of Navarre’s sign. Opening the next leaf and noticing the faint interlinear hand, which the published note on the letter had missed. Fumé, vice-admiral of Guyenne, proposes a reconciliation between Navarre and the addressee (probably Guise). Some words of the fast secretary hand and of the verso glosses remain uncertain.
Erving → Madison; J. Q. Adams → Secretary of State
Madrid, 24 Mar 1807 (Pinckney’s code); St Petersburg, No. 88, 25 June 1812 (the = 1385)		1807–12 No cryptanalysis of an unknown system. Erving: Madison’s interlinear decode (NARA M31 reel 12) collated with the undecoded LoC duplicate and with Pinckney’s decoded despatches of 1803–04; every hole in the printed text closes (Hanover, in the scale, see that he was, Gibraltar), and the only loss is Erving’s own dropped -eak. Adams: the premise that he used Armstrong’s THE = 972 code was checked and refuted on M35 reel 3; his own code (the = 1385) was rebuilt to about 370 groups from the clerk’s interlinear decodes by the roll-13 method; the nine lines Ford printed as not decyphered read 124 of 134 groups, 27 of them by slot inference. Testing an editorial premise against the microfilm instead of applying the table already built, then rebuilding a second code from scattered pencil. A passage read, not a whole despatch: ten groups unread and the rest of No. 88 only in threads. The Erving result is a small completion of a text already in print.
Cardinal Soglia → the nuncio Viale Prelà
L’Italia del Popolo no. 40, 30 June 1848 (Roman reprint)		1848 Joe’s 2014 transcription checked against the scan (one digit restored, one printer’s 5-for-4 found). 5 found to be the word break and 8 the code marker; a letters-and-syllables annealer gave the first whole words once plain substitution had failed and a synthetic control had shown the solver sound; the rest read by hand. The 8XXX code runs in alphabetical order, so ten context readings were checked by a rank model fitted on nine firm anchors, and Conte was ruled out. Eleven code groups open. Structure first: the parity of runs between 5s, the missing 8 in second place, and Ramliden’s 1814 nunciature design with the marker digit changed. The Italian reading and the history (Spada, the Chigi diary, the DBI) were done in the session. The finding that the order arrived a day late comes from setting the reading against the DBI.
Ferdinand the Catholic → Jerónimo de Vich
Seville 4 Apr 1511, Burgos 5 June 1512, Logroño 1 Sept 1512, AHN Estado 8715 N.45, N.57, N.60		1511–12 No cold break. Two siblings in the same key, N.46 and N.52BIS, carry the clerk’s decipherment. They were transcribed sign by sign from the PARES images and lined up with the decipherment word by word, giving a homophonic alphabet and the common code groups. The three cipher-only letters were then decoded by script, and the unread groups were fixed from context and accepted only when they read the same way elsewhere: 153 groups, some of them syllables inside spelt-out words. Checks: letters not used to build the key read as Spanish, the clear datelines match the catalogue, and the contents fit the dates. A PARES sweep of the Vich papers to sort the deciphered letters from the undeciphered ones; transcription of 27 pages of cipher in glyph labels; spotting the look-alike groups (fug señor / fuq Francia, plart / plort) and the two barred-q signs. N.73 (1515) left unread after n-gram annealing failed. Terrateig’s 1963 edition, which may already print the letters, has not been checked.
Mary Queen of Scots → the Duke of Norfolk
“the 20th” [Feb or Mar 1570], BL Cotton MS Caligula C II f. 74r		1570 No new cryptanalysis. Tomokiyo’s key, rebuilt from the deciphered siblings ff. 66, 69 and 75, was checked on f. 66r against its contemporary decipherment and applied to f. 74r. The leaf adds three sign uses (a lone ǂ is and, εʃ is me, a barred б is friend). 23 lines read; five nomenclator signs have no value in the key. Getting full-resolution images from a IIIF server that refuses outputs over about 1,024 px, by stitching tiles, then cutting slope-following line strips. Turning Tomokiyo’s letter-by-letter overlay into a continuous reading, filling his gaps and the line his transcript skipped. Identifying Lady Scrope, Morton, Lord Talbot and veyt = wyte, and dating the letter after Moray’s murder to 20 February or 20 March 1570.
Bordeaux → Brienne
London, 30 May 1653, Thurloe’s intercept, BL Add MS 4200 ff. 88–90		1653 The English Deciphering Branch’s key sheet for “Mr. Bordeaux”, BL Add MS 32263 f. 1 (DECODE R7537): plain 15–37 the alphabet, and three marked series of syllables in alphabetical order with words filed in place. Applied to the provisional transcription after 21 corrections from the page images: two signs, ꝺ = u and ∂ = n, had been merged as d, and tokens and diacritics were lost. Six nomenclature codes and a dozen rare tokens open. Before the key: identifying the Brienne-office design and building a solver with the alphabetical order as a hard prior, which read its controls but not the letter, and a misread-diacritic control that predicted why. After: reading the key sheet, re-checking the transcription glyph by glyph, separating the two d-signs, collating the two duplicate copies, and reading the French. The key did the decisive work.
Herbault → Béthune
Paris, 13 Feb 1626, BnF fr. 3669 no. 25		1626 No cryptanalysis and none needed: the catalogue’s single undeciphered letter is one of two copies of the same despatch, and the twin (no. 26, ff. 72–74) carries a contemporary interlinear decipherment. Identity established on the word-for-word clear text, the cipher runs falling at the same points, and matching groups at two checked places. Answering the catalogue’s own verify step (“check that no. 26 is not simply the decipherment of no. 25”) instead of attacking the cipher, then reading the 1626 decipherer’s hand. The ciphered passages are the Valtelline, the Huguenot peace and the Savoyard attempt to draw France into war with Spain. Contributed by Arya Sanketbhai Patel.

Raince → Montmorency
13 May and 20 Nov 1526, BnF fr. 2984 pp. 29–31, 105		152686 of about 106 lines read, gaps left as gaps; 20 lines machine-only Tomokiyo published the key in 2020 and no plaintext. Henri IV → Béthune
9, 10 and 22 Nov 1601, BnF fr. 3484		160110 Nov read in full; 9 Nov about six words in ten; 22 Nov open The notice’s “copie du n° précédent” is the clear minute of the 10 Nov letter. Aligning cipher against minute, with the marginal decipherments of 8 Nov and 10 Dec, gave a Villeroy-office key of the design Bazeries printed in 1599 for Béthune’s brother; a 4× re-transcription fixed the doubling sign and five syllable groups. Nevers → Pisany
8 Sept and 14 Oct 1593, BnF fr. 3985 f. 209, fr. 3986 f. 168		1593Two of seven letters read; the five to Revol are in a different key Tomokiyo’s Nevers cipher no. 46 re-read from the full-resolution Gallica image of fr. 3995 f. 87, checked against the office’s decipherment of a Gondi letter in the same key, then applied: a two-digit alphabet with homophones, three word lists and 1 as a null. Key-hunting across volumes and re-transcribing a published key at glyph level; noticing that the Revol letters were a separate system rather than forcing no. 46 onto them.
Pelissier → Jeannin
Burgos, 13 Sept 1592, BnF fr. 3982 no. 22		1592All nine pages transcribed; half to two-thirds of the cipher read, gaps marked; 315 = the Béarnais Tomokiyo’s key for Pelissier’s later letters, rebuilt from their clean decipherment, fits this one, and the decipherer’s scattered glosses agree with it. Pellevé’s secretary → the abbé d’Orbais
Rome, 23 Aug 1589, BnF fr. 3413 no. 62		1589Letter mostly in clear; of about ninety cipher signs, all but about thirty read (Cassin, la protection, vostre regne, depeschera, mon maitre, signature probably Baron) Not the three keys bound in the volume, which were all tested and do not fit. Testing and rejecting the catalogue’s own hypothesis first; then finding a second text in the same cipher with a decipherment and using it as the crib. The last groups resisted a pattern search over period French and are left open rather than guessed.

03 Explained, verified or found already solved

Not readings, but questions closed. Five items were shown to carry no message: the Roosevelt number block (a hand-written permutation of 1–52; ordered-key readings fail while matched controls succeed), the Shanghai gold bars (ten of every letter, flatter than any cipher of a real text), Hyde’s superscriptions (dummy numbers, per the 1724 editor), the D’Agapeyeff challenge (not enciphered English) and Beale no. 1 (argued a fabrication). Eight list entries were found already solved by others, with pointers added here and nothing more: Du Bellay 1528–29, the ADFGVX residue, Perwich, Ferdinand III, Milroy, Feynman 2 and 3, the Confederate dictionary code and Mazarin–Bordeaux 1654. One more was resolved from print rather than deciphered: Henri IV to Brèves, 1603, whose key survives in BnF fr. 3462 and whose letters Berger de Xivrey printed in 1853 from a clear copy. The cipher leaves are not online, so they were not read against the key. And the oldest item examined, a Catalan letter to the King of Aragon of 2 May [1413–16], listed on DECODE as an undeciphered cipher of unknown type, is a columnar transposition of word chunks that Xavier de Salas read and printed in 1931; the rule was checked on the image.

04 Where the AI was strongest

Patterns over the thirty-three items above, not measurements.

  • Reading the catalogue and the leaf as a historian would. Six items were unlocked by noticing something in the archival material rather than by a solver: the Béthune minute described as a “copie”, the Mendoza fair copy mistaken for a second cipher, the Du Bellay years hidden by the notice, the Ormonde news sitting in Knowler 1739, the Feuquières sibling in the 1819 Mémoires, the Bazeries table never applied to the printed Catinat despatches. In each case the model knew the edition existed or recognised a clerk’s working habit.
  • Structural hypothesis before search. Ségur (the mod-5 test), Warsaw (a plain-order alphabet), Lucca (polyphony), Huang Xing (kana rows as digits), Urquhart (the page-index rule). The solvers were written to the hypothesis. Blind annealers on comparable data generally failed their own controls, as the Ottobon, Birago, Bordeaux and Joyeuse notes record.
  • Period language as the language model. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century French, Italian, Spanish and Scots, and Chinese telegraph-code output, read to judge candidate plaintexts, and the model adjusted to period spelling where it mattered (Forster: u for v, i for j).
  • Small purpose-built solvers plus matched controls. Every solve and every negative was run against a control of the same length and design, so a failure says something about the method and not only the target. That discipline is what let some thirty items be closed honestly, and what stopped the Armstrong frame-scoring and the SP 53 same-key claims from standing once retested.
  • Fetching from awkward archives. The JACAR raw-PDF path, Gallica IIIF, the HCPortal photographs, MDZ OCR, NARA microfilm frames, archive.org OCR of 1688 and 1739 editions. Much of the reading depended on getting an image or a text that the lists’ authors never had.

05 Where it was weakest

  • Transcription from images. Ottobon at about 70 % per token, the Mondoucet aligners at the shuffled baseline, Béthune needing a 4× re-transcription, Tomokiyo’s normalised strings hiding glyph variants. Nearly every partial reading stops at a transcription problem, not a cryptanalytic one.
  • Blind homophonic and syllabic solving on short texts. The annealers fail their own controls below roughly 500 tokens at 130 or more symbols, and word nomenclators are out of reach without a crib. Colbert, Chaulnes, Joyeuse, Birago and SP 53 all stop there.
  • Over-claiming, caught by controls. The Armstrong pencil score, the SP 53 same-key inference and the Urquhart distich each looked good until the control was run. The record is honest because the controls were run, not because the first reading was right.
