From the Queen’s household at Paris to Charles I, January 1646

A long letter to the King against Sir Henry Compton’s embassy and on Prince Charles’s marriage, deciphered by George Lasry on DECODE in 2020; his key, applied here as a check, gives back his text.

The letter
A writer in Henrietta Maria’s household at Paris to Charles I, January [1646]. Signed with the number 385.
Where
The National Archives, Kew, SP 106/10 ff. 235–236; DECODE R924. It was part of catalogue item 59, which grouped six letters in four different keys.
The cipher
One- and two-digit numbers for letters, up to four for a vowel, and a numbered word list from 100 up, roughly in alphabetical order (100 all, 108 and, 141 for).
How it was deciphered
George Lasry reconstructed the key (“TNA SP106-8”) and deciphered the letter on DECODE in October 2020. His plaintext is the text; it stays on DECODE as his work and is not in this repository, so the reading audit of 22 October 2026 counted the letter as decoded, not read. On 23 October his files were fetched and the letter decoded afresh; with four code values set by sense it builds most sentences, but counting those as holes it stays just under the reading bar, so it is still decoded, not read. Applied here group by group, his key gives his text on every line that can be compared.
What it says
The writer argues against sending Sir H. Compton as ambassador and against the treaty for Prince Charles’s marriage with Portugal. He reports on Montreuil’s mission and on levies of five or six thousand men.
Still open
58 three-digit numbers not in Lasry’s word list and 11 low figures with no value in the key: 162 groups in all.
Other keys
The five other letters once filed with it are in different ciphers: R785, R790, R923 (TNA SP 106), R787 (SP106-2), R929 (SP106-5).

01 The letter and the key

Catalogue item 59 grouped six cipher letters of SP 106/10 from Henrietta Maria’s household in France to Charles I. George Lasry reconstructed four keys for the run, and on 14 October 2026 the entry was split so that each page presents one key. This letter is the only one of the six in his “SP106-8” key, dated January 1646. In his files the same key also covers the next record, R925 (ff. 238–240), a letter of the Queen on the same marriage.

The writer is not the Queen (“the Queen sends …”, “the Queen desires your Majesty …”) and signs with the number 385. Lasry dates the letter from its content: Montreuil’s mission, and a frost also mentioned in the Hague letter of January on ff. 245–246. He prefers 1646 to 1645. DECODE’s date of 26 June 1646 does not fit the letter’s own “Paris Jan”; the correction is queued for DECODE.

The key has one- and two-digit homophones (A 5, 15, 23, 25; E 2, 11, 22, 26) and a numbered word list starting at 100: 100 all, 101 are, 102 as, 103 at, 108 and, 109 be, 141 for, 173 I, 174 in, 176 -ing. Lasry’s file says the key has no nulls.

02 Lasry’s decipherment

The plaintext is Lasry’s and is on the DECODE record, quoted here with punctuation added:

PARIS JAN … IT PLEAS YOUR MAJESTY, YOU HAVE SO MUCH <797> <320> HANDS THAT I WILL NOT BEGIN ANOTHER WITH YOU BUT I LAY IT DOWN … FIRST THOUGH YOUR MAJESTY SHALL KNOW YOU ARE OBEYED AND SR H COMPTON IS <487>D … THE TREATY OF MARRIAGE, WHICH IS A THING OF IMPORTANCE ENOUGH WHEN IT SHALL BE <307> <142> TO BE SET AFOOT WITH PORTUGALL …

… FOR WE KNOW NOW THAT MONTREUL HAS BEEN WITH YOU … THE LEVIES NOW IN HAND IN SO GOOD AN ADVANCE THAT WE HAVE GOOD BRING TO HOLD THAT WE SHALL PASSE A BODY OF AOR [5 OR] 6 THOUSAND MEN AS GOOD AS ARE ANY <330> TO BE FOUND … I AM YOUR MAJESTY’S <219> OBEDIENT <SERVANT> <385>

03 The check made here

Lasry’s interlinear file pairs each line of Sofia Pettersson’s transcription with his plaintext. His key was applied group by group to the cipher lines, without his plaintext. Of 2012 groups, 1850 (91.9%) get a value. On 11 lines his plaintext line has a different number of words from the cipher line, so the columns shift and those lines are not compared; on all the others (1604 groups) the key gives exactly his text. The 162 groups with no value are 58 three-digit numbers (85 occurrences; 324 and 428 four times each) and 11 low figures (77 occurrences; 21 alone 24 times), which Lasry writes as “_”. The replay in section 02 shows the first 120 groups of that run.

04 What stays open

  • The three-digit numbers that are not in Lasry’s word list, left in brackets in his plaintext. Some are probably words of the list, others names.
  • The low figures 21, 30, 42, 50, 16 and a few others, which have no value in the key. They may be word dividers, nulls or letter values missing from the alphabet; this is not settled here.
  • Who 385 is. The letter speaks for the Queen’s council at Paris and does not name its writer, but Eric Sams deciphered this letter and the Queen’s (R925) in 1976 for Ian Roy (Archivaria 21, 1985–86, pp. 89–90): the writer is Henry Jermyn, at Paris on 26 January 1646, the day of his letter to Nicholas referring him to the King’s letters on the Portugal embassy. Sams’s solution was never printed; it is said to be in the Bodleian with Roy’s papers.
  • Re-reading, 23 October 2026. Lasry’s files were fetched with the project’s DECODE login and the letter decoded afresh from Pettersson’s rows (R924_reading.md, English R924_en.md). The unvalued low figures are nulls between words; “Ian. 26” and “5 or 6 thousand” are numerals in clear; four codes were valued by sense at every occurrence: 168 hope (Lasry hold), 116 can (Lasry brought), 334 what, 227 new. Counted as holes, those four and the 85 open code tokens leave the letter just under the reading bar: decoded, not read. Sams’s decipherment would settle them.
