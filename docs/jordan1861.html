Jordan to “My Dearest Rachel”, 1861: the burnt original and its clear copy

→

Two catalogue entries are one letter from Thomas Jordan to Rose Greenhow, a burnt cipher fragment and a clerk’s clear copy; the key found on her body in 1864 gives the copy’s letters.

The letter
Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Jordan, Beauregard’s adjutant, to “My Dearest Rachel”, Rose O’Neal Greenhow, summer 1861, seized at her house on 23 August 1861. A clear letter with words and parts of words in cipher.
Where
National Archives, RG 59, Seized Correspondence of Rose O’Neal Greenhow: the cipher original is naId 6925618, a photostat of burnt fragments (catalogue entry 646); the clear copy is naId 1634051, images 1–2 (catalogue entry 645).
The cipher
One symbol per letter, the system of the 1864 key sheet found on Greenhow’s body (NARA M1091, naId 62017285, image 0484).
How it was deciphered
The clerk’s copy gives every cipher passage in plain words, and a lighter hand wrote “h a r” under the first group on the original. Matched sign by sign, the 1864 key gives the copy’s letter for all 82 securely transcribed signs.
What it says
Jordan asks for Hardee’s and Scott’s Tactics, Mahan’s Field Fortification, eight yards of navy-blue flannel and shoulder straps for a lieutenant colonel: “These books are essential to me in my position”.
Still open
Three short stretches of the fragment (a line before “handed”, a central strip, the end of “for the rest to”) are too faint or torn; the copy has dashes there too.
Credit
The identification of the two items as one letter, the transcription and the key match by WAINUT (pull request #100, 27 October 2026), done with AI language-model agents. The decipherment is the clerk’s copy of 1861.

01 Two items, one letter

NARA’s naId 6925618, “Copy of a Letter Fragment Partially Written in Cipher”, is a white-on-black photostat of burnt and torn fragments pasted on a sheet: clear words with groups of symbols, cross-written in places. The catalogue had it as a letter from Greenhow to an unknown contact, written at Washington.

In the file “Letters from Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Jordan to Mrs. Greenhow and to My Dearest Rachel” (naId 1634051), images 1–2 are a fair copy in a clerk’s copperplate of a letter to “My Dearest Rachel”, all in clear, with dashes where the original is lost. Every clear phrase of the fragment (“to get for me”, “and if to be had conveniently / 8 yards navy flannel (blue)”, “for the rest to”, “me from Alex”, “essential to me in my / position as”, “by sending”) is in the copy, in the same order, so the fragment is the original of the copied letter, and the copy is its decipherment. Who made the copy, and when, the images do not show; a Federal clerk working on the seized papers is likely but not proved. Images 3–6 of the same file are a different clear letter and an envelope.

02 The key on the fragment

The 1864 key sheet (see the mourning-paper letter, which deciphers with the same key) was applied unchanged to the fragment’s 22 cipher groups, 89 signs, transcribed on inverted and contrast-stretched crops. Of the 82 signs transcribed without doubt, all 82 give the letter the clear copy has at that place: HAR(dee’s), (Tacti)CS, (n)EW COP(ies), SCOTT, (Tact)ICS, (vol)UMES, (Ma)HANS FIELD FORTIFICATION, (Sh)OULDER, (Str)APS, LIE(utenant), CO(lonel), (b)RIN(g), ANY, CAN G(et), HIM, BOO(ks), LIEU(tenant), COLONEL, IN(fantry).

Controls (decode.py): 20,000 random substitution keys average 3.1 of the 82 signs (best 36); the key shifted by 1, 2, 3 or 13 letters gives none; the true key with the groups set against the wrong words of the copy averages 5.5 (best 19); 22 of 22 groups come out whole, against at most 4 for a random key. The signs were transcribed knowing the copy’s text, but the key comes from a separate document of 1864 and each sign was matched to it by shape, so the bias is limited to the seven signs marked doubtful.

Points of detail: the first sign of HAR’s group is an O-sign (J on the key) that is not explained; SCOTT and BOO write their doubled letters plainly, not by the sheet’s inverted-second-letter rule; the first sign of OULDER may be the key’s sh digraph.

03 What it says

The fragment with the copy’s words supplied in brackets (cipher in capitals):

… to get for me HAR[dee’s Tacti]CS, [or the n]EW COP[ies of] SCOTT[’s Tact]ICS, [also … vol]UMES [of Ma]HANS FIELD FORTIFICATION[s], and if to be had conveniently 8 yards navy flannel (blue) [Indigo …]ing of [SH]OULDER [STR]APS for a LIE[utenant] CO[lonel. … handed …] he [will b]RIN[g] ANY you CAN G[et to] [H]IM, [and I will …] for the rest to […] me from Alex[andria …]. These BOO[ks are] essential to me in my position as LIEU[tenant] COLONEL of IN[fantry] and you will [do me a great favor] by sending [them by hand …].

Jordan, newly a lieutenant colonel, asks “Rachel” for the drill and engineering manuals of the day and for the cloth and insignia of his rank, to be sent by hand. On the first page of the copy he fears that an earlier letter did not reach her: he had no access to his cipher at the time, and a letter “in ordinary cha[racters]” “was intercepted”. The content is domestic. Its interest is the cipher: it shows the Jordan–Greenhow system in use in 1861 with the key found on Greenhow three years later. Jordan himself wrote on 29 October 1861 that he had arranged this cipher “last April”, hastily, under the alias Thomas John Rayford (Official Records, Series II, vol. 2, pp. 564–565).

04 What remains open

  • Three stretches of the fragment are not transcribed: line 12 before “handed”, the central strip, and the end of “for the rest to”. The signs are faint or torn, and the copy has dashes at two of them.
  • The copy’s dashes: the original is torn at those places too, and the surviving cipher does not fill them.
  • Seven signs are doubtful (marked in the transcription) and are left out of the count of 82.
  • The date: after Jordan became a lieutenant colonel and before the seizure of 23 August 1861. The place is presumed to be Beauregard’s headquarters at Manassas.
  • No printed text of the letter was found: not in the Official Records, Series II, vol. 2 (pp. 561–577), Pinkerton’s Spy of the Rebellion or Greenhow’s My Imprisonment. Fishel and Blackman could only be searched through the web.
