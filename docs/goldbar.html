Shanghai · 1933 · Latin letters stamped on gold bars · Schmeh Top 50 no. 49

The Chinese gold bar cryptograms

Seven bars said to certify a deposit of $300,000,000, stamped with sixteen strings of Latin letters for which no decipherment has been proposed in ninety years

The strings contain almost exactly ten of every letter, a distribution flatter than any encryption can produce. The letters were counted out and arranged by hand, so there is no ciphertext and no message. The flat count had been noticed before (a commenter on Schmeh’s blog, Nick Pelling, Bret Bowen); this page checks the transcription against the photographs and measures how improbable the pattern is.

01 The object

The story, as Kevin McCurley set it down for the IACR in 1996, is that seven gold bars were issued to a “General Wang” in Shanghai in 1933, functioning as metal certificates against a deposit with an American bank. The Chinese text on them describes a transaction of more than $300,000,000 and gives the bars’ combined weight as 1.8 kilograms. A dispute over the claim was live at the time, and McCurley published the photographs in the hope that reading the cryptograms would help settle it.

Elonka Dunin has carried the item on her list of famous unsolved codes ever since. In 2020 she reached the attorney named on the IACR page, who confirmed he had seen the bars, described them as about the size of a remote control, and said the banks had not treated the claim as legitimate; his file was closed. Others have noted that the aircraft depicted on the bars is a model that did not fly until 1934 or 1935, after the date the bars claim for themselves.

03 Ten of everything

Counting the letters shows the pattern.

Gold is the observed count for each letter; the pale red behind it is what 263 letters of English would give. English towers at E and falls away to nothing at Z. The bars do neither. Twenty-one of the twenty-six letters occur exactly ten times, and the five that do not are off by one, except I which is off by three:

lettercountlettercount
E11O9
I13T9
S11all other 21 letters10

Ten of each would be 260 letters exactly; the transcription gives 263. Checking the photographs removes one letter (the fourteen-letter string is thirteen on the bars) and leaves one more in doubt, so the true total is 261 or 262. It does not remove the deviations: I is thirteen on every reading, and depending on how the two doubtful glyphs are transcribed the letters off by one are three to seven. The bars themselves carry about ten of each letter; the deviations are not transcription noise. Under every defensible reading the chi-squared stays between 1.2 and 1.5 and the probability near one in a trillion. A person counting out ten of each letter and losing count by one or two gives this pattern.

04 Why no cipher can do that

testvalue
index of coincidence0.0350   (English 0.0667, random 0.0385)
chi-squared against uniform, 25 df1.251   (random sampling predicts 25)
P(chi-squared ≤ 1.251)9.3 × 10−13   about 1 in a trillion
chi-squared against English letter frequencies1488
random 263-letter strings at least this uniform, out of 200,0000

The argument rests on the distribution being flat beyond what randomness allows. The candidate ciphers in turn:

  • Simple substitution renames letters but cannot change how often they occur, so it preserves the plaintext’s skew. Index of coincidence stays near 0.066.
  • Any transposition reorders letters and leaves the counts completely untouched.
  • A short-key Vigenère flattens partway, to roughly 0.045, and leaves periodic structure behind.
  • A long key, or a one-time pad, reaches a flat expectation, but by random sampling, and random sampling is noisy. Draw 263 letters uniformly and the counts scatter with a standard deviation near 3.1, so you expect a spread from about 4 to 16 and a chi-squared near 25. Twenty-one letters landing on exactly ten is not what randomness looks like.

Only deliberate construction produces it. The arrangement of the letters into the sixteen strings looks random. There are 71 places where a letter repeats inside a string, against 65.6 expected when the same pool of letters is shuffled at random into the same lengths. Somebody counted out ten of each letter and distributed them.

05 What it means

The inscriptions are decorative filler, made to look like cryptography on an object whose purpose was to look like a security. That sits with everything else known about the bars: an aircraft that had not yet been built on a bar dated 1933, banks that declined the claim, and a legal file closed without resolution.

The item should be reclassified on the lists of famous ciphers as a construction with no message in it. The statistics explain why ninety years of attempts produced no decipherment.

On the transcription: the argument was first made on the IACR’s 1996 transcription alone, with its author’s warning that some letters were hard to read. The letters have since been re-transcribed from the fifteen IACR photographs, enlarged and contrast-stretched. Fourteen of the sixteen strings are confirmed; one is a letter shorter than published and one letter in another is unresolved at the resolution available. The statistics were recomputed for every reading, and the result does not move.
