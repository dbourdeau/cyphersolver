01 The letters

Volume 1 of the Intercepted Letters in the Papers of the Continental Congress holds British letters taken by the Americans between 1775 and 1781. The National Archives catalogue describes them as including “letters in cipher (some decoded) exchanged between General Charles Cornwallis and Colonel Nisbet Balfour, 1780–1781”. The image descriptions supplied by Fold3 (one per digitised page) were filtered for cipher, Cornwallis and Balfour, which found these items:

  • A. Balfour to Cornwallis, Nos. 1–3, pp. 717–725 (images 562–570), wholly in cipher, with a letter-by-letter decipherment written over every line. The docket on p. 728 reads “Letters dated the … March 1781 … in Cypher … supposed Balfour to Cornwallis”. The three sheets are one dispatch: the last words of No. 1 run on into the first word of No. 2 (“their post near Heron’s / bridge”).
  • B. Balfour to Cornwallis, pp. 729–732 (images 572–575), wholly in cipher, interlinear decipherment, docketed “Balfour to Cornwallis, about March 1781”.
  • C. Balfour at Charleston to Lt Col. Alexander Stewart, 12 August 1781, pp. 733–736 (images 576–579): clear text with runs of cipher, most of them with the decipherment over them; signed N. Balfour.

The dates come from the text. No. 1 reports the Chatham’s arrival on 29 January, the raid on Monk’s Corner on 2 February and Craig’s letter of 28 February; No. 2 reports Sumter’s attack on the Congarees on the “nineteenth ult.” (19 February), so the dispatch is of March. B has Rodney’s capture of St Eustatius (3 February) and news of Arnold at Portsmouth on 10 February.

02 The cipher

Ian Saberton described this cipher in 2019 from letters in the Cornwallis Papers: an inner ring of numbers turned against an alphabet, the number standing for A given first, and numbers of 30 and over as fillers. The letters here fit that description exactly, and the transcription gives the whole ring:

  • The ring. 25 numbers in a fixed cyclic order, 5 24 29 1 25 23 22 6 19 9 17 13 16 7 12 8 14 15 26 4 18 21 3 2 11 (1–29 without 10, 20, 27 and 28), against a 25-letter alphabet with i and j as one letter. With A set to 19 the table is a19 b9 c17 d13 e16 f7 g12 h8 i14 k15 l26 m4 n18 o21 p3 q2 r11 s5 t24 u29 v1 w25 x23 y22 z6.
  • Fillers. Numbers of 30 and over mean nothing and are scattered through the words (24 50 8 69 16 = “the”).
  • The setting moves. A group of fillers holding one real number resets A to that number. The decipherer marked each such group with a brace. No. 1 has 13 of them, No. 2 has 15, No. 3 has 3 and B has 4. Nos. 2 and 3 start without one and carry on with the previous sheet’s last setting.
  • Numerals in clear. A number standing alone between dashes is a numeral: the 82nd, 84th, 71st and 64th regiments, dates (29 January, 28 February, 26), a French 64-gun ship, 18 men and 40 horses.

On p. 718 the encipherer slipped: the indicator group 47 49 31 25 40 would set A to 25, but the decipherer underlined the 25 and wrote “should be 13”. With 13 the text goes on “two troops under Colonel Fenwick”; with 25 it does not read. This decipherer was working from the numbers, not copying a clear text.

The Stewart letter of August is in another cipher: one fixed table with no settings, the numbers 12–38 for letters and 40 and over as fillers. Over a run the decipherer wrote “day notice is sent”, and the fifteen numbers under it, fillers removed, match the fifteen letters one for one (d32 a31 y29 n35 o16 t37 i24 c22 e13 s27). Its table, rebuilt from the interlinear, is a31 b12 c22 d32 e13 f23 g33 h14 i24 k34 l15 m25 n35 o16 p26 r17 s27 t37 u18 v28 w38 y29, with e also 1.

03 Balfour to Cornwallis, Nos. 1–3

Word division, capitals and punctuation are editorial. Numbers that stand in clear in the cipher are printed as numerals. The cipher’s spellings are kept (“governer”, “offisers”, “cary sfort”).

No. 1. The principal occurrences since you left us are the arrival of the Chatham with convoy from Europe, Governor Bull and Sir Egerton Leigh passengers, both of which are now at the Board of Police. Fifty thousand pounds came in the Chatham; she arrived 29 January. A party of Marion and Lee surprised the sergeant’s party at Monk’s Corner on the 2 February, killed two and made some prisoners, which, with burning three waggons and carrying off some horses, was all they effected. Colonel Small with the 84 and fifty Hessians marched immediately there; the church was fortified and the inhabitants’ horses ordered in to mount the infantry there, as well as at Neilson’s, where fifty men are placed. Two troops under Colonel Fenwick and Lieutenant Campbell, 71 regiment, raising and in forwardness. The light company of the 84 sent to Pocotaligo to cover that country from plunderers. The 82 with convalescents of the army took possession of Wilmington the 28 January without opposition; the enemy were driven from their post at Heron’s Bridge by Major Craig; the marines and seamen landed and a post close to the town taken, which is now in a state of defence not to be forced but by a large corps [with] cannon. Craig is informed of your movements so far as we know them. Tho’ not afraid of the militia now against him, wishes much for a reinforcement; this is not in my power to send. He has all the articles wanted by the army and is perfectly safe. His last letter, 28 February, mentions the enemy being about six hundred and in their post near Heron’s

No. 2. Bridge. If you mean to communicate by Cross Creek the banks of the river must be cleared, as the enemy have posts on it. Georgetown was attacked and Colonel Campbell with some few men taken prisoners as they slept in a house out of the redoubt. Some small parties of men have since been at that post, from the bad conduct of the officers. Fanning’s is now moved from thence and joined Watson’s corps; a detachment of one hundred men are now in Georgetown under Captain Gray of Fanning’s. A fleet from Ireland with provisions and stores under convoy of the Assurance arrived 23 ult., and being in want of water put into this and Beaufort before they proceed to York. A packet from England with duplicates of the dispatches by Ross 20 ult. On the last of February a packet sailed for England with accounts of your being at Salem and the state of affairs here. An express from General Arnold in the Chesapeak 23 ultimo, informing of a French 64 and two frigates having blocked up that place, and desiring assistance. This letter was shewn to Captain Barclay, who altho’ possessed of the Chatham, Carysfort, Assurance, Galatea and Blonde, would not proceed in quest of the enemy. The Chatham with her convoy sailed 26 for New York. Sumpter, having raised at least three hundred men, attacked Congarees nineteenth ult., but by the conduct of Maxwell was beat off; at Thompson’s he had the same fate and was forced to cross the Santee. Lord Rawdon moved with great speed after him to Congarees, Camden and Sumpter’s [?], detaching 64 and York Volunteers in such directions as gave reason to expect the success his movements merited, by misconduct in

No. 3. some of the detachments and other unforeseen events he only got at him as he crossed the river, by Watson’s corps, which acted with […] and drove him with the loss of 18 men and 40 horses; we lost none. Watson is now in pursuit of him, and Marion with the [Hightown?] and Fanning’s. Lord Rawdon is also towards [Pedee?]

No. 3 ends at the foot of its sheet; no further sheet survives in the volume.

04 Balfour to Cornwallis, the second letter

The same ring and the same setting rule decipher it unchanged, with four indicators (A = 23, 18, 6, 5).

In Gen. Arnold’s letter is the following paragraph: “If the cooperation will be of service, I intend to send some boats with four or five hundred men through Currituck Inlet to sweep the Albemarle Sound as high as Edenton, and to go to Newbern and destroy their shipping, stores &c., while a few armed vessels take post at Ocracock Bar and a frigate cruises without.” The General’s intentions to prosecute this plan is corroborated by Capt. Eyons of the Charlestown, who arrived yesterday from Gardner’s Bay; left Arnold at Portsmouth on the tenth of Feb[ruary]. By the progress of Arnold, his operations are mentioned in a newspaper. By the bearer [?]: the removal of the French and intelligence of your movements are both necessary before Arnold proceeds to execute his plan. I have sent him by Evans the cypher, in case he can communicate with you. Evans left this today, being sent to collect the force here, which is now dispersed, to attack the French in the Chesapeak, and proceeds to try to get to Arnold. Having reason to believe the enemy are gone from thence [Savannah?] is well, but seriously alarmed for want of provisions; the river Savannah must shortly be our dependance for supplying that post. The enemy make such serious inroads [and] raise such force that I fear, without an addition soon to our corps on the frontier, we shall be awkwardly situated. A fortunate stroke at Sumpter and Marian will do much. What is to be done with the troops from England, if they come, as is expected, here? What is to become of the post at Wilmington if your movements leave that place uncovered by your army? The bearer, I believe, if he delivers this, he must be well rewarded; he is to proceed to Philadelphia and will be useful there if you wish it. Scott’s letter is a blind. Sir George Rodney has taken possession of St Eustatia, has engaged a Dutch line of battle ship, taken her and a large fleet of merchantmen under convoy for Holland, so that I think there is no kind of doubt a war with the Dutch must take place.

“I have sent him by Evans the cypher”: Balfour sent Arnold a copy of this cipher, which Saberton identifies as the one Arnold and later Leslie used with Cornwallis in Virginia.

05 Balfour to Stewart, 12 August 1781

A clear letter, “Charles Town August 12th 1781”, to the commander of the field army after Rawdon’s departure, with the sensitive parts in cipher. The cipher runs, in order (the clear text between them is in the transcription):

… the escort will not be at Martin’s till Tuesday [?] … day notice is sent … detachment to wait [?] … for the militia will not keep Orangeburgh … possible at present to supply a post there; our present communication assisted by water carriage … keep it … two hundred guineas are rent [?] … spare, or is safe to send at once … not one dragoon is here; employed to the southward getting provisions, rice … your mounted convalescents belonging to your army … by then [the next?] escort … officers when recovered … as to Thomson, I am clear he will be better here; very dangerous; be assured [it] is greatly to be guarded against, as we are vulnerable in so many posts. The depot near Monk’s Corner put in a state of defence … to get possession of the boats upon the Santee by means of the negroes … the garrison [?] … Caldwell near you, but is not to act with the cavalry, by Lord Rawdon’s directions … from your intelligence that the enemy are in force on the other side low down, cross either at Manigault’s or Murray’s ferries, intercept the escort; a small movement of a corps towards Nelson’s would effectually cover them, if convenient.

06 Method

The two volumes of Intercepted Letters (848 images) were listed through the National Archives Catalog’s records API. The pages were fetched as JPEG 2000 and cut into strips, and three transcription agents copied every number, the interlinear letters and the decipherer’s marks: 6,349 cipher tokens on 17 pages.

A single number-to-letter table per sheet, aligned against the interlinear, agreed only 20–27%: the table changes inside a sheet. Saberton’s ring, given a setting for each word, read in long runs. A 25-letter alphabet fixed the remaining i/j and h/i slips, and then one ring order fitted every page. The braced groups proved to be the setting indicators. From then on the decoder (decode.py) works from the numbers alone: it reads an indicator’s number as A, skips fillers and prints lone numbers as numerals. Where an indicator has only two fillers and could also be the word “a” padded with fillers, the next 40 letters are scored both ways with the project’s English model. The only outside correction is the decipherer’s “should be 13”. Two fillers cut off at the page edge on p. 722 were restored as “4x” from the visible first digit, which completed two indicators.

Measured on the decoder’s output, a letter counts as read when a 24-letter window containing it scores at least −3.3 per letter on the en-modern model. No. 1: 1,213 of 1,213; No. 2: 1,292 of 1,292; No. 3: 237 of 237; B: 1,530 of 1,539; Stewart: 850 of 850. The same measure on the same numbers deciphered with a corrupted ring gives 0–2%. The project’s validation test (docs/_validate.py) scores the reading at −2.24 per letter, against −7.26 for random keys of the same shape and −4.16 for a key refitted to shuffled ciphertext: pass.

Against the interlinear as transcribed, the decoder agrees on 62–82% of letters where the interlinear is legible. The differences checked are faint or misread interlinear letters. The one real disagreement is in No. 3, where the cipher has the numeral 18 for the men lost and the decipherer wrote “fifteen”.

07 What remains uncertain

  • Stewart letter: the table values 2, 3, 19 and 30 (five tokens). 30 reads as “w” in “will” and as “n” in “present” and may be a misread; the interlinear is illegible there. Grade C for the words around them.
  • Twelve numbers in A and B illegible on the microfilm scan (page edges of pp. 718 and 724, blots); each falls inside a word that otherwise reads.
  • No. 2, after “Sumpter’s”, and No. 3, after “acted with”: a word the cipher does not give (grade I). “Hightown” and “Pedee” at the end of No. 3 are conjectures for “hitown” and “s?edee” (grade C).
  • Stewart letter: “Tuesday”, “rent” and “the next” are grade C; the runs are short, and the clear text between them has not been edited here.
  • The cipher passages of Cornwallis to Wemyss and to Balfour (7 October 1780) and Craig to Cornwallis (23 July 1781), in the same volume, were not transcribed; their numbered translations are on pp. 759–761.
  • The decipherer’s hand is not signed. James Lovell broke the cipher of Clinton and Cornwallis in 1781, and these pages are probably his work, but the hand has not been compared with his.

# A. Balfour to Cornwallis, Charleston, early March 1781, Nos. 1-3 (pp. 717-725)

**No. 1.** The principal occurrences since you left us are the arrival of the Chatham with convoy from
Europe, Governor Bull and Sir Egerton Leigh passengers, both of which are now at the Board of Police. Fifty
thousand pounds came in the Chatham; she arrived 29 January. A party of Marion and Lee surprised the
sergeant's party at Monk's Corner on the 2 February, killed two and made some prisoners, which, with burning
three waggons and carrying off some horses, was all they effected. Colonel Small with the 84 and fifty
Hessians marched immediately there; the church was fortified and the inhabitants' horses ordered in to mount
the infantry there, as well as at Neilson's, where fifty men are placed. Two troops under Colonel Fenwick and
Lieutenant Campbell, 71 regiment, raising and in forwardness. The light company of the 84 sent to Pocotaligo
to cover that country from plunderers. The 82 with convalescents of the army took possession of Wilmington
the 28 January without opposition; the enemy were driven from their post at Heron's Bridge by Major Craig;
the marines and seamen landed and a post close to the town taken, which is now in a state of defence not to
be forced but by a large corps [with] cannon. Craig is informed of your movements so far as we know them.
Tho' not afraid of the militia now against him, wishes much for a reinforcement; this is not in my power to
send. He has all the articles wanted by the army and is perfectly safe. His last letter, 28 February,
mentions the enemy being about six hundred and in their post near Heron's

**No. 2.** Bridge. If you mean to communicate by Cross Creek the banks of the river must be cleared, as the
enemy have posts on it. Georgetown was attacked and Colonel Campbell with some few men taken prisoners as
they slept in a house out of the redoubt. Some small parties of men have since been at that post, from the
bad conduct of the officers. Fanning's is now moved from thence and joined Watson's corps; a detachment of
one hundred men are now in Georgetown under Captain Gray of Fanning's. A fleet from Ireland with provisions
and stores under convoy of the Assurance arrived 23 ult., and being in want of water put into this and
Beaufort before they proceed to York. A packet from England with duplicates of the dispatches by Ross 20 ult.
On the last of February a packet sailed for England with accounts of your being at Salem and the state of
affairs here. An express from General Arnold in the Chesapeak 23 ultimo, informing of a French 64 and two
frigates having blocked up that place, and desiring assistance. This letter was shewn to Captain Barclay,
who altho' possessed of the Chatham, Carysfort, Assurance, Galatea and Blonde, would not proceed in quest of
the enemy. The Chatham with her convoy sailed 26 for New York. Sumpter, having raised at least three hundred
men, attacked Congarees nineteenth ult., but by the conduct of Maxwell was beat off; at Thompson's he had the
same fate and was forced to cross the Santee. Lord Rawdon moved with great speed after him to Congarees,
Camden and Sumpter's [?], detaching 64 and York Volunteers in such directions as gave reason to expect the
success his movements merited, by misconduct in

**No. 3.** some of the detachments and other unforeseen events he only got at him as he crossed the river,

# B. Balfour to Cornwallis, Charleston, about March 1781 (pp. 729-732)

In Gen. Arnold's letter is the following paragraph: "If the cooperation will be of service, I intend to send
some boats with four or five hundred men through Currituck Inlet to sweep the Albemarle Sound as high as
Edenton, and to go to Newbern and destroy their shipping, stores &c., while a few armed vessels take post at
Ocracock Bar and a frigate cruises without." The General's intentions to prosecute this plan is corroborated
by Capt. Eyons of the Charlestown, who arrived yesterday from Gardner's Bay; left Arnold at Portsmouth on the
tenth of Feb[ruary]. By the progress of Arnold, his operations are mentioned in a newspaper.

By the bearer [?]: the removal of the French and intelligence of your movements are both necessary before
Arnold proceeds to execute his plan. I have sent him by Evans the cypher, in case he can communicate with
you. Evans left this today, being sent to collect the force here, which is now dispersed, to attack the
French in the Chesapeak, and proceeds to try to get to Arnold. Having reason to believe the enemy are gone
from thence [Savannah?] is well, but seriously alarmed for want of provisions; the river Savannah must
shortly be our dependance for supplying that post. The enemy make such serious inroads [and] raise such force
that I fear, without an addition soon to our corps on the frontier, we shall be awkwardly situated. A
fortunate stroke at Sumpter and Marian will do much. What is to be done with the troops from England, if they
come, as is expected, here? What is to become of the post at Wilmington if your movements leave that place
uncovered by your army? The bearer, I believe, if he delivers this, he must be well rewarded; he is to
proceed to Philadelphia and will be useful there if you wish it. Scott's letter is a blind. Sir George Rodney
has taken possession of St Eustatia, has engaged a Dutch line of battle ship, taken her and a large fleet of
merchantmen under convoy for Holland, so that I think there is no kind of doubt a war with the Dutch must
take place.

Notes. "By the bearer" is the end of a sentence in the cipher ("...in a newspaper by the bearer"); its
place is uncertain. The "&" before "Marian" and in "French & proceeds" is a cross sign, not a number
(the decipherer wrote "&" over it on p. 731).
