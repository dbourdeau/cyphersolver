Charles III of Lorraine to Vaudémont, 18 June 1592 — a reciprocal pair cipher, read in part

→

BnF Français 3621 no. 97, f. 109 · the Duke writes to his son in the field about promises not kept, pioneers and munitions, Chasteauvillain and the quarters of La Fauche · DECODE record 9449 has it as Non-decrypted, and no key was ever published

Broken ciphertext-only on 18 September by a contributor · re-read from the page with the key in hand on 22 September: every letter stands for its partner in eleven fixed pairs · 790 of 1,105 cipher tokens read; special signs, eleven figures and four runs still open

Summary. A copy of a letter from Charles III, duke of Lorraine, to his son the comte de Vaudémont, Nancy, 18 June 1592, bound in BnF Français 3621 as no. 97. The DECODE database gives this exact piece as Non-decrypted; no key for it has ever been published, and the hoped-for crib, no. 22 of the same volume, is a plaintext decipherment with no cipher on it. On 18 September 2026 the cipher was broken cold from the single copy: the glyphs turned out to be ordinary cursive letterforms, and a search calibrated on known-key controls produced a 44-symbol key that spells chasteau twice where Chasteauvillain stands in the clear on the same page. That break is the contributor’s. On 22 September the letter was re-read line by line from the page with that key beside it, and the key turned out to be something simpler: a reciprocal letter-pair substitution (a/i b/p c/f d/q e/t g/u l/y m/z n/s o/r h/x), with two dots under a letter to double it, a looped æ as a null word-divider, a few special signs, and figures that are at least partly syllabic (31 = ma, 139 = vous, 145 = me, graded C). The 44 symbols had come from two merged glyphs in the transcription, the secretary e with c and the looped h with b. Measured by one rule, 790 of 1,105 cipher tokens now read, 71.5%; the 18 September key gives the same words for 40.6%. The re-read also corrects prisonniers to pionniers. Status: read in part. Open: most special signs, 11 of the 14 figures, and four garbled runs; no key or second letter in this cipher is known. The 18 September break was contributed by aryasn2026 in pull request #4 and merged after review; the 22 September re-read is the project’s.

01The document, and the verification question answered negatively

No. 97 is at folio 109 (Gallica canvas 227): “Coppie d’une lettre … de Charles de Lorraine à monsieur de Vaudemont”, Nancy, 18 June 1592. The volume is the duc de Nevers’s intercept and correspondence file for 1591–92. The task came with an instruction to check first for the ciphered originals of the letters intercepted in January 1592. They are not there. No. 22, at folio 31, is a plaintext decipherment only — a fair copy of the French of three intercepted Vaudémont letters, with no cipher anywhere on the sheet, headed “Dechiffrement des lettres de Monsieur de Vaudemont à Monsieur de Lorraine [son] pere, interceptees et apportees à Monsieur de Dinteville, le XX[e] Janvier 1592”. DECODE confirms it independently: its record for no. 22 (9444) is marked Decrypted. The other ciphered pieces of the volume (Birago nos. 31 and 36, Potier no. 79, Dinteville nos. 114 and 116) are Nevers’s own correspondents and their keys, not Lorraine’s. So no. 97 is a single copy with no sibling.

The letter is not a wholly ciphered despatch: clear French and cipher alternate inline on the same lines, and the postscript is clear. The clear text is the same business as the cipher — munitions promised and undelivered, Chaumont, the sieur de Buzonville, the attempt on Chasteauvillain.

02No key has ever been published

DECODE record 9449 is this letter, by shelfmark, sender, receiver and date, and its status field reads Non-decrypted; of the eight DECODE records for Français 3621 only this one and no. 116 are undecrypted. No key record in DECODE is attributed to Charles III or to Vaudémont. Keys for other Lorraine ciphers are published and are not this system: Bréval to Henri II of about 1620, and Bassompierre to Charles III of 1 August 1593, both reconstructed by Satoshi Tomokiyo. Desenclos and Lasry’s 2024 paper on the Henri IV–Nevers ciphers of Français 3995 never mentions Français 3621, and Henri Lepage’s 1864 edition of Charles III’s League correspondence stops in 1591 and prints no cipher table.

Four 1592 key sheets in the Nevers key book, Français 3995, whose DECODE profile matched this cipher, were fetched and examined. All four are eliminated on documentary grounds.

SheetWhat it actually is
fol. 32A jargon dictionary of covert equivalents — Nouvelles = “Draps de soye”, Necessité = “Medecine” — in geometric signs and figures 2–40. Not a letter cipher.
fol. 83–84An all-digit cipher with a Nulles row. “Duc de Lorraine” appears in it as nomenclator entry 46, so it is somebody else’s key that mentions Lorraine.
fol. 98Endorsed in its own hand: “1592 / Chifre d’entre le Duc de Parme et le Roy Cath[olique] en l’an 1592 / lequel sert aussi p[our] le Duc de Sessa et don Diego de Ybarra”.
fol. 103A Nevers-circle nomenclator, its flap listing Gondi, Nemours, Guise and Montpensier, with three homophones for every letter — not a one-for-one pair cipher. It shows what a full key of this milieu holds: syllable figures 1–72, double-letter figures 73–98, word figures 99–353, and lists of places in which Lorraine is 30 and Metz 68.

03The break of 18 September (contributed)

An earlier attempt had treated the page as a symbol cipher: segment the glyphs, cluster them by shape, solve the cluster sequence. The segmentation worked — the baselines slope, and a shear of −0.031 resolves the block into 21 line bands — but the clustering was unnecessary. The glyphs are ordinary cursive minuscules, the same letters as the clear text, some with dots or a bar, with a handful of special signs, and at one and a half times native they can be read straight off the page. The contributor’s eye transcription recorded 1,109 tokens: 1,071 letter symbols in 44 distinct forms, and 38 nomenclator figures. Its letter frequencies match French rank for rank through fourteen places, and its coincidence statistics sit between a monoalphabetic and a fully homophonic system.

The random-move annealer of the earlier attempt was shown, on a control with a known key of the same size, to recover only 37–56% of letters at −2.64 per character while the true key scored −1.62: the failure was in the search. A steepest-ascent sweep, kicked out of local optima, recovers known keys at 98.7–99.6%. Two traps were closed on the way (free nulls that delete every hard position, and a degenerate etenete… key). The key it found was checked against the manuscript, not the model: the eight-form group it reads chasteau occurs twice in the cipher, and Chasteauvillain stands in the clear on the same page, in the body and in the postscript.

Because a 4-gram search over 44 free parameters could manufacture French from nothing, the pipeline was also run unchanged on shuffled nulls of the same symbols and frequencies, and scored blind:

Thirty-three long words against nought to three: there was real French under the transcription. The contributor judged, from controls with deliberately merged glyphs, that about a quarter of the glyph readings were still wrong, and proposed re-reading the page with the key in hand. The 18 September decode read, for example, chasteauilainss [145] nntaises deramenermonarme dooites esquartiers de-, with the words standing whole and the joins between them not.

04The re-read of 22 September, and what it corrected

The re-read was done as proposed: the native IIIF image of canvas 227 (4046 × 5762), sheared −0.031 and cut into 21 line bands, each viewed in five crops at 2.3×, every word read against the 18 September decode. Laid out as pairs, the 18 September values already said what the system was: glyph a gave i and glyph i gave a, o gave r and r gave o, q gave d and d gave q, n gave s and s gave n. Every letter of the hand stands for its partner in eleven fixed pairs, the whole 22-letter alphabet of the period:

So at least part of the nomenclator is syllabic, like the syllable figures of the fr. 3995 keys, and not a list of persons and places.

Why the 18 September key had 44 symbols instead of eleven pairs: two transcription merges did most of the damage. The transcription’s c was two glyphs, the secretary e (which reads t) and c (which reads f). The search had to send the merged form to t, so every f in the letter was lost (intiniment, satistait) — which is why the old key had no value at all for b, f, g, x and z, a gap that page already flagged as the place where mis-read glyphs had gone. Its B was likewise two glyphs, the beta-like b (p) and the looped secretary h (x, hence h): du peu but ceulx and d’execution. The dotted and barred variants that the search had spread over extra homophones are doublings. Correcting those turned most of the “connective tissue” of the old decode into plain French with no further guessing.

What changed in the reading:

  • prisonniers is pionniers — the Duke complains of pioneers and munitions promised, not of prisoners.
  • 139 and 145 are not names. The earlier page guessed, from the clear text, that they might be Chasteauvillain and Chaulmont; in context they read vous and me (grade C).
  • The f words come back: infiniment, satisfait, fait, affin, faire, fauldra, la Faulche.
  • The chasteauilains slip that the null test exposed is confirmed at both places (L14, L18): the scribe left out the v twice, so it is an emendation, not a fault of the key.
  • The measure: the old claim that about three quarters of the letters were right was an estimate from controls. Counted word by word against the new reading, the 18 September key and tokens give the same words for 449 of 1,105 tokens, 40.6%; the re-read gives 790, 71.5% (section 06).

In substance: the Duke has been infinitely ill satisfied with the little execution of what was promised. Certain people (57, “ceux de la 98”) had made so many fine promises to help him with pioneers and munitions; his son is to make it plain to them and to the deputies of 98, and not fail to demand of them that they recognise le juste mescontentement. Prudence will be needed so that 141 do not abandon me all at once, for munitions, horses and other supplies. After the clear line on taking Chasteauvillain, someone must be put there pour y commander et conserver la plaine; the Duke means de ramener mon armée ès quartiers de la Faulche — La Fauche lies between Neufchâteau and Chaumont, the theatre the clear postscript describes — and wants to be préadverti of the day. Vous tiendrés [les] chemins. He sets out ma resolution … le peu de moien que [j’ai] de secours, what l’on avoit promis about Chasteauvillain, and whether to passer oultre.

06How much is read, measured

The rule, stated in measure_reread.py: the unit is the cipher token (one letter sign, a dotted double counting once; one special sign; one null; one figure). Each word of the reading is graded H secure, C probable, M doubtful or I unread, and a token is read if its word is H or C. Every word is also decoded mechanically with the pair key, so a reading that departs from the key shows up as an emendation.

07What remains uncertain

315 tokens are not read. Each open piece, with the reason it is open:

  • Special signs with no settled value: λ (11 times), m̄ (9), U (8), γ (5), E (4), π (4), ꜗ, t with a low bar, the figure-4 form, and the reversed 3 in some places — about 60 tokens, and most of the unread words contain one. m̄ and λ stand between whole words and may be nulls, but that is not proved. Grade I. Open because no key survives and each sign occurs one to eleven times, too few for context to fix most of them.
  • Eleven figures: 57 (3), 141 (3), 88 (2), 98 (2), 13, 103, 121, 122, 123, 137, 146 — 17 tokens, grade I. 141 is a plural subject (affin que 141 ne me delaissent), 57 and 98 are persons or bodies (ceulx de la 98, les deputés de … 98). One letter, no key, no sibling in the same cipher.
  • Four garbled runs, about 45 tokens, grade I: L12 after passer; L13 between des and apres; L18 between pour and Chasteauvilain; L20 around m’a d…te. At IIIF 1:1 the glyph sequence does not decode to French under the pair key even with the specials set aside; the original or a higher-resolution scan is needed to tell e from c, b from h and the dotted forms apart there.
  • Doubtful readings (grade M): dressera (L02), …ien (L03, L13, L17), manquiés and demander (L04), et re-/-solument (L12–13), ne fais, es and droites (L14), pour (L15), one vous (L16), asseurté (L20). Not counted as read.
  • Probable readings (grade C), counted as read: the figures 31, 139 and 145; pionniers, belles, ont, avoient, l’entendre, ausdits, conno-issent, mescontentement, prudence, affin, delaissent, remettre, commandement, quelq[u’un], induiront, l’effect, vilain(s), préadvertir, jour, ainsy, l’on avoit, oultre and a few short words. Everything else read is H.
  • Not consulted: the Archives départementales de Meurthe-et-Moselle, where a duplicate or a key would most plausibly survive; the remaining key sheets of Français 3995 one by one. Both need physical access or a catalogue not online.
