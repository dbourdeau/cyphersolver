England → an officer of the Orange troops in Germany · 6×6 digit square with word signs · late Nov–early Dec 1795

Prince Frederick of Orange to an officer of the Orange troops, late 1795

Koninklijk Huisarchief, The Hague, Prins Willem V, inv. 339 (DECODE R1892), two pages catalogued with no decipherment, as a letter of “1 January 1795” from an unknown writer; it is Prince Frederick’s, of late 1795. Each plaintext letter is a pair of digits 1–6 written one above the other. The square was recovered from the ciphertext alone and decrypts both pages, the first in French, the second a Dutch postscript: 1,816 of 1,829 digit pairs have a value, and about twenty graphic word signs are not identified. No earlier decipherment was found. In private communications, Lasry wrote that he had independently recovered the key in 2021; his solution has not been published.

At a glanceCiphertext-only break · partial (95% of the cipher)

The letter
Prince Frederick of Orange, the Stadholder’s younger son, by then in England, to the officer in charge of the Dutch émigré troops in Germany, late November or early December 1795: a French page in cipher and a Dutch postscript.
Where
Koninklijk Huisarchief, The Hague, Prins Willem V, inv. 339 (bundel A18); DECODE R1892, catalogued as an unknown writer’s letter to William V of 1 January 1795, with no decipherment.
The cipher
Each letter a pair of digits 1–6 written one above the other (a 6×6 square), with graphic signs for frequent words.
How it was deciphered
The square was recovered from the Dutch page alone by simulated annealing and then decrypted the French page unchanged. On 2 October 2026 the values R2242, the sibling letter, gives for its graphic word signs were tried at every occurrence (five adopted, six ruled out), and Colenbrander’s Gedenkstukken gave the writer and the date. On 3 October six lines were transcribed again on the full-resolution images, eleven one-digit slips of the writer emended, and one more word sign found (P = voor).
What it says
If General Dundas stops the pay or orders the troops to embark, accept it and ask only for a gratuity for those who will not submit; tell the troops on what footing they will enter the service of His Britannic Majesty; the writer would much rather have stayed at Nienburg.
Still open
About twenty word signs of the Dutch postscript, several of them recurring (one nine times, one seven) and still workable from context, the rest seen once; and a faded line on each page. The postscript has 90.7% of its tokens valued but does not yet build sentences in its first half (ll. 2–12), so it is decoded, not read (reading audit, 22–23 October 2026). The French page builds coherent sentences but its word-divided reading reads at 89.6% of its words (measured 24 October 2026), under the 95% a complete reading needs.

01 The document

  • Record: DECODE R1892, “unkmown (Germany) to Prince William V”, 1 Jan 1795, Non-decrypted, two images (photocopies, photographed sideways).
  • Shelfmark: Koninklijk Huisarchief, The Hague, archive of Prince William V, bundel A18, inv. 339.
  • Languages: page 1 French, entirely in cipher; page 2 a Dutch postscript (“het volgende schrift … in het hollandsch met cijffer”), cipher mixed with clear words, closing with a clear French sentence.
  • Keys tried first: the two key records DECODE files in the same series, R2240 (the four-digit Orange name key) and R2233, do not apply: R1892 uses only the digits 1–6.

The postscript opens with “den generaal Bertincy” (probably Bentinck).

The postscript deals with keeping the corps together, naming and placing the officers, giving those who will not serve a fixed number of days to choose, and the writer’s weeks without an answer to his first letter.

04 Who and when

The writer calls William V “mon père” and asks that the cipher be shown to “mon frère”, so he is one of the Stadholder’s two sons. The addressee is an officer managing the rassemblement, the Dutch troops who followed the House of Orange into Germany in British pay after the French conquest of January 1795.

Colenbrander’s Gedenkstukken (vol. II, 1795–1798; searched in full text on 2 October 2026) does not print R1892 but fixes its setting. No. 724, of 7 October 1795, is Prince Frederick writing to General Dundas from Nienburg, where he commanded the rassemblement, and the writer of the Dutch postscript would much rather have stayed at “Nam..nburg”, Nienburg (M). On 26 November 1795 the Princess writes that her younger son has arrived in England, “c’est aussi pour le rassemblement qu’il est venu” (p. 893). The writer is therefore Prince Frederick, in England, and “mon frère” is the Hereditary Prince.

No. 734 (the Hereditary Prince to William V, 25–27 December 1795) reports that Henry Dundas’s new orders of 8 December had decided against the rassemblement; troops not taking British service were to go to Nassau. Page 1 still anticipates that decision (“au cas que le général Dundas vous déclarât … que les troupes devroient se laisser embarquer”), so it was written before the orders reached the continent: late November or early December 1795. DECODE’s “1 January 1795”, before the flight of the House of Orange, cannot be the date of the letter. “To William V” in the record reflects where the letter is kept, not its addressee.

05 Method

  1. Fetched the record and its two images, and the key records R2240 and R2233, from DECODE with a session login.
  2. Turned and cut the photocopies into strips; two transcription passes (one per page) wrote each column as a TOP+BOTTOM token, clear words in brackets and signs by name: 1,039 pairs on page 1, 790 on page 2.
  3. Page 2’s pairs, split at every sign and clear word, went to a simulated-annealing solver scoring Dutch quadgrams from the shared corpus, with a one-to-one key (swap moves). A many-to-one first run collapsed onto e and n and was discarded. The one-to-one run gave hetrassemblementtezamentehouden and officieren at once.
  4. The same square applied unchanged to page 1 gave French text; four values were adjusted from it (54 = q, 14 = x, 13 = y, 12 = z in French).
