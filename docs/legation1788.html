The letters
William Short to Jefferson, The Hague, 26 July 1792 (private); William Carmichael to Jefferson, Madrid, 14 April 1788; Thomas Pinckney to Jefferson, London, 28 August 1793. Each is partly in a numerical code. Six more letters in Pinckney’s code were added in November 2026: Pinckney from Spain and Paris in 1795, William Eaton from Tunis in 1801–02, Tobias Lear in 1813.
Where
Library of Congress, Thomas Jefferson Papers, series 1 (Short: microfilm reel 16, frames 998–1009; Carmichael: reel 9, frames 223–230) and National Archives, RG 59, Despatches from US Ministers to Great Britain (microfilm M30 roll 2, frame 140). Catalogue no. 484. The added letters: National Archives microfilm M31 roll 7 and T303 roll 2, and the Library of Congress, James Monroe Papers, reel 5.
The cipher
Three different two-part codes of about 1,700–1,850 numbers for words, syllables and letters, written with dots between the groups; the numbers follow no alphabetical order.
How it was deciphered
Jefferson decoded most of Short’s letter between the lines, and the editors of The Papers of Thomas Jefferson decoded the rest and the other two letters. Here every group was transcribed from the originals and checked against values decoded at the time in other letters in the same codes. The six added letters carry a decipherment of the time between the lines, or, for one, a translation printed in 1802.
What it says
Short’s bitterness at being passed over for Paris and his wishes for a post; the ratification of the Constitution in Maryland and Massachusetts; Grey’s expedition preparing for the West Indies. In the added letters: Spain’s delays over the treaty of 1795, Eaton’s plans against Tripoli and Tunis, the Bey of Tunis asking for a frigate, and the Spanish Regency’s talk of war in 1813.
Still open
Ten groups in the three letters: Short’s 401 (twice), 1083 and 359 199 34 724; Pinckney’s 447 (how many regiments) and the first group of a general’s name; Carmichael’s 597. In the added letters, line ends hidden in the binding and some blotted words.
Credit
The readings were first printed by the editors of The Papers of Thomas Jefferson (vols. 13, 1956; 24, 1990; 26, 1995). Jefferson’s own interlinear decoding and George Taylor Jr.’s decodings of Pinckney’s other letters supplied the values used to check them. Andrew Beling, working with AI agents, found Code No. 16 in the six later letters, transcribed them, rebuilt 537 groups of the code and corrected the grading of the 1793 passage (November 2026).

01 Three codes, three letters

As Secretary of State, Jefferson corresponded with his ministers abroad in numerical codes that the editors of his papers number in the order they first appear. Each is a two-part code: some 1,700 to 1,850 numbers, each standing for a word, a syllable or a letter, written with dots between them. The numbers follow no order. On Short’s page, 7 is s, 68 president, 153 al, 224 the, 426 to, 977 no, 1093 in and 1336 be, so an unknown number cannot be placed between its neighbours. No key sheet for these three codes has been found. The editors decoded the letters “using partially reconstructed” keys and named the groups they could not read.

02 Pinckney, 28 August 1793: Grey’s expedition

Pinckney’s despatch is on the State Department microfilm of his London letters. Jefferson pencilled “copy so far only” above the coded lines, so the clerks’ copies stop there and no decoding was made at the time. The coded passage is five lines, 58 groups; the first digits of four lines are lost in the binding.

Pinckney wrote six other letters in the same code that George Taylor Jr. decoded between the lines at the State Department (20 June, 25 September, 27 November and 17 December 1793, 28 January and February 1794). He also sent an “Explication of the Cyphers” for his letter of 25 November 1793. All fifteen coded frames of the roll were transcribed (about 2,600 groups), and every value decoded there was set against the 28 August groups. Thirty of the 58 groups were confirmed that way, word for word as the editors had them: 753 i (reg-i-ments), 1490 1517 to be, 709 with, 323+ generals (+ marks a plural), 856 and, 513 525 under him, 885 is, 175 thought, 1226 for. The later letters in the same code (section 05) confirm nine more, which makes 39: 923 are (twice), 234 time, 501 secret, 1328 1374 Si-r, 668 at, 1010 tin (des-tin-ation) and 675 ly.

447 regiments are to embark in a short time on a secret expedition. They are to be commanded by Sir Charles Grey with Generals Stuart and [?55]ats under him. Their destination is generally thought to be for the West Indies.

The group the editors printed as 497 is 447 on the original. It occurs nowhere else in Pinckney’s code, and none of the number words decoded elsewhere matches it (6 is 1244, 8 is 531, “nine” is 383 or 6?4), so how many regiments he wrote stays open. The editors’ guesses time (234) and secret (501) occur nowhere else in Pinckney’s London letters, but both stand under those words in Tobias Lear’s letter of 1813, so they are no longer guesses. Their third guess does not hold as spelled. The second general’s name is three groups, ?55 668 800, and the editors printed [Dunda]s; 668 is at in Eaton’s letter of 1801, Pinckney’s of 1795 and the Bey’s of 1802, so the groups give a name ending in -at-s, and its first group is in the binding. Because Sir is 1328 1374, Charles is the single group 672. The long letter of 15 August 1793, about a thousand groups with no interlinear decoding, is not open: the State Department made a clear copy of it.

03 Short, 26 July 1792: two holes in a long complaint

Short’s private letter is a long complaint at being passed over for Paris in favour of Gouverneur Morris, and he coded its most personal parts. All 674 code groups were transcribed; Jefferson’s decoding stands over 511 of them, and the editors read the rest. Two places are open.

This would have been putting on my conduct a censure which no place would have induced me to have exposed myself to, if I had not counted enough on the justice of government to have 401 [been?] sure there 1083 [would?] have 401 [been?] no[ne?] of it if my conduct was satisfactory.

The next day Short copied the whole paragraph into a second letter, in the same code and with the same numbers, so the digits are certain. He first wrote 757.1312.687 (salaries) there, struck it out, and wrote 757.427 (salary). But the copy carries no decoding. “Been” is 1700 in this code (Jefferson decoded it in this letter and three others), so if 401 is been, it is a second number for the word. The obvious would is 1192, not 1083. Short’s small caret under 401 also appears under 985 (less), so it does not single out 401. None of the six numbers occurs in the other Code No. 10 letters checked: the editors’ notes on all 39 documents in the code, and the originals of Short’s letters of 26 September and 27 October 1790 and 11 and 13 November 1793, and of Jefferson’s fully coded letter of 31 May 1793 (about 1,100 groups).

04 Carmichael, 14 April 1788: a test of a faulty code

Carmichael could not decode Jefferson’s letters with the code David Franks had left with him, and he used “some phrases of my cypher” so that Jefferson could see whether their codes matched. Five numbers stand in a clear letter:

Had I been in [Maryland] I should in all probability have been of the sentiment of Paca and the other you mention. … [Maryland] has not acceded to the Constitution, [Massachusetts] has. The [majority] seem pleased with it, what says 597?

This is not Code No. 11, the code Carmichael used from May 1788: in the table rebuilt from Jefferson’s decodings (jefferson1787), 597 is freedom and 223 trouble. No other letter in Code No. 13 is known, so only the context reads it. That context is strong for 108, Carmichael’s own state and William Paca’s; Maryland ratified on 28 April 1788, two weeks later. It is also strong for 223, Massachusetts, which had ratified on 6 February. For 597 the editors’ you is a guess, and Virginia, which had not yet voted, fits the sentence as well. It stays open.

05 Code No. 16 in six later letters

In November 2026 Andrew Beling, working with AI agents, showed that Pinckney’s London code went on being used. Weber’s study of American diplomatic codes (1979, p. 155) calls it the “663” code, after its number for the, and lists it as not located. Beling found it in six more letters. All but one carry a decipherment written between the lines at the time:

That the six letters use Pinckney’s code can be checked without Beling’s key. The value table made here in October holds only values from Pinckney’s letters of 1793–94. Set against the single-group words of the new letters, it agrees on 61 of 70 in Lear’s letter, 177 of 178 in the Bey’s, 29 of 36 in Eaton’s of 1801, 66 of 70 and 42 of 44 in Pinckney’s of 1795, and 21 of 28 in Eaton’s of 22 October 1802. The best of 200 shuffled tables agrees on between 2 and 20. The misses are syllables and inflections: 541 is an, not another.

The letters themselves:

  • Pinckney, 1795. The coded phrases of his reports from Spain while he negotiated the treaty with Spain. The court is “still anxious for further delay, which is to them equivalent to a cession of our rights so long as we shall acquiesce therein”. There is talk of “a triple alliance between france, Spain and ourselves” and of a guarantee of Spain’s American possessions. In October he “had asked for my passports to return”.
  • Eaton, 25 May 1801. Tripoli has declared war. In code Eaton proposes to seize the Tripolitan commodore, “a British Renegade, Morad Rais; Alias, Peter Lisle”, by approaching the corsairs under English colours, and to go on board the American squadron himself.
  • The Bey of Tunis, 8 September 1802. The Bey thanks Jefferson for the stores and jewels and asks for “a good frigate of thirty six guns”. Eaton put the Bey’s letter into code and added a postscript of his own: “If the Bey’s letter should provoke resentment, Porto Farina may be taken by surprise” and the Bey’s arsenal and ships destroyed. He repeated it in his letter of 22 October.
  • Lear, January 1813. The Spanish Regency has laid before the Cortes “an exposition of the conduct of the United States toward Spain” and would recommend “a declaration of war instantly, if the actual circumstances of this Kingdom did not forbid it”. The sheet reached Washington under a blank cover, and a note on it says “Mr Lears Cyp[her] was used to read it”.

From these letters Beling rebuilt 537 groups of the code, each graded: 158 stand alone under one word of a decipherment; 192 are the only entry of Weber’s printed word list of some 1,600 elements that fits the words they occur in; 125 were taken by aligning Pinckney’s undecoded London letters to their printed texts; 62 are taken from the October table. By his record, he froze a key of 311 groups before testing it (the key and the declaration reached us together, so the order rests on his timestamps). On Pinckney’s 1795 passages it gave the clerk’s word for 93 of 105 groups (0.886, against 0.105 for the best shuffled key), which passes the bar of 0.85 set beforehand. On Eaton’s letter of 1801 it gave 48 of 60 (0.80), under the bar; four of the twelve misses are right syllables that the scoring rule does not credit. On Eaton’s passage of 22 October 1802 it gave 44 of 49 (0.898), but that passage repeats the postscript of 9 October, so it is not new text. His re-implemented scoring script, re-run here, gives 94 of 107 (0.879) and 51 of 64 (0.797), the same verdicts; the original runs were not kept, and the 22 October score could not be re-run here.

Earlier work, as Beling found it: the Madison Papers editors deciphered Eaton’s postscript and Lear’s letter with a key from the Lear family papers that was never printed, and the Jefferson Papers editors used a partial reconstruction of their own, also unprinted. He found no one who had said that Pinckney’s London code and the code of Eaton and Lear are one. (The editions were not opened for this page.) His key also decodes 81% of the 1,982 groups in Pinckney’s London letters that have no decoding of the time, 62% without the values taken from those letters or from the October table. That decode is not yet built into sentences and is not shown here; the editions print most of those letters in clear.

06 How it was done

  • The three printed texts and notes were taken from Founders Online. The originals came from the Library of Congress (Jefferson Papers master images) and from the National Archives catalogue (M30 roll 2, frames 101–330).
  • Every code group of the three letters was transcribed from the images, and all fifteen coded Pinckney frames of 1793–95 with their decodings. Subagents did the long transcriptions; the open groups were re-read at 2× by hand.
  • The decoded values of each code were collected in key.tsv: 171 values of Code No. 10 from Jefferson’s interlinear, 262 of Code No. 16 from Taylor’s decodings and the Explication. Each group of the three letters was then graded: decoded over the group or elsewhere in the same code (H), the editors’ reading only (M), a conjecture by context (I), or open.
  • For the six later letters, Beling transcribed the numbers and the words above them from the National Archives and Library of Congress images, the 1801 letter from both copies and the 22 October 1802 passage twice, by two readers working apart and without a key. The texts below were put together from his files and read through as sentences. Four words of Eaton’s 1801 letter where the interlinear was doubtfully transcribed (one of them blotted) are marked doubtful.
  • Each open group was searched for in every letter of its code that could be reached. Code No. 10 was also checked for alphabetical order, and Code No. 11 was tried on Carmichael’s numbers; neither helps.

07 What remains

  • Pinckney 447: before “regiments”; one occurrence, no decoding, not among the code’s known number words, and in none of the six later letters. Pinckney’s own press copy, in the Pinckney Family Papers at the South Carolina Historical Society, is not online.
  • Pinckney ?55 668 800: the second general’s name, ending -at-s by the code. The first digits of ?55 are in the binding.
  • Short 401 (twice), 1083, 359 199 34 724: only in this passage and its copy of the next day; no decoding in any Code No. 10 letter reached. Several originals with decodings are outside the Library of Congress series (William & Mary, Princeton, the Short Papers, the State Department copies) and were not seen.
  • Carmichael 597, and 108, 223, 273 by context only: Code No. 13 survives in this letter alone, and no key is known.
  • The later letters: line ends of Pinckney’s letter of 28 October 1795 are hidden in the binding and the last coded line of 18 December is in shadow; six words over Eaton’s passage of 22 October 1802 are blotted. Both texts are partial.
  • The code itself: 537 of about 1,600 groups have a value, and 125 of those come from alignment to printed texts. The original key from the Lear family papers has not been printed.

I had seen others younger than myself, and under other less favorable circumstances, employed in equal or higher stations from countries where they had many more competitors. It would be still worse in Spain, less so in Paris and London, because there every body and particularly such characters are less in view.

I have often reason to wish my grade here was plenipotentiary even with my salary. I was formerly of a contrary opinion, but experience has convinced me of my error. It would be much more honourable both for myself and America for several reasons. In Spain it would be particularly to be desired on account of the forms of the principal court. What would be worse than all would be to be turned away from here without any place, as it would be like a laquay turned away without even a certificate of honesty; and yet after what has happened in the late appointments I should not be surprized at it. The machine being now properly mounted at Amsterdam, it may be conducted from Paris or London, and the place here may be thought useless. I have one consolation however, that I have done every thing in my power in this and every other case to deserve better treatment. If others after employing the same time in serving themselves are to reap the benefit of honour I cannot help it. I only wish that in future the public may feel that their services are in like manner solely devoted to them. After being employed in a business of such a nature, my being now turned out of service altogether, after others being called in who had not before been employed, is only saved perhaps by the accident of Carmichael's asking leave to retire. This paper enters generally into the conduct adopted by the Agent of the United States resident here, the possession of East Florida, the numerous incendiaries employed by the Executive of the United States in different parts of their american possessions, naming several, particularly a doctor Robinson; the forces collecting near baton rouge, as intended to join the insurgents in Mexico: but more particularly the correspondence of the Chavalier Onis, which exhibits many unfounded speculations and false conclusions upon subjects that he is totally unacquainted with, or which he has viewed thro a false medium.

The Regency concludes with saying that they should recommend a declaration of war instantly, if the actual circumstances of this Kingdom did not forbid it, and the hope that Clinton, the friend of Onis and of Spain and her ally, would succeed to the President [?before]; in which case the alternative would be unnecessary.

This Paper was, in secret session, committed to a commission to report thereon. It is supposed no report will be made for some considerable time, say two months. I am led to think that neither the British Minister nor Lord Wellington had any thing to do in this affair, which I deem of too much importance not to make an attempt to communicate it.
