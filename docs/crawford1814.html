Crawford to Monroe, Paris, 5 November 1814: a private letter in code

→

The coded passages of the American minister’s private letter to the Secretary of State, on the restored French monarchy and a coming crisis, deciphered with the code table Weber printed in 1979.

The letter
William H. Crawford, US minister to France, to James Monroe, Secretary of State, Paris, 5 November 1814, headed “(Despatch No 33-2) Private”, in Crawford’s own hand. About a third of it is in code: 292 groups in 39 lines, with clear words written among them.
Where
US National Archives, RG 59, Despatches from US Ministers to France vol. 15, microfilm M34 roll 18, frames 0063R–0067L; the code is on 0064R, 0065R and 0066L. No decipherment is on the film.
The cipher
A State Department code of about 1,600 numbers for words and syllables, printed by Ralph E. Weber as WE025 (1355 = the, 1397 = of), with small marks on the groups for -s, -ed, dropped and doubled letters.
How it was deciphered
Weber’s table, transcribed and frozen for Poinsett’s letters in the same code, applied as our machine-read copy (16 of its 156 rows used here were repaired by hand; it was not compared with the book row by row). Two blind transcriptions agree on 291 of 292 groups; the reading passes a shuffle test written down before the run. 223 of the 246 words of the coded passages (90.7%) give the key’s output, under the 95% bar.
What it says
Crawford’s belief that the French government will “sacrifice every thing for the friendship of England”, a conspiracy reported around Savary, and three parties ready if there is a struggle. It is his report, not established fact.
Still open
Nine groups supplied from the sense where the number as written gives another word, letters and words supplied where no group gives them, and any decipherment made at the time, which was not found.
Credit
Code table: Ralph E. Weber, United States Diplomatic Codes and Ciphers, 1775–1938 (1979). Found, transcribed and read by AI agents working for Andrew Beling, who set the task and the rules; reading review by Brice Dudout, assisted by an AI model.

01 The letter

Crawford wrote two letters to Monroe on 5 November 1814. The official Despatch No. 33 begins on frame 0061R and is in clear. This private one follows it on 0063R. Its clear opening is about the American letter of marque Decatur; the first groups come in the middle of a sentence on 0064R and runs, between clear passages, to 0066L. Two groups Crawford struck through are left out.

What the code says, in Crawford’s words (bracketed words are supplied, see What remains open): “I am inclined to believe that this government has come to a determination to sacrifice every thing for the friendship of England.” The King, “so far from viewing with an eye of jealousy the augmentation of British troops [and] others in British pay” in Belgium, looks to that force for protection against his own people. A conspiracy is reported, with Savary at its head; those opposed to the government [say] it is “the creature of the police”, fabricated to justify the arrest of about thirty persons the court is afraid of.

On 0065R things are “approaching [a] crisis which must be decis[ive] of the fate [of] the King and right[s] of the nation”; the rights of the nation will most probably be lost without [a] struggle, but many believe “that the royal famil[y] will again be expelled”. On 0066L he sets out the sides: the army would be for recalling the Emperor; the part of the new nobility opposed to the army for the King of Rom[e] with [a] regen[c]y, or for the Duke of Orleans; the genuine republicans would join either of the la[s]t spec[ified] projects.

These are Crawford’s report of what was believed and said in Paris. None of it is checked here against other sources.

02 The code and its marks

The groups are in the code Weber prints in full as WE025 (pp. 443–454). It is the code of Poinsett’s letters from Chile, and Weber notes that Crawford used it in 1814. The 156 rows of the table for the numbers in this letter are in key/we025_crawford1814.tsv, cut from the transcription of Weber’s table that was frozen by checksum for the Poinsett work before this letter was decoded. The table was not changed for this letter; it is our machine-read copy of Weber’s page, of which 140 rows read as printed and 16 were repaired by hand (including 812, 780 and 791, which sit under unsettled or supplied readings), and it was not compared with the book row by row. What the repairs were checked against is not recorded in the sources.

Crawford marks many groups, and the diamond, caret and cross follow the directions Weber prints after the table (p. 454): a diamond above a group adds -s; a caret makes -ed; a cross under a figure doubles a letter. The strokes under a group are read here as dropping that many final letters; Weber’s notes say only that the tick withdraws the corresponding letter, and the clerk’s “expulsion” for one letter supports the reading (1036 crisis with three strokes is “cri”, 1046 spire with one is “spir”, in con-spir-acy); the cross cases are 1115 al is “all” in rec-all-ing, 372 gle is “ggle” in stru-ggle; u and v are interchangeable, so dis-cou-er-ed is “discovered”. The marks were first read from their use, and the printed directions were seen on 15 November 2026, after the reading was made.

03 How it was deciphered

  1. A scan of the text layer of the National Archives catalogue’s France despatches flagged frames 0064–0066 of roll 18 as runs of code with no decipherment between the lines; the frames were fetched at full size.
  2. Two AI readers transcribed the 292 groups from the images, neither seeing the other’s work. They agree on 291 of the numbers (they also differed on the owner of six marks between lines and on two struck-through groups); the one difference (307 or 317) was settled on the image as 307 (ge-nu-in-e).
  3. WE025 was applied unchanged with decode.py: 283 of the 292 groups have a table value that the reading uses, with Crawford’s marks applied; the other nine are supplied from the sense and bracketed. Letters and words are also supplied at the places listed under What remains open, so by words the share is lower (the replay counts 281 groups exact; the difference from 283 was not traced). Counted by words, with the key replay (replay.py), 223 of the 246 words of the coded passages give the key’s output (90.7%), under the 95% bar, so the extent is partial (see What remains open).
  4. A shuffle test written down before the run: the decode scores −0.909 on a letter-trigram model, against a mean of −1.191 and a best of −1.067 for 200 tables made by shuffling WE025’s elements (z = 5.7; the shuffle test ignores the marks and was not rerun after the diamond over 1025 was added). A first run had a parsing bug and was repaired and rerun once; both outputs are kept. The project’s refit test (docs/_validate.py) is underdetermined on so short a text: a key fitted to shuffled text scores as well.
  5. The reading was graded A by B. Dudout, assisted by an AI model, as complete and coherent English (a grade of the English, made on 15 November, before the changes of 16 November); the model compared lines 64R01, 64R02, 64R05 and 64R06 with the image and found them to agree group for group. Weber’s table itself was not re-checked against the book in that review. All the transcription and reading work was done by AI agents; no person has checked the whole transcription against the images (the maintainer’s review of the pull request did catch the diamond over 1025 on frame 0065).

04 A check from September 1814

After the reading was made, two earlier private letters of Crawford’s were found on the same roll with short coded passages that a State Department clerk deciphered between the lines. Their 81 groups were set against the tables. On frames 0031R–0032L (26 September 1814 as read, WE025), 66 of 68 give the clerk’s word: one miss was an error in the project’s transcription of Weber’s table (1181 is “pl” in the book, not “Pi”; 1181 is not in the 5 November letter), the other a blotted figure. On frame 0025R (mid-September, in the older code WE028), all 13 agree. The clerk reads the hooked tail under a group as a withdrawn last letter and the diamond as -s, as the printed directions say, and with the printed directions that let “troops”, first supplied from the sense, be read from the code (the cross under 1116 is not attested by the clerk). He also copied a one-number slip of Crawford’s literally, the kind of slip supposed for some of the supplied groups. This check came after the fact and had no pass mark set in advance.

05 What remains open

Nine groups are supplied from the sense, where the number as written gives another word:

  • 1512, four times, written where “a” is needed: the table has 1512 = our, 1215 = a (the same digits in another order). Not settled.
  • 1276 (fish) read as y in “sa-y”: 1275 = y; the notes say the image shows 1276, and the transcription marks one digit as doubtful.
  • 556 (late) read as e in “Rom[e]”: 1556 = e, the first digit left out.
  • 361 (run) read as [these]: 561 = these.
  • 1474 (cy) read as [any]: 1472 = any.
  • 812 (june) with one stroke, read as “decis[ive]”: not settled.

Other places where the reading goes beyond the table as written, all marked in crawford1814_reading.md: [be brought by] supplied on 0066L, where Crawford’s sentence lacks a verb; 556 with one stroke read as la[s]t (lat[ter] is possible); 791 speci read as spec[ified] (the bracket also takes in the i the group gives); 413 ma read as ma[de]; 780 right read as right[s]; and the cross under the first figure of 1116 read as doubling the first letter, which the printed direction does not cover. A few letters were also mended in the reading where no mark is written, and are now marked there: 1046 spire without its stroke in [conspiracy] on 64R12; 739 mean as mean[s]; re-ult as re[s]ult; fam-ili as famil[y]; 578, which the table copy gives as “eney”, as the end of regen[c]y; and two words with no group under them, [and] in “troops [and] others” (0064R) and [of] in “the fate [of] the King” (0065R). “Very”, written in clear before 1025 thing, is read as “every” and left unmarked, as clear words are.

The extent is counted by words. The groups run on without word division, so the words are those of the reading that the groups carry. The key replay gives 225 of 241 as the key’s output (93.4%), with every difference marked. Every other word counts as a hole: the nine supplied groups, right[s], ma[de], la[s]t, spec[ified], and the mended cipher words above. The replay takes Rom[e] and [conspiracy] for the key’s output, though a letter is supplied or left out in each, and does not count the five words the reading supplies where no group stands, [be brought by] (three words), [and] and [of]; all seven are counted here as holes. That gives 223 of 246 words, 90.7%, under the 95% bar: the extent is partial. The small diamond over 1025 on 0065R (“Things”), missed in both transcriptions, was added after the maintainer’s review pointed to it.

No decipherment of the time and no printing of the coded passages was found: none on the film (frames 0059–0070), nor in Founders Online, American State Papers, the 1902 calendar of Monroe’s papers at the Library of Congress, Mooney (1974), Willson (1928), Shipp (1909), Tomokiyo’s Cryptiana, or phrase searches of HathiTrust and Google Books. Not seen: the clerk’s clear fair copy (only its last page, 0067R, is on the roll), the Crawford papers at the Library of Congress, the Monroe papers at the New York Public Library, and Green’s Life of William Harris Crawford (1965).

06 The setting

Crawford, minister to France from 1813, wrote to Madison on 1 November 1814 that the Decatur had lain fifty days below Nantes without leave to enter while British vessels came in, and that he meant to keep away from the court. In the clear part of his private letter of 21 October (frame 0060L) he had already expected “another civil commotion” in France within six months. The coded part of this letter gives his private reading of the restored monarchy’s weakness.

# Crawford to Monroe, Paris, 5 Nov 1814, "(Despatch No 33-2) Private": the coded passages as sentences
# English, so no translation. NARA M34 roll 18, frames 0063R-0067L; code on 0064R, 0065R and 0066L. Line labels as in
# crawford1814_transcription.txt. This is Crawford's report to the Secretary of State of what he believed and what was
# said in Paris; the conspiracy, the parties and their plans are his account, not established fact.
# Marks: [word] = a word supplied where the group as written gives another table value or none (each listed in
# NOTES.md section 3); decis[ive] = letters supplied inside a word; [conspiracy] = a word whose group gives a letter
# the reading leaves out. Words written in clear among the groups are part of
# the text and not marked; [...] = clear text not given here (the Decatur affair, the Duke de Brancas at the barracks).

## 0064R (after the clear account of the Decatur affair on 0063R-0064L)

I am inclined to believe that this government has come to a determination to sacrifice every thing for the
friendship of England. The use of the Telegraphs has been granted to Lord Wellington, to enable him to ascertain with
the greatest dispatch what passes in Belgium. The King, so far from viewing with an eye of jealousy the augmentation
of British troops [and] others in British pay in that country, looks to that force for protection against his own
people. It is reported and believed that a conspiracy has been discovered, at the head of which they have placed
Savary. Those opposed to the government [say] that this [conspiracy] is the creature of the police, fabricated to
countenance the arrest of about thirty persons, of whom the court is afraid. [A] report also prevails to day that the
discussions at Vienna have assumed a hostile tone [...]

## 0065R (after the clear account of the war minister's orders and the Duke de Brancas at the barracks)

[...] His grace made a precipitate retreat. Things are approaching [a] crisis which must be decis[ive] of the fate [of]
the King and right[s] of the nation. It is impossible to form a correct opinion of the re[s]ult of this struggle. It is
indeed most probable that the rights of the nation will be lost without [a] struggle. This however is not the common
opinion, among many of the best friends of the new order of things. Many believe that the royal famil[y] will again be
expelled, but none of them appear to have any distinct view of the mean[s] by which it is to be effected.

## 0066L

The army would be for recalling the Emperor. The republicans will with difficulty [be brought by] that circumstance to
consent to this. The great mass of the senate would be compelled to emigrate if any struggle is ma[de]. Those who make
it will form three parties. I have already stated the designs of the army. Such of the new nobility as may be opposed
to the views of the army will be for the King of Rom[e] with [a] regen[c]y, or they will be in favor of elevating the
Duke of Orleans to the throne. The great difficulty will be to reconcile [these] three different parties. The genuine
republicans will unite in either of the la[s]t spec[ified] projects, if [any] effort should be made.
