Synthetic cipher experiment · 23 November 2026

How fast could modern AI have solved the Zodiac 340?

Hand an AI the original sheet and it may remember the answer. We built a fresh cipher to find out how quickly it could discover one.

Try a blind challengeSee the timing

One question, three attempts

David Oranchak, Sam Blake, and Jarl Van Eycke solved the historical Z340 in December 2020, 51 years after it was mailed to the San Francisco Chronicle. Our experiment started with their hardest problem: how do you discover the reading order when several symbols can also stand for the same letter? The solvers’ reconstruction

The original Z340

The AI recognized the nine-row blocks. Its English scorer preferred lots of friends, yet it tried the remembered lots of fun.

Prior knowledge spoiled the timing.

The clock-shop cipher

A fresh solver read all 391 letters. Reversing each row and shifting its starting point unlocked the entire story.

One rule worked everywhere. Inspect the grid

Three new 340s

We kept the historical grid size and concealed changes of direction inside it. A deliberate copying slip disturbed one row in each puzzle.

Finding the opening left a second problem.

What counted as blind?

Each Codex solver received one grid, the language, and eight minutes. It could write code and score its own sample English. Instructions excluded the web, external language data, and answer files. Agents shared a filesystem with the setter, so blindness relied on those instructions. SHA-256 commitments fixed the answers before dispatch.

The pattern that opened C

All three solvers withheld a plaintext at eight minutes. A found a misleading route. B cracked its own practice cipher but stalled on the challenge. C found two breaks, after rows 10 and 18, and a promising diagonal on either side. A second solver took those clues and recovered the key.

Trace the reading order

Follow a symbol across the grid’s edges. The highlight follows the printed ciphertext, including the displaced symbol in row 14.

  • Rows 1–10 · Up one, right one. Wrap within the block.
  • Rows 11–18 · Move row 14’s last symbol into fifth place, then go up one, right two.
  • Rows 19–20 · Read row 20 backwards, then row 19 backwards.
Why try diagonals?

Across the first ten rows, a window of 40 symbols contained an average of 31.71 distinct symbols in row order. Along the proposed diagonal, the mean rose to 35.27. The next eight rows showed a similar increase. That signal gave the second solver a route to test before either solver had readable words.

The follow-up had no time limit. The clock-shop solver recovered a passage about a piano tuner. Errors clustered in one physical row, revealing where to look for the copying slip. It submitted the full route, correction, and all 63 mappings before seeing the answer.

Read the recovered plaintext
For weeks no one had touched the piano in the empty hall. They sent me to open the hall for the piano tuner. He set his bag on the floor and lifted the lid. One key made a dry little rattle each time he struck it. A green marble lay in a fold of yellow paper inside. He rubbed the dust off with his sleeve and smiled. Then he told me he had lost it there as a boy. I never saw anyone so pleased to finish a small repair. I was glad to be there.

The elapsed time includes the handoff and overlapping work. We pursued C because it had produced useful leads. A and B received only the timed attempt. The historical 51 years spanned many separate human efforts. What we can measure here is how quickly the solvers turned a promising pattern in one newly constructed cipher into a verified reading.
