The document
Giovanni Maria Malvezzi, Ferdinand I’s resident in Constantinople, copied three letters of Henry II to Suleiman the Magnificent into Latin and cipher, with his own notes in Italian: the letter of credence for the sieur d’Huyson, a long letter on the truce, and a third brought by a captain. The letters are of autumn 1547; the copy went to Ferdinand as an enclosure to Malvezzi’s dispatch of 8 March 1548.
Where
Österreichisches Staatsarchiv, Haus-, Hof- und Staatsarchiv, Vienna, Staatenabteilungen Türkei I 7-2-18, fols. 143r–146r, 207 lines of cipher. The archive’s catalogue marks it “Inhalt nicht dechiffriert”. Catalogue entry 500 (the other Türkei I files stay open there).
The cipher
Malvezzi’s alphabet: two to four graphic signs for each letter, numbers for doubled letters, null syllables and null Latin words, and a few code syllables (Bi the King of France, Ras the Sultan, Ab the French).
How it was deciphered
Brice Dudout took Malvezzi’s alphabet from DECODE R367 and R386, calibrated it on another enciphered copy of the series whose decipherment of the time survives (Türkei I 7-3-20), froze it, tested it blind on an unglossed letter of 1550, and then transcribed and deciphered 7-2-18 line by line on the archive’s images.
What it says
Henry II accredits d’Huyson, asks the Sultan to have France’s allies, the Swiss first, included in the truce Charles V and Ferdinand have just made with the Porte, warns that the Emperor’s ratification smuggles in the lapsed promises of Crépy, and asks for the Ottoman fleet. Malvezzi adds that the French present their king’s letters in Turkish and undated, “per posser negare”.
Still open
18 unresolved groups in the contributor’s count (27 places marked as open in the established text, some of them part of a word), and 86 readings given as probable; the captain’s name rests on one sign.
Credit
Transcription, decipherment, Latin and Italian text and English translation by Brice Dudout, with Claude Opus 5.5 agents, 27 October 2026; his write-up on Europe chiffrée. The key is Malvezzi’s alphabet as filed on DECODE (R367, R386); the calibration used the decipherment made at the time of Türkei I 7-3-20.

01 The copy

In the autumn of 1547 Henry II, king for six months, sent his pantler, the sieur d’Huyson (the copy spells him Duson, Dusson and Dussun), to Constantinople with a letter of credence for Suleiman and instructions; the French ambassador at the Porte was Gabriel d’Aramon. Charles V and Ferdinand had just obtained a five-year truce from the Porte, signed at Adrianople in June 1547. Henry wanted his allies included in it and the Emperor’s good faith put in doubt. Ferdinand’s resident at the Porte, Giovanni Maria Malvezzi, got hold of the texts and sent them to his master in cipher.

The copy fills four leaves, fols. 143r–146r, 207 lines. It has six parts: a two-line Italian heading by Malvezzi; the letter of credence (143r ll. 3–17); a note by Malvezzi on how the French handle their king’s letters at the Porte (143r ll. 18–30); the long letter on the truce (143r l. 31 to 145v l. 15); a second short Italian heading; and the letter brought by a captain after d’Huyson (145v l. 18 to 146r l. 19). The archive’s catalogue dates the item about September 1547 and records that it came as an enclosure to Malvezzi’s letter to Ferdinand of 8 March 1548.

Malvezzi says how he made the Latin. The French, he writes, do not present the king’s letters in the form the Most Christian King wrote them: they have them put into Turkish language and script, adding and removing what they please, and leave out the date and place, so as to be able to deny them. He renders them dal turchesche in latino de uerbo ad uerbum. The Latin is therefore a back-translation from the Turkish, which explains its wavering between “we” and “I” and between “Your Majesty” and the third person, and the formula et {loco suo} before the signature, probably the place of the seal or the royal monogram in the Turkish version.

No image of the manuscript is reproduced on this page: the Austrian State Archives ask to be notified, and charge a fee, before reproductions of their holdings are published. The signs on this page are typed from the transcription.

02 The cipher and the key

The cipher is Malvezzi’s own, the one that also carries Ferdinand’s letter to him of 23 January 1548, which has its own page: Ferdinand I to Malvezzi, 1548. Each letter has two to four graphic signs: planetary and alchemical signs, Greek letters, and Latin letters used with other values (e for r, f for m, h for n, a for s). Doubled letters are numbers (12 tt, 13 ss, 16 nn). Nulls are of three kinds: syllables written as separate groups (Dam, Dim, Dem, Dom, Dum, Dar, Der, about 64 times in this copy), loose letters (b, d, n, z), and whole Latin words (cum, etiam, idcirco, atque, porro). The last make the reading treacherous, because cum and etiam also occur as real words, spelt in signs or not. A short repertory of code syllables covers the principals: Bi for the King of France, Ras for the Sultan, Ab for the French and Mes, probably for His Majesty, Malvezzi’s master.

The key is the alphabet filed on DECODE with Ferdinand’s letter (R367, Hadtörténelmi Levéltár 1548/3) and its second copy (R386, Széchényi Library, Quart. Lat. 2254). Brice Dudout calibrated it on another enciphered copy in the same Vienna series, Türkei I 7-3-20 f. 44, whose decipherment made at the time is on f. 45, and froze the result as K-C01 before opening 7-2-18 (key_KC01.json, with its SHA-256 fingerprint). Several sign shapes run close together, and a few signs carry two values that the Latin settles (n or o, g or s, e or u); the family of 8-like signs is told apart by its foot. Values added during the reading are justified in the reading notes; K-C01 itself was not changed.

03 The letter of credence

The credence, 143r ll. 3–17, as established by Brice Dudout. Braces mark probable readings, ‹…› an unresolved group; the copyist’s spelling is kept.

Serenissime Ottomanorum Imperator inuictissime, domine domine Sultan Soliman, mi frater carissime, salutem et dilectionis affectum, omni{que} bone fortune {et} felicitatis {continue} incrementum. Serenissime princeps et frater carissime, significamus qualiter istum nobilem et nobis sincere dilectum {credencerium} nomine Duson ipsum misimus cum literis nostris ad curiam ‹…›, {ut} {quam} primum accedat ad eius curiam simul cum oratore nostro Gabriele de Ramon ‹…› circa negotia magna que ore dicenda sunt; indubitatam fidem {prestare} debeat tanquam praesentialiter affuissem. Que quidem negotia, Serenissime, et nobis postea multum proderit; cura ut bene ualeat et diu feliciter uiuat, et {loco suo} Francie rex Enricus.

Most serene and most invincible Emperor of the Ottomans, lord, lord Sultan Suleiman, my dearest brother, greeting, the feeling of affection, all good fortune and continual increase of felicity. Most serene prince and dearest brother, we let you know that this nobleman, sincerely dear to us, our pantler named Duson, we have sent with our letters to [your] court ‹…›, that he may go as soon as possible to his court with our ambassador Gabriel de Ramon ‹…› on great matters that must be told by word of mouth; [Your Highness] is to give him undoubted faith, as if I were present in person. These matters, Most Serene, will also profit us greatly afterwards; take care to fare well and to live long and happily; and, {in its place}, Henry, King of France.

Ribier printed the French credence in 1666. It opens “Très-Haut, Très-Excellent … Soltan Soliman Sach” and names the bearer as the king’s beloved and loyal ordinary pantler, the sieur d’Huyson. The Latin opens “Serenissime Ottomanorum Imperator … mi frater carissime”: it does not translate that French, and comes back from the Turkish text actually delivered at the Porte.

04 The truce and the Swiss

The long letter, 143r l. 31 to 145v l. 15, about 1,100 words of Latin, is the case Henry II makes against the Habsburg truce. Its argument, in the order of the text:

  • The two truces. Francis I had obtained a one-year truce for Charles V and Ferdinand. Then, without his knowledge, their envoys came back and made a five-year peace; they came, the King says, to deceive the Sultan. When the Emperor sent envoys to ratify it, he wrote Henry literas lusorias et ridicolas, and the French ambassador at the Emperor’s court obtained a copy of the letters of the peace ab ipsis cancellariis regis Ispanie, which d’Huyson now carries to the Porte.
  • No oath, and Crépy at the end. The Emperor did not swear, as emperors and kings do. At the end of the ratification he slipped in the articles of the peace of Crépy (1544): the marriage of Henry’s brother Charles of Orléans to a daughter or niece of the Emperor with Milan as dowry, and France’s restitution of places taken in the wars. Charles of Orléans died in 1545 and Francis I in March 1547, so these promises have lapsed; the Emperor counted on the Porte having no interpreters able to explain them. The Sultan should not let them into the ratification.
  • The proper form. Had the Emperor meant the peace, he would have sent princes and great lords to confirm it, as he did when he made peace with the King of England.
  • The allies. The ratification should say that Henry, with his friends and confederates, stays in the peace, each keeping what he holds. The Swiss above all, michi iam multis annis confederati, raise armies for him; if the Emperor subdued them, France would be greatly weakened. If he will not confirm the article, the Sultan should not hold the peace as firm.
  • Hungary. The Emperor’s envoys promised thirty thousand florins for some castles and places of the kingdom of Hungary; had the Sultan held them and asked a hundred thousand, they would have paid it, since with those places they can recover the whole kingdom. Enemies are to be kept weak on every side.

The French instructions that Ribier prints ask for the inclusion of “the thirteen cantons of the Swiss leagues and the two of the Grey League”. The Latin develops the Swiss argument and does not name the Grey League; the group between populi and Suiceri is unresolved. The oath, the Crépy articles at the end of the ratification, the comparison with the English peace and the thirty thousand florins are not in the printed French.

05 The captain’s letter

Malvezzi’s second heading introduces lo spaccio quale ha portato un certo capitano {Bar[t]olamio} doppo Dussun, the dispatch carried by a Captain Bartolomeo after d’Huyson. In it Henry II tells the Sultan that the King of Spain is uir multa sciens, attamen deceptor; that he, his brother and the princes of Germany have held a great council in ciuitate Augusta, the Diet of Augsburg (September 1547 to June 1548), and are ready day and night for war. He asks the Sultan to keep his fleet ready at sea and to send it to his help if needed, promises the French fleet if the Spaniards raid Barbary, and confirms dominus de Ramon, Gabriel d’Aramon, as his ambassador. This third letter is not printed by Ribier as a letter to the Sultan.

The signs of the captain’s name give b-a-r-e-o l-a-m-i-o. Reading the fourth sign, very close in shape, as t (its value elsewhere on the page) gives Bartolamio, a Venetian form of Bartolomeo. A Captain Bartholomeo reached Venice with d’Huyson in March 1548, according to the ambassador Morvilliers in the letters printed by Charrière, and d’Aramon was expecting a “capitaine Barthélemy” at the Sultan’s camp.

06 How it was deciphered

  1. Starting key. Malvezzi’s alphabet from DECODE R367/R386, as used on this site for Ferdinand’s letter of 23 January 1548.
  2. Calibration. Fitted on the first lines of Türkei I 7-3-20 f. 44, another enciphered copy in the series, against its decipherment of the time on f. 45 (for instance an 8 stacked on a stem for e, and υ for l), then frozen as K-C01 before any reading of 7-2-18.
  3. Held-out test. On an unglossed copy of an enciphered letter of 1550 (Türkei I 8-3-8), the decoding was frozen before its contemporary decipherment was opened: 92.5% of letters agree with it (84.3% mechanically from the key alone), against 20% on average and 31% at most for permuted keys.
  4. Hand. An atlas of the copyist’s letter forms was drawn up; the show-through on 145v was subtracted digitally for reading.
  5. Transcription and reading. All 207 lines were transcribed sign by sign on the archive’s native images in four passes, with no known text, and every group was counted as sure, probable or unresolved.
  6. Review. Brice Dudout looked again, without the decryption, at 20 doubtful signs shown as shapes: 19 confirmed, one re-examined. Ribier and Charrière were used to identify the people and check the sense, never to fix a value.

By section, Brice Dudout’s counts: 143r ll. 1–17, 94%; 143r l. 18 to 143v l. 16, about 94%; 143v ll. 17–32, 97.5%; 144r, 98.8%; 144v, 98.6%; 145r, 99.6%; 145v ll. 1–27, 97.4%; 145v ll. 28–30 and 146r, about 95–97% (the last four estimated line by line, the others counted group by group). The 1,146 groups of the final two passes read at 98.1%; the whole copy, about 1,550 groups, at about 97.5%. Read as sentences against the translation on this site, the Latin and the Italian hold together throughout: the holes are scattered, the longest run of them three groups after ne deceptione (143v l. 30).

07 Earlier work and print

The archive’s catalogue records the item as not deciphered, and no earlier decipherment of this Latin text was found. The content of the letter of credence and of d’Huyson’s instructions has been known in French since 1666: Guillaume Ribier, Lettres et mémoires d’estat, prints the credence and the instruction, and Charrière’s Négociations de la France dans le Levant, vol. II, summarises them and prints the letters on d’Huyson’s journey. What the cipher adds is the Latin delivered at the Porte, Malvezzi’s notes and the third letter. Malvezzi’s correspondence is edited in Austro-Turcica 1541–1552 (Munich 1995), which was not consulted.

08 What remains uncertain

  • Unresolved groups, in the contributor’s list (18): Malvezzi’s Italian heading, 143r l. 2 (two and a half groups); 143r ll. 10 and 12 (one each); 143v l. 30 (three, after ne deceptione); 144r ll. 4 and 6 (three); 144v l. 1 (two) and ll. 25–26 (one); 145r l. 4 (one); 145v l. 3 (two), l. 15 (a final abbreviation) and l. 17 (one). The established text marks 27 places with ‹…›, because it also marks parts of words and the gaps in Malvezzi’s Italian note on 143r. No value was added to the key to force them.
  • Grades. Groups that come straight from the frozen key are H. The 86 readings in braces are M: probable, chosen by sense between close sign shapes or between the two values of a sign. The nine restorations in square brackets (words such as [curiam] and [erat], letters such as salute[m]) are I, supplied from the sense where the copyist left them out.
  • Null or word. Cum and etiam are sometimes null and sometimes meant; Itcirco on 146r opens a sentence and has been kept as a word.
  • Names. Captain {Bartolamio} rests on reading one sign as t. The sense of et {loco suo} before the signature (the place of the seal or monogram in the Turkish) is an interpretation.

## h3 (143r l. 18–30)

## h4 (143r l. 31 – 145v l. 15)

From this I saw the fraud: in these letters he did not swear, as is the custom of emperors and kings. Another point: had the King of Spain wished to remain amicably in this peace, he ought to have sent princes and noblemen to confirm it, as he did when he made peace with the King of England; let [Your Highness] know that, by custom, the King of Spain and Ferdinand his brother ought to have sent men great and capable enough to promise and ‹…› ‹…› until the term of the promise was fulfilled.

I also inform you that my ambassador at the court of the King of Spain then, with remarkable diligence and skill, obtained a copy of those letters from the very chanceries of the King of Spain, and sent them to me at once; from them I saw that the King of Spain, with his brother Ferdinand, means to deceive. As soon as these copies reached my hands I sent them in all haste to [your] court by my pantler Dusson, so that [Your Highness] may know their wickedness and how they wish to deceive [Her], together with Her friends and confederates; and I hope [Your Highness] will not let Herself be deceived once She has understood their schemes, laid for Her ruin.

And if [Your Highness] will accept these letters of the promise made, and if he will again give such letters of confirmation of the peace upon them, I ask that [Your Highness] should not accept the article promised by my father, since my father is dead; but an article of this kind must be written into the letters of confirmation: that King Henry, our faithful friend, with his friends and confederates, remain in this peace already concluded with Our Majesty, on the ‹…› condition that each remain firm and peacefully possess what is at present in his hands; and that the King of Spain and his brother must likewise be told that if they wish to remain at peace until the promised term, without fraud, he must accept me, King of France, as friend and confederate, with my friends and confederates.

I let you know that there are certain provinces in Germany, of the people ‹…› of the Swiss, who have been my confederates for many years; they have many men fit for war, and whenever I need it I quickly raise an army from them; they give me the greatest help. If he does not have the King of Spain confirm [the inclusion of] these with my confederates and friends, the King of Spain will make war on these Swiss, my confederates; and let [Your Highness] know that if that king should happen to defeat and subjugate those Swiss peoples, I should afterwards be greatly weakened by their oppression. I therefore ask Her to have this article confirmed, namely that of the confederates and friends of the King of France; and if the King of Spain will not confirm it, that She not consent to hold this peace as firm, ratified or agreeable. Likewise, that the King of Spain neither dare nor presume to ask me to restore what my father acquired in war, wherever it may be.

Again I inform you that when [Your Highness] had in hand the ambassadors of the King of Spain and of Ferdinand, they promised thirty thousand florins for some castles or places of the kingdom of Hungary, and asked Your Majesty’s court for letters of confirmation of the peace; had She then not accepted this deceitful bargain of thirty thousand and asked for a hundred thousand florins, not letting the ambassadors leave, they would then have promised and paid a hundred thousand, for with those places they can {recover} the whole kingdom of Hungary. Let Her know also that there is no need to give such enemies help or favour; on the contrary ‹…› with ‹…›; it must always be ensured that they remain weak on all sides; if She makes peace and gives them part of the kingdom of Hungary, they will grow stronger every day and their enemies {will become} weak.

I have informed you of everything, and I shall always let you know what news I have, because of the singular friendship long kept with my father, which I hope to keep perpetual. Again and again I ask you to see to it that the King of Spain observe the peace {according to} the articles written, for they do not keep their word; it must be well considered. ‹…›

## h6 (145v l. 18 – 146r l. 19)

Most serene Emperor of the Ottomans, lord Sultan Suleiman, my dearest brother, greeting, the feeling of affection, all good fortune and increase of felicity. I inform you, as I wrote before, that the King of Spain is a man who knows much, but a deceiver; I continually have my ambassador at his court, who lets me know by letter all the affairs that occur there; and this king, with his brother and the other princes {of Germany}, has held a very great council in the city of Augsburg, and all have united into one; the King of Spain has asked these princes for help, an army and money; day and night they are ready for any occasion of war.

Therefore, as I warned before, it seems he is plotting some deceit: let [Your Highness] open Her eyes. I ask Her to keep Her fleet ready at sea for every occasion; and if war must be made anywhere, to send commanders and captains to sea with the fleet, and to order them, whenever I should need help, to come to my aid; and if the Spaniards make any incursion in Barbary, I shall send my fleet together with the armada and with Her other confederates. I also let you know that the lord de Ramon, faithful and sincerely dear to us, was already ambassador at [your] court in my father’s name; I confirm him in the same office, and in all that he may happen to negotiate he will give faith in my name as if I were present in person. In witness whereof, and {in its place}, Henry, King of France.
