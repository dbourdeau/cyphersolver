Elgar’s Dorabella cipher

Edward Elgar’s 87-sign note to Dora Penny, tested here in fifty calibrated rounds; no language, key or plaintext form gives a reading, and what is left needs a second text or a better image.

Daniel Bourdeau · attempted 28 October 2026 · updated 29 October 2026

At a glanceNot solved · text not obtained

The document
A slip of 87 signs in three lines, dated 14 July 1897, sent by Edward Elgar to Dora Penny inside a letter from Alice Elgar to Dora’s stepmother after the Elgars’ visit to Wolverhampton.
Where
The original is lost. The source is the plate on p. 98 of Dora Powell’s Edward Elgar: Memories of a Variation (1937); the 1949 edition differs by a second dot.
The cipher
Each sign is one, two or three arcs facing one of eight directions: 24 possible signs, 20 of them used. Elgar’s notebook key of the 1920s is one ordered layout of these 24 signs.
How it was tried
Fifty rounds, every hypothesis scored on Dorabella, on shuffled copies of its signs, and on real English put through the same system; a reading has to reach the level the real text reaches.
What it shows
The signs flip to the opposite direction 27 times, against 12.6 by chance. Any key that changes as it goes would wash that out, so a message would use one fixed table; under one fixed table nothing reads.
Still open
Whether it is a message at all, or a sequence with strong writing habits; this cannot be decided from 87 signs. A second text in the same system or a cleaner plate would.
Credit
Transcription: Hauer, Choi, Hindle, Smallwood and Kondrak (2025). Independent tests: Cooper Jager (2026). Prior analysis: Wase (2023), Nick Pelling, Klaus Schmeh.

01 The note

Dora Penny printed the note as an appendix to her memoir of Elgar, saying it was “the third letter I had from him, if indeed it is one” and that it had come enclosed in a letter from Alice Elgar after the Elgars’ visit to Wolverhampton in July 1897. On the back was written “Miss Penny”. She never had an explanation of it. The mark under the third line is a date, 14 July ’97, not a signature.

The transcription used throughout is the five-reader consensus of Hauer et al. (2025): each sign is written as its orientation, A to H going clockwise from “opens to the right”, and its number of arcs, 1 to 3. The three lines have 29, 31 and 27 signs. Twenty of the 24 possible signs occur; the commonest, F2, eleven times. Readers disagree on 11 positions; a measurement of the 1937 scan here reclassifies 17 signs, and Cooper Jager’s 2026 reading differs at five. All of these readings were tested.

02 What the signs show

Three facts hold whatever key is assumed.

  • The order is not random. The note repeats 19 pairs of adjacent signs; shuffled copies of the same signs repeat 11.2 on average (P = 0.004).
  • It behaves like English letters on every statistic no key can change. Number of distinct signs, index of coincidence (0.059), repeated pairs, doubled signs and singletons together are typical of 87 letters of English (P 0.84) and of Elgar’s own letters (P 0.89), and atypical of speech sounds (0.002), music (0.03) and Latin (0.04).
  • Consecutive signs face opposite ways far too often. A 180° turn between neighbours occurs 27 times against 12.6 expected; none of 5,000 shuffles reached 27. English under a random key gives 11; under the best layout of letters on the dial, about 25.

One caution, first made by Jager: shuffles that keep every adjacent pair of the note match it on all of these, and on repeats two to four signs apart. A writer who chooses each sign from the one before, with strong habits, fits the statistics as well as English does. At 87 signs the two cannot be told apart.

03 How it was tested

Every hypothesis was scored three ways: on the note; on copies with its signs shuffled, or shuffled keeping every adjacent pair; and on real text, usually windows of Elgar’s own letters to the Pennys, put through exactly the system being tested. A result counts as a reading only if the note reaches the range of the real text and its decode splits into real words of the language; scoring above the shuffles alone only reflects the note’s known structure.

The solver’s power was measured, not assumed. With the fast settings used in the first rounds, 87 letters of Elgar’s letters were recovered only 42% of the time; the failures were search failures, and with sixty restarts eleven of twelve windows were recovered (93–100%). From round 31 on, the main branches were run again at that strength or more, up to 920 independent long runs on 23 processor cores.

05 The flips and the fixed table

The flips are a property of how letters sit on the dial, so they survive only if the dial stays the same. When English is enciphered with a fixed table that puts common neighbours on opposite sides, the flips come out at 24.5 on average and reach 27 a third of the time; when any changing key is added (a periodic dial turn, Vigenère, autokey, a running key, Nihilist), they drop to 8–12 and reach 27 in at most one sample in a thousand. If the note is a message, Elgar wrote it with one fixed table throughout. English under such a flip-heavy table reproduces all six measured statistics at once (P 0.71), which neither a simple pseudo-writer (0.008) nor English under a random key (0.013) does.

That is what makes the failure informative. With the transcription as it stands, English under one fixed table would be found about nine times in ten; the long search instead returns a flat field of different gibberish texts, none recurring, all below the worst real Elgar window.

06 Where it stands

Under the consensus transcription, the note is not English, Elgar’s own register, any of eleven other languages or any of the plaintext forms tried, written with one fixed table, with up to six misreads or with homophones. Two explanations remain, and 87 signs cannot separate them:

  • A message the tests cannot reach: more misreads than the plate allows to be modelled (ten or more), a key changing partway through, or a plaintext form not tried.
  • No message: a deliberate sequence written with strong habits (the flips, the repeated pairs), which fits every statistic measured.

Undecidable at this length: a writer’s pair habits against language, a key changing partway, ten or more misreads, sign pairs or word signs standing for letters.

07 What would finish it

  • A second text in the same system. Elgar’s Courage cards (the first carries about ten such signs) and the “Microcryptogram” sold at Sotheby’s (lot 92). Any surviving key could then be checked on two documents.
  • The rest of the Schooling cards. Elgar documented his 1896 solution on a series of index cards at the Elgar Birthplace Museum; cards 1–4 are printed in Bauer’s Unsolved! and the “Cryptogram” card is an alphabet exercise. Photographs of the whole series would show whether any other card carries text in the signs.
  • Better images. The 1947 printing is scanned in greyscale on the Internet Archive (lending only); the 1946 and 1949 printings of the memoir (the 1949 one has a second dot at line 2, sign 11 or 12) and the Elgar Birthplace Museum’s photograph, to settle the 23 doubtful signs.
  • How people fake ciphers. Recordings of people inventing cipher-like squiggles would test the no-message explanation directly.
