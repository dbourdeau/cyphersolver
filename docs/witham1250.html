Lincoln, River Witham · inscription of 18 letters, system unknown · c. 1250–1330

The River Witham sword inscription

A medieval sword blade found in the River Witham near Lincoln in 1825 carries eighteen letters in gold wire; nothing here, and nothing published, reads it with any way to check the reading.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 9 November 2026 · updated 11 November 2026

Method: not solved · Extent: text not obtained

At a glanceNot solved · text not obtained

The document
A line of capital letters inlaid in gold wire along one face of a sword blade, between two crosses, with no word division.
Where
British Museum, museum number 1858,1116.5; found in 1825 in the River Witham near Lincoln and dated 1250–1330.
The cipher
Unknown. The vowel count (3 of 18) fits initials or abbreviations, which is not the same as showing it.
How it was tried
Letter counts, a search of the published proposals, a re-reading of letters 10–11 on two British Museum photographs, and a measured comparison of the letter order with eighteen published sibling inscriptions (Brice Dudout, 11 November 2026).
Why it resists
With eighteen letters and free abbreviation, many Latin, Germanic or Welsh phrases fit equally well, and the sibling blades are themselves unread.
Credit
Harald Winter’s initials reading is his own; it is recorded here, not tested or confirmed.
Still open
Every expansion. The blade is shown to belong to the SDX invocation family; what the family’s letters stand for is untested.

01 The inscription

The letters, as published by the British Library (Julian Harrison, 2015) and by Winter (2016), are +NDXOXCHWDRGHDXORVI+: eighteen letters between two crosses, without spaces.

Corrected reading (Brice Dudout, 11 November 2026). On two British Museum photographs (the detail on the British Library blog and BM collection image 00296785; examined, not reproduced) the 10th letter is an uncial N (no bowl; the R at #16 has bowl and leg) and the 11th is a C with a curled upper terminal, the mirror of the C at #6: +NDXOXCHWDNCHDXORVI+. The Stamford Mercury of 18 August 1826 (quoted by Rob Wheeler, British Library blog comment, 7 August 2015) has N and C at the same places, and Marc van Hasselt read +ND XOX CH WDN CH DXO RVI+ in a blog comment of 6 August 2015. Letters 17 (V or Y) and 18 (I or N) stay uncertain. No image is reproduced here because none with a free licence was found.

Three of the eighteen letters are vowels (O, O, I), about 17%; Latin running text has more, roughly 40% (a figure from general knowledge, not measured here). That fits an inscription made of initials or abbreviations. It does not say what the initials stand for, and W is not a Latin letter.

02 What has been proposed

  • British Library, 2015. “As yet indecipherable”; perhaps a religious invocation; a Welsh sense was floated.
  • Language Log and Cipher Mysteries threads, 2016. About twenty readings by commenters, among them Latin initials (Nomine Domini, Dominum Iesum Christum), a name for W, gematria, Hebrew and “Croats”. None can be checked.
  • Harald Winter, Waffen- und Kostümkunde 58/2 (2016), revised 13 March 2026. Initials, with X read as CH(risti): N(omine) D(omini) X=CH(risti) / O(pe) X=CH(risti) CH(evalier) W / D(efendit) R(e)g(em) H(onorem) D(omini) X=CH(risti) OR(bos) VI(duas): “In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. With Christ’s help Sir W defends the king, the honour of God, orphans and widows.”

Winter’s reading is coherent and fits the period. Each expansion is still a choice made to fit: X is taken as CH where the same letter is also read as a cross or a spacer, W is a name, and “chevalier” sits inside Latin. Its “D(efendit) R(e)g(em) H(onorem)” rests on letters 10–11 as R and G, which the corrected reading gives as N and C; “CH(evalier)” sits where the sibling blades have EH.

03 The sibling blades

Other blades carry similar inlaid lettering: Alphen aan den Rijn, Berlin, Marburg and the Swedish Fyris blades, whose readings Wagner, Worley, Holst Blennow and Beckholmen published (Waffen- und Kostümkunde 51, 2009). Marc van Hasselt placed the Witham blade in this family in 2015.

Measured match (Brice Dudout, 11 November 2026). The longest common subsequence of the Witham letters with each of 18 sibling inscriptions (16 as published by Wagner et al. 2009, plus two unverified blog transcriptions, Stevensweert and Berg) was compared with 5,000 shuffles of the Witham letters (order destroyed, letters kept). Two stand out: Fyris UMF/B 74, 13 of 18 letters (DXOXCDNCDXORI), p = 0.0002, and Marburg, 11 of 18 (NDXOHNDXORI), p = 0.0012; 0.004 and 0.02 after a Bonferroni correction for 18 comparisons. The other sixteen give nothing significant. Controls (Ulfberht, IN NOMINE DOMINI, the St Benedict medal initials, Witham reversed and others) give p 0.3–1.0, and the old reading passes too (Fyris 74 12/18, p = 0.0008; Marburg p = 0.0006). Against 44 Latin prayers and formulas the longest run of initials is 3 letters (p = 0.73): not the acrostic of a known prayer. The inscription reads as a shortened copy of the SDX invocation of the Fyris 74 / Marburg / W 879 group.

What this shows is membership of the family, not a reading: the meaning of its elements (DX as Dominus Christus, O as an invocation, EH(R) as Middle High German “ehre”) is Wagner et al.’s conjecture and is not tested.
