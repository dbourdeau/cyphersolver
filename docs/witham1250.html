Lincoln, River Witham · inscription of 18 letters, system unknown · c. 1250–1330

The River Witham sword inscription

A medieval sword blade found in the River Witham near Lincoln in 1825 carries eighteen letters in gold wire; nothing here, and nothing published, reads it with any way to check the reading.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 9 November 2026

Method: not solved · Extent: text not obtained

not solved
On this pageThe inscriptionWhat has been proposedThe sibling bladesWhat would finish itSources

At a glanceNot solved · text not obtained

The document
A line of capital letters inlaid in gold wire along one face of a sword blade, between two crosses, with no word division.
Where
British Museum, museum number 1858,1116.5; found in 1825 in the River Witham near Lincoln and dated 1250–1330.
The cipher
Unknown. The vowel count (3 of 18) fits initials or abbreviations, which is not the same as showing it.
How it was tried
Letter counts, a search of the published proposals, and a check of the sibling inscribed blades for a key.
Why it resists
With eighteen letters and free abbreviation, many Latin, Germanic or Welsh phrases fit equally well, and the sibling blades are themselves unread.
Credit
Harald Winter’s initials reading is his own; it is recorded here, not tested or confirmed.
Still open
Everything. Photographs and the catalogue of inscribed swords would allow a comparison of the family.

01 The inscription

The letters, as published by the British Library (Julian Harrison, 11 August 2015) and by Winter (2016), are +NDXOXCHWDRGHDXORVI+: eighteen letters between two crosses, without spaces. No photograph of the blade was examined for this page; no image is reproduced here because none with a free licence was found.

Three of the eighteen letters are vowels (O, O, I), about 17%; Latin running text has more, roughly 40% (a figure from general knowledge, not measured here). That fits an inscription made of initials or abbreviations. It does not say what the initials stand for, and W is not a Latin letter.

02 What has been proposed

  • British Library, 2015. “As yet indecipherable”; perhaps a religious invocation; a Welsh sense was floated.
  • Language Log and Cipher Mysteries threads, 2016. About twenty readings by commenters, among them Latin initials (Nomine Domini, Dominum Iesum Christum), a name for W, gematria, Hebrew and “Croats”. None can be checked.
  • Harald Winter, Waffen- und Kostümkunde 58/2 (2016), revised 13 March 2026. Initials, with X read as CH(risti): N(omine) D(omini) X=CH(risti) / O(pe) X=CH(risti) CH(evalier) W / D(efendit) R(e)g(em) H(onorem) D(omini) X=CH(risti) OR(bos) VI(duas): “In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ. With Christ’s help Sir W defends the king, the honour of God, orphans and widows.”

Winter’s reading is coherent and fits the period. Each expansion is still a choice made to fit: X is taken as CH where the same letter is also read as a cross or a spacer, W is a name, and “chevalier” sits inside Latin. Nothing in eighteen letters can confirm or reject it.

03 The sibling blades

Commenters name other blades with similar inlaid lettering: Alphen aan den Rijn in the Netherlands, one in Berlin and one in Sweden, sharing letter forms such as a curved X and an I-S ligature. The Alphen blade (BENEDOXO … MTINIUSCS) is itself only speculatively read (“Benedictus, Sanctus Martinus”), so it gives no key for the Witham letters. Wagner and Worley’s work on “talking swords” (2009, 2013) was not seen.

04 What would finish it

not solved  The attempt is closed for lack of material.

whatwhy it would work
Photographs of the Witham, Alphen, Berlin and Swedish bladesThe letters could be transcribed and compared as one family, which tests whether a common formula repeats.
The Wagner and Worley catalogue of inscribed swordsA corpus of inscriptions with agreed readings would give the abbreviations a base to be tested against.
