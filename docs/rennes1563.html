Catherine de Médicis to the bishop of Rennes, 31 July 1563

BnF français 3181 f. 55 · thirteen lines of cipher set between two passages of clear French · catalogue item 11, class B

Printed in 1885 with the cipher left blank, and listed as undeciphered ever since · read here with a reconstructed key calibrated on the margin decipherment of a sibling letter · the enciphered subject is the Council of Trent and the marriage negotiation, not the war news in the same letter

Daniel Bourdeau · solved in part 20 September 2026

Summary. On 31 July 1563, from the camp before Le Havre, Catherine de Médicis answered Bernardin Bochetel, bishop of Rennes, her ambassador at the imperial court. The letter survives as a minute in BnF français 3181, folio 55. It opens in clear, breaks into thirteen lines of cipher, and returns to clear for the long account of the recovery of Le Havre from the English. Hector de La Ferrière printed the letter in 1885 in the Lettres de Catherine de Médicis — and printed the ciphered block as an empty bracket, with the footnote « Partie chiffrée ». S. Tomokiyo, who reconstructed the Bishop of Rennes’ cipher from the letters that carry decipherments, likewise lists f. 55 as undeciphered. So the passage had never been read. It reads. Tomokiyo’s key is correct but is a table of shapes, and this scribe’s hand does not match the table glyph for glyph; the way in was the sibling letter f. 57 of 13 August 1563, which carries a line-by-line decipherment in its margin, and whose first ciphered line proves to read que les princes. That fixed the scribe’s forms for p, r, i, n, c, e and s, and the rest followed. About three fifths of the passage is now securely read, including sceu ce que vous avez descouvert, du mariage, vostre advis, d’iceulx en mon intention, l’avancement du concile, à ce que and avoir l’utilité qui en sortira. Status: read in part. What limits the reading now is the transcription of near-identical homophones in this hand, not the key. The three sibling items in the Cinq cents Colbert volumes are in the same cipher and are still unread.

01The letter, and the hole in the middle of it

Bochetel had written on 18 June. Catherine, at the camp before Le Havre while the English garrison embarked, replied on the last day of July. La Ferrière prints her text from this very folio (« Orig. Bibl. nat. fonds français, n° 3181, f° 55 r° et v° »), and it runs:

Monsieur de Rennes, je n’ay receu vostre lettre du xviiie de juing que depuis sept ou huit jours, c’est pour [   ]
— footnote: Partie chiffrée.

Au demeurant, je veulx bien vous advertir que, après avoir essayé tous moyens de faire départir la royne d’Angleterre de la place du Havre-de-Grâce qu’elle avoit occupée durant ces troubles…

That bracket is the thirteen lines. Everything after it — the siege, the plague among the English, the artillery and ships left behind, the hope that the French and the English « n’auront plus que faire ensemble » — Catherine was content to send in clear. What she put in cipher was something else.

02The cipher, and the trick of the clear-word nulls

This is the Bishop of Rennes’ cipher, in use between 1561 and 1564 for the embassy to the Emperor. It is homophonic: two to eight invented signs for each letter, an overbar to tell close shapes apart, about thirty word-signs (que, vous, nous, vostre, qui, mais, par, la, le, lettre, bien, faire, faict, and signs of their own for l’Empereur and Roy de Boheme), and a set of nulls.

The nulls are the interesting part. Besides the meaningless squiggles, the key licenses ordinary French words written out in clear as nulls — que, est, pour, plus, xix, vre, do, fo. They are scattered through the cipher so that a reader glancing at the page sees what looks like ordinary writing interrupted by symbols, and a codebreaker trying to anchor on them wastes his time. On f. 55 the last line ends with a run of five of them before the clear text resumes.

03The way in was the next letter but one

A reconstructed key drawn as a table of shapes is not the same thing as being able to read a page. Several of this scribe’s forms are near-identical — the s-shapes that serve r and s, the c-shapes that serve o, h and t, the loops that serve e and l — and the table does not say which is which in his hand. What settled it was f. 57, the letter of 13 August 1563, thirty-eight views on in the same volume. Its ciphered passage carries a decipherment written down the left margin, phrase by phrase, line for line.

Reading the margin against the cipher gives an exact crib. The first ciphered line of f. 57 glosses as que les princes, and the signs are q̄q̄ · l · e · s · p · r · i · n · c · e · s — seven signs for « princes », which pins p, r, i, n, c, e and s at one stroke. The margin runs on: moien de se passer par Ferrare les enfans dudict Roy des Romains allans en Espaigne, pour eviter l’incomodité de la mer et le dangier que les temps aigres peult porter a ceste occasion… Every phrase of it is another handful of confirmed shapes.

04What f. 55 says

The thirteen lines are 401 signs, 71 of them distinct. The key gives a value to 397 of the 401; what is not yet certain is which of two near-identical shapes the pen actually made in a given place, and that is what keeps parts of lines 2, 4, 8, 12 and 13 dark. Plain type below is secure; … is a stretch I cannot resolve; square brackets are restoration.

  1. … par … a vostre — sceu ce que vous avez descouvert
  2. … par … du mariage … vostre advis … [con]duict
  3. … d’iceulx en mon intention, et b[ien] que tou[s] …
  4. … fort … [qu’il] ayt tenu ce chemin d[e] …
  5. ce qui pourra … ayant aussi entendu ce que le …
  6. vous mist en avant, qui l’avoit … dél[ibéré] … faire … la lettre bien
  7. de la … bien tost … l’avancement du concile, à ce que ju[sques] …
  8. … chacun faict … de … sa part … monstre …
  9. … fructueuse … de ce concile, mais nous …
  10. … avoir l’utilité qui en sortira … mes …
  11. mes … ce qui … mect des se[ss]ions Descembre des …
  12. que … vous avez oy … par lettres … vous …
  13. … [tou]chante … [nulls to the end of the line]

05Why it was in cipher

The subject is the Council of Trent and the marriage negotiation with the house of Austria — the two things Catherine had reason not to send in clear through the empire in the summer of 1563, and exactly the two things she left out of the open part of the letter. She acknowledges what Rennes has descouvert, she names le mariage and asks after his advis, she states her own intention, she reports what somebody « mist en avant » and what he « avoit délibéré », and she returns twice to the council: l’avancement du concile, the hope that it prove fructueuse, and the plain calculation of l’utilité qui en sortira. Line 11 has sessions and, written out in clear among the signs, the month Descembre. The council in fact closed on 4 December 1563.

The contrast with the rest of the sheet is the point. Charles IX’s majority had been declared at Rouen a fortnight later, on 17 August; the Havre had just fallen; those were things to be published. What went under the cipher was the ecclesiastical and dynastic bargaining that the French crown was conducting at Trent and Vienna at the same moment.

06Method

Images by IIIF region request from Gallica at the manuscript’s native 8720 × 5935, not the 2500-pixel page derivative: at page resolution the overbars that separate homophones are not reliably visible, and a first attempt at the transcription had failed on exactly that. One correction worth recording for anyone following: in français 3181 the folio number is the Gallica view plus nineteen, so f. 55 is view 36 and f. 57 is view 38.

The transcription is mine, glyph by glyph, from thirds of each line at native resolution; the token boundaries are mine too, since the cipher has no word division. The decoder is a flat table lookup (rennes1563/decode.py) applied to that transcription — there is no search and no solver here, because the key was already known; the whole problem was palaeographic. The calibration against f. 57 is described in section 03.

07What remains uncertain

Grades, as in the repository’s conventions:

  • H — the phrases set in bold in section 04. They are continuous French, they agree with the clear text that frames them, and the word-signs in them are the ones the key gives.
  • C — se[ss]ions on line 11 and dél[ibéré] on line 6: the letters either side are secure and the restoration is the obvious one, but the middle signs are ambiguous between two homophones.
  • M — the sense of lines 4, 8 and 13 as wholes. The individual words given are read; the connective tissue between them is not, so the syntax of those lines is inferred.
  • I — the opening of line 2 and the tail of line 12 are not read at all. Roughly a hundred and sixty of the 401 signs have a letter assigned but do not yet fall into words I will defend.

None of this is a key problem. The limit is that in this hand the r and s shapes, the e and l loops, and the o, h and t c-shapes differ by a stroke that the parchment does not always preserve. A second transcription pass, and the same treatment applied to the three sibling items — Cinq cents Colbert 390 pp. 139 and 357 (p. 139 is the one with the endorsement « Deschiffrez vous mesmes ») and Cinq cents Colbert 392 p. 231, a Bourdin letter of some thirty lines entirely in cipher — would settle most of it. All three are in the same cipher and all three are still unread.
