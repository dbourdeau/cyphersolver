Armstrong's coded postscript to Madison, 1808

→

Bourbon-l'Archambault, 30 August 1808 · Library of Congress, James Madison Papers, Series 1, reel 10 · Founders Online, Madison/99-01-02-3466

The “THE = 972” code was recovered from the State Department's own pencil decodes of Armstrong's 1806 despatches and from Tomokiyo's partial table, then applied to the postscript: 48 of the 49 groups give sense, and the last does so with one digit emended. Tomokiyo's table covered about a third of the postscript's groups; no full decipherment was found in archive, print or online.

Summary. John Armstrong, United States Minister to France, ended a private letter to Secretary of State James Madison (Bourbon-l'Archambault, 30 August 1808) with a postscript of 49 numerical code groups. The letter is printed in Founders Online with the postscript undeciphered, and S. Tomokiyo's Unsolved Historical Ciphers lists it, with a partial reading of about a third of the groups obtained from one letter with known plaintext. The code, a word-and-syllable code of about 1,600 groups that Armstrong used from 1804 to 1810, conventionally labelled by its group for "the", 972, has not been published. It was recovered here from a different source: the microfilmed State Department despatch books (NARA M34, roll 13), where a Department clerk pencilled the decode above every group of a long despatch of October 1806. Some 580 code groups are now known, enough to decode all of the postscript and most of a second coded letter of 22 February 1808. The decoded postscript:
Russel ought to be the consul: he is an American by birth, and is much better qualified than any other candidate. In a word, he is above men in general. Next to him in fitness is O'Mealy, but he is, like Warden, an Irishman.

Companion: John Quincy Adams’s St Petersburg despatches, said to be in this code, are in another, and Erving’s 1807 letter is in Pinckney’s: two legation codes.

01 The document

The letter is a "private" one, written from the spa town of Bourbon-l'Archambault where Armstrong was taking the waters. Its clear text answers Madison's private letter of 20 July: it dismisses the complaints of Fulwar Skipwith (the departing commercial agent at Paris), warns that the embargo is "not felt" in France and "forgotten" in England, and says of David Bailie Warden, secretary of the legation and candidate for the Paris consulate, only that "a letter of the same character from me to the President … will give you my opinion of the fitness of M. Warden for the consular office at Paris: to this therefore I refer." The opinion Armstrong would not put in clear follows in code, as a postscript.

Founders Online (Early Access document 99-01-02-3466, from the forthcoming Papers of James Madison, Secretary of State Series) prints the groups as follows; the reading of the manuscript differs in one group (§6):

02 The code

Armstrong's despatches from Paris between 1804 and 1810 use a single large code in which 972 = the. Ralph Weber's survey of American diplomatic codes notes some forty letters in it among the Despatches from United States Ministers to France (DUSMF, NARA Record Group 59, microcopy M34, rolls 13–14). It is not the code of Armstrong's letter of 20 February 1808, a distinct system whose key has not been recovered. Its structure, as recovered:

  • Groups run from 1 to about 1600. Each stands for a whole word (Emperor 32, Spain 326, Minister 61), a syllable (con 470, tion 352, ness 832), or a single letter (s 1116, n 821, d 1001). Names are spelt out in syllables: Gu-sta-va-us, Et-ru-ria, Y-ru-jo, Dan-ish, O-mea-ly.
  • The vocabulary is laid out in many short alphabetical runs, not one alphabetical sequence. For instance 1–130 is one run of words (circumstance, commerce, communicate, character … Minister … these, thousand, toward … week, without, yesterday), 130–417 a second (America, between, business, confide, contrary, country … Prince, Queen, reason, report, Russia, second, secret … which, whom, word, would, your), and above 417 the syllables come in blocks that restart the alphabet every few dozen numbers (1201 a … 1230 c; 1268 ed … 1298 ex; 1302 ide … 1348 broa; 1405 be … 1443 good; 1500s fa … man … now). Within a run the order is alphabetical, so the value of an unknown group is bracketed by its known neighbours.
  • Common elements have homophones: re is 555, 561, 562, 869, 871 and 1379; on is 945 and 946; an 1210 and 1216; ing 1478 and 1594.
  • Armstrong writes a point after each group; the clerk who decoded despatches at the Department wrote the plaintext in pencil above each group and a short dash above groups that continue a word already written out (Et|–|–, for Etruria).

03 Sources for the key

3.1 The known-plaintext letter of 4 May 1806

Tomokiyo had already used the one letter for which both the coded original and a clear copy survive, Armstrong to Madison of 4 May 1806 (Founders 99-01-02-0393) on the Spanish minister's discovery of the confidential Florida proposals, to assign about 230 groups, with a few marked doubtful. This gave a base of 68 firm values used here, among them the, of, to, is, he, a, and, by, in, s, n, and showed the alphabetical layout.

3.2 The Department's pencil decodes (M34 roll 13)

The main source is the microfilm itself. NARA has digitised M34 roll 13 ("Nov 12 1804 – Dec 27 1807", catalogue ID 188671172; 393 frames). Frames 0192–0201 carry a long political despatch of October 1806 (written just after Lord Lauderdale's departure from Paris, with news of "two Russian armies now entering Silesia") whose every code group has the clerk's decode written above it. A scoring pass over the roll for faint mid-grey pixels away from ink located the annotated frames; the pairs were then transcribed by eye, frame by frame, and recorded with a confidence grade and their frame/line reference.

About 500 pairs were collected this way, 352 of them legible with confidence. Merged with the 4 May 1806 values the table stands at 580 distinct groups (§7). Ambiguities in the pencil (the roll is a 1940s microfilm of faint graphite) were resolved by requiring each reading to sit in its alphabetical slot and to recur consistently across frames.

3.3 Interpolation

Twenty groups needed for the postscript and the February letter were not in either source. Each was inferred from context and then checked against its alphabetical slot; all twenty fit. Examples: 1394 between 1393 purp and 1395 Italy must open a new run, and it gives ru in three independent places (ru-in, Et-ru-ria, Y-ru-jo); 250 lies between 249 other and 252 own and reads ought; 1273 between 1272 eight and 1279 Emp reads el; 384 between 383 w and 385 was reads ward; 1320 between 1319 li and 1325 long reads like; 1483 between 1482 is and 1484 it reads ish.

3.4 A control text: the letter of 22 February 1808

Armstrong's private letter of 22 February 1808 (Founders 99-01-02-2733) contains about 150 code groups embedded in a clear political survey of Europe. It shares no groups of interest with the known-plaintext letter, so it serves as an independent check. With the merged table it decodes, with gaps, as follows:

The ruin of Gustavus is at last resolved. Russia is to seize Finland, while France & Denmark take possession of Sweden. Denmark cannot ha[ve] very willingly … independence. And it is certainly amongst the most cruel circumstances of the British attack on her Capital that it … her … of her … and [like]wise … of conduct, and thrown her completely into the arms of France, while the Danish Minister was this morning pouring out his complaints to me upon this subject, and stating the … of his government and the means taken by it to avert the introduction into Denmark of a general army … of Hamburg waited in the antichamber, with the intelligence that the Prince of Ponte Corvo had already entered Holstein … There is no one of even tolerable knowledge and sagacity who does not see this. Yet wonderful to tell Russia not only does not … but actually assists him in accomplishing one half of the object. Can Finland compensate for a state of things which shall put it in the power of France to lock up the Baltic …?

The sentences that come out whole (the ru-in of Gu-sta-va-us is at last resolve-d, the Dan-ish Minister was this morning po-ur-ing out his com-plain-t-s to me upon this sub-ject, a-ve-rt the in-t-ro-duct-ion into Denmark, as-sist-s him in ac-com-pli-sh-ing one half of the object) confirm the table and fix the values ru, ish, Russia (305, which the pencil on frame 0200 also gives) used in the postscript. One group there is anomalous: 946 is on in the 4 May 1806 letter but must be Gu in Gustavus (914 = gu elsewhere): a homophone, or a slip in the manuscript or the transcription.

4.1 The people

  • Warden. David Bailie Warden (1772–1845), born in County Down, a United Irishman exiled in 1799, naturalised American, Armstrong's private secretary and then secretary of legation. He was the front-runner for the vacant Paris consulate and got it; Armstrong removed him in 1810, and when Madison reinstated him in 1811 Armstrong protested in a letter of 3 March 1811 that Warden was "without a single grain of attachment to the U.S." and asked whether "our own country furnish[es] no native citizen willing & able to fill the consulate at Paris". The postscript shows the same animus three years earlier, and its cause: Warden was not American-born.
  • O'Mealy. Michael O'Mealy, a Baltimore merchant resident in France since 1793 and still there in 1810 (identified in the Papers of James Madison, Secretary of State Series 10:497 n. 2). The name, of obvious Irish origin, is spelt O-mea-ly.
  • Russel. Spelt Ru-s-el. The most probable candidate is Jonathan Russell of Providence, Rhode Island (1771–1832), a merchant in the European trade whom Madison did in fact appoint chargé d'affaires at Paris when Armstrong left in 1810; the identification is inferential, since no other document places him in France in 1808.

05 Method

  1. Collect the ciphertext. Founders Online transcriptions of Armstrong's coded letters (4 May 1806, 22 February 1808, September 1807, 30 August 1808) were gathered, and the LOC manuscript images of the 30 August letter retrieved for collation.
  2. Locate the decoded despatches. The NARA catalogue entry for M34 roll 13 was downloaded (393 frames). A simple image score (the proportion of mid-grey pixels not adjacent to dark ink) was used as a triage aid while browsing the roll; the annotated frames 0190 and 0192–0201 were confirmed by eye. Rerun over all 393 frames on 16 September 2026 the score does not locate them on its own: grey blank leaves outscore the annotated pages, and only the first, densest page of the October despatch ranks high (third of 392) once the score is restricted to the interlinear band near ink. See armstrong/NOTES.md.
  3. Harvest. Each annotated frame was cropped into thirds, contrast-enhanced, and transcribed group by group; every pair was recorded with frame/line and a confidence grade (H/M). Groups seen on several frames were cross-checked.
  4. Merge and rank. A script merges the 4 May 1806 values, the harvested pairs and the inferences, ranking readings H > C > M > inferred, and renders any coded passage with unknowns and doubtful values flagged.
  5. Infer and verify. Remaining gaps were filled by context and checked against the alphabetical slot; the 22 February 1808 letter served as the independent control.
  6. Collate the manuscript. The LOC image of the postscript was enlarged to check every group against the Founders text.

06 Corrections to the printed transcription, and one residual doubt

  • Founders prints 1218 in the last line; the manuscript reads 1216 (an), which is also what the sentence requires.
  • Founders prints 970. at the start of the second line where the manuscript has 972. (the). The fourth-line group is 1319 (li), as printed. A pencil s below 1116 on the Library of Congress copy shows that a reader in Washington began, and abandoned, a decode.
  • The final group is 555 both in Founders and in the manuscript. 555 is re in the code (frame 0195: sco-re), which makes no sense after Irish, whereas 1555 is man (frames 0195, 0200: the command of Prince Charles, man). The reading Irishman assumes Armstrong dropped a digit. The alternative, that the postscript breaks off with "an Irish re–", is noted for completeness.
  • In the 22 February letter Founders' 631 (wise) in "his com-631-t-s" is almost certainly 651 (plain), and 916 (had) in "Denmark cannot 916 very willingly" is probably 921 (have); both are single-digit slips of the kind the microfilm hand invites.
