Eger → Ferrara · two-tier cipher of graphic signs with code words · 9 March 1519

Giuliano Caprile, Eger, 9 March 1519

Seven lines in Caprile’s 1519 cipher about his own lawsuit at Rome; values rebuilt from his letters deciphered in 1882 give no Italian on it, so it stays unread.

At a glanceNot solved · 183 cipher columns, none deciphered

The letter
Giuliano Caprile, canon of Eger and secretary of Cardinal Ippolito d’Este, to the ducal secretary Bonaventura Pistofilo, Eger, 9 March 1519: seven lines of cipher and a clear postscript.
Where
Archivio di Stato di Modena, Ambasciatori, Ungheria b. 4, Caprili 1519–20 no. 1; DECODE R1128 (catalogue item 157), Vestigia 1853.
The cipher
Two tiers: a small sign written over a base stroke (7, m, n, a, t, 4), and bracket-shaped signs that stand for whole words. 183 columns of 78 kinds, 39 of them code words.
How it was tried
Caprile’s other 1519 letters in the same cipher were deciphered in 1882. Two of them were paired with those decipherments to rebuild tables of values, and the tables were run on R1128 against random-key controls.
Why it resists
The two pairings disagree on how the cipher works (one letter per column, or a syllable per column), and only about nine letter values are secure. None of the tables gives Italian on R1128.
What would finish it
A careful transcription of another 1882-paired letter (R1132 against its copy 6b, R1131 against 5b) to fill the table and the code list. The key material exists; the work is not done.
Other ciphers
Caprile’s 1520–21 letters use a different cipher, with its own page, where most of them are deciphered; Alfonso Cistarelli’s numerical cipher of 1520 has its own page.

The ASMo inventory notes that the decipherments of almost all the 1519 despatches are missing and were made only in 1882; those are the “b” and “c” items filed after the originals. No. 1 (R1128) has none. Its clear part and Caprile’s next letter (“In un’altra mia ve mandai in zifra…”) say it concerned his own lawsuit at Rome (“le ragioni mie … l’adversario”). The other 1519 letters are copies of reports to the King of France on the imperial election.

02 The cipher

Each column is a small sign over a base stroke. Paired with its 1882 decipherment 7c, R1133 (1,327 columns, 16% illegible in a faded band) reads as one plaintext letter per column, with homophones: “oratore” = L/7 -/n u/7 x/7 c/7 m/n -/a, and c/7 = o, m/n = r, x/7 = t hold across the page. Bracket-L signs with an inner sign are code words: the run [d][s][&] recurs where the decipherment has “de vostra maesta”. A fifth of R1133’s columns are such codes.

R1130 paired with its decipherment 4c points the other way: the upper sign gives a consonant and the base a vowel (7 none, m e, n o), and the brackets are syllables; about twenty values predict 16 of 18 held-out columns, against 8 for a shuffled table. The two readings conflict, and neither is settled.

03 What was tried on R1128

  • A language-model search (simulated annealing) over R1133, R1134 and R1128 with the nine secure values fixed gives only scattered words.
  • The known tables (2 October 2026): the R1130/4c syllabary covers 32% of R1128’s columns and scores like a random key with the same values (−3.22 against −3.24 per character); the R1133/7c values cover 13%.
  • A factorised syllabary (consonant from the upper sign, vowel from the base) trained on both pairings learns the base tier cleanly but not the upper; decoded with it, R1128 scores worse than the same tables permuted (−2.92 against −2.41).

Benedetto Fantini’s letters of 1517–18 from the same household use a cipher of the same family, and the 1519 values do not fit them either (Fantini 1517–18).

04 What would finish it

The key material is in the file: R1131 and R1132 (eight pages) have their 1882 decipherments 5b and 6b. A line-by-line transcription of one of them against its decipherment would fill the letter table and the code list, and then R1128 can be tried again. Until then R1128 is attempted, not solved.
