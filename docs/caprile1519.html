Caprile and Cistarelli at Eger, 1519–1521

Archivio di Stato di Modena, Ambasciatori, Ungheria b. 4 (DECODE R1128–R1139): the ciphered letters of two agents of Cardinal Ippolito d’Este, bishop of Eger, to the ducal secretary Bonaventura Pistofilo and to Duke Alfonso I.

Catalogue items 157 and 158. Most of the letters turned out to be read already. Two that were not are read in part here, with a key rebuilt from a decipherment printed in 2025.

Daniel Bourdeau · solved in part 21 September 2026 · updated 22 September 2026

solved in part

Summary. The catalogue listed seven DECODE records for Giuliano Caprile, canon of Eger and secretary of Ippolito d’Este, and one for Alfonso Cistarelli, the cardinal’s agent at Eger in 1520, all marked partly decrypted. The Hungarian research database Vestigia holds the whole Caprile file. It shows that the 1519 letters were deciphered in 1882: the Modena inventory says the decipherments “were drawn out only in 1882”, and they are filed after each cipher original as items “b” and “c”. One letter carries the decipherment between its own lines. The postscript of 16 February 1521 (R1138) was deciphered by Judit W. Somogyi in 2025. Her two printed texts made a crib of about 700 letters for the 1520–21 sign cipher. Aligned against a transcription of the signs, they give a homophonic key, and that key reads two letters with no decipherment anywhere, R1139 (6 April 1521) and R1136 (8 March 1520), in part. Both concern the see of Eger and Ippolito’s estate: the custode, the governor, money and debts. Two items stay unread. R1128 (March 1519) is in a different cipher, about Caprile’s own lawsuit in Rome. Cistarelli’s figure cipher (R1137) gives only phrases to a ciphertext-only search.

02 The key

The 1520–21 cipher is a run of invented signs with no word division: φ, ψ, λ, ÷, ss, ff, a crossed b, m and n with a cross, an inverted T, and some forty more. There are 49 labels in all, over 2,278 signs in five texts, transcribed from the DECODE and Vestigia images. Somogyi calls it homophonic, and it is. The key was built in two steps:

  1. A ciphertext-only anneal over all five texts, scored with a sixteenth-century Italian n-gram model. It gave a rough key in which scritti, regno, signore and Ungaria could already be read.
  2. That key seeded a monotone alignment (Viterbi with gaps, re-estimated until stable) of the transcription against Somogyi’s two texts. 714 letters align, and 90% of alignments agree with the majority value of their sign.

The values:

a = q, T, ⊥ · c = φ, θ · d = ψ, r · e = y, ss · g = w, u · i = ƀ, ɔ, L · l = ff, n · m = z, Ħ · n = ÷, p · o = ɛ, the stacked q/b, oσo · p = & and its looped variant · r = *, x · s = m+, n+ · t = h, f · u = λ, a

A lone o between letters is usually a null. So, in one place, is the figure 8.

03 R1139, 6 April 1521 (read in part)

A slip pasted into the letter of 6 April. Nine lines of cipher, then the clear text “Expettamo sino a S. Georgio…”. The phrases the key gives with confidence (grade C):

Post: il custode … io scrissi … è tornato governator de[l] … ogni cosa … è tenuto Agria … in castello … la venuta … in Italia … li scritti … vedendo io … deli danari del suo … come mi disse … lui m’ha resposto dice de voler andar in Italia quando al dare … Milan … parlaremo a longo sopra ciò … per andar … el mi dirà …

It is the estate business of Somogyi’s article: the keeper (custode) and the governor of Eger, “the money of his” (the late bishop’s), and someone who says he wants to go to Italy.

04 R1136, 8 March 1520 (read in part)

Twelve lines after a clear opening, written the day after Ippolito crossed out of Hungary for the last time (7 March 1520). Grades C and M:

e la susseguente matina … il custode … questo episcopato … il custode … governator … Lactantio … tesoro … in gran … conclusi … congrega[tione] … ogni episcopa[to] … in questo modo … poco spensi … Excellentia … al custode … de Ungaria sopra il suo … secondo … tornato … intendere … [s]pensier suo … tal debito

05 What stays open

  • The rest of R1139 and R1136. The transcription is a single reading of small, crowded signs, and misread signs leave most letters between the quoted phrases wrong. A second reading of the images against the key would go most of the way to a full text. The mechanical decrypt is in decrypt21_raw.txt.
  • R1128 (9 March 1519). Attempted, not read. The 1519 system was worked out on R1133 against its 1882 decipherment. Each column, a small sign over a base sign, is one plaintext letter with homophones: “oratore” = L/7 -/n u/7 x/7 c/7 m/n -/a, and c/7 = o, m/n = r, x/7 = t hold across the page. Each bracket-L sign stands for a whole word: the run [d][s][&] recurs where the decipherment has “de vostra maesta”. The 1519 letters turn out to be copies of reports to the King of France on the imperial election. A fifth of R1128 is code words, and only nine letter values are secure, so a language-model search gives nothing readable. Its clear part and Caprile’s next letter (“In un’altra mia ve mandai in zifra…”) say it concerned his own lawsuit at Rome. A second, careful transcription of R1133 and another 1882-paired letter would fill the table.
  • R1137 (Cistarelli, 25 July 1520). Two-digit groups, nearly all ending in 5 or 0, and 83 other signs, mixed with clear Italian. A ciphertext-only anneal settles part of the key (e = 0, 25; a = 86; o = 70; i = 45; t = 95; r = 85; s = 90; n = 65) and phrases such as “lettere per”, “presto”, “saria meglio”, “non se poteria” and “dua milla” (grade M). This is not a reading.
