The Rosamonde telegrams

→

Twenty-two telegrams in enciphered English between Sun Yat-sen and Soong Ching-ling, copied by Japanese officials in 1915–16 and left untranslated, now deciphered by Brice Dudout from the cipher alone.

Method: key recovered from ciphertext-only · Extent: partial

The documents
Twenty-two telegrams, 17 February 1915 to 13 May 1916: fifteen from 1915 between Tokyo and Shanghai, seven from May 1916 between Shanghai and Tokyo. Most are Sun Yat-sen to Soong Ching-ling; two or three go the other way.
Where
Diplomatic Archives of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan, 1.6.1.4-2-1-2, copies made for the Foreign Ministry by the Communications Bureau; JACAR B03050087600, B03050087700 (1915) and B03050089000 (May 1916). No translation was made at the time.
The cipher
English without spaces in ten-letter words, enciphered by twelve swapped letter pairs; d and p stay as they are. One key from February 1915 to May 1916.
How it was found
Brice Dudout recovered the key by simulated annealing scored by an English model, from the cipher words alone, and checked it on telegrams that were transcribed blind before the key was known.
What it says
A courtship and its sequel in Sun’s own English: “Whenever ye see Yung you inflict tor[t]ure [u]pon me”; in 1916, a dangerous iron trunk to be left with Inuzuka.
Credit
Brice Dudout, The Rosamonde telegrams (2026, CC BY 4.0), with Claude (Opus 5.5): discovery, transcription, key and edition. This page summarises his work; three telegrams were re-deciphered here from the scans.
Still open

01 The telegrams

From 1913 to 1917 the Japanese Ministry of Communications had the telegrams of Sun Yat-sen’s circle in Tokyo copied for the Foreign Ministry. The copies form the series “inquiry into the mail of designated persons” (特定人ノ発受郵便物取調), now digitised by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records. Most are in Chinese telegraph codes; the project’s page on Sun Yat-sen’s intercepted telegrams, 1916–17 covers the condenser systems, a different cipher.

In the 1915 volumes Brice Dudout noticed a separate family: ten-letter words with no pattern of consonants and vowels, but recurring groups such as hea. They leave Tokyo in the name of Tōyama Mitsuru (頭山滿), Sun’s neighbour and protector, for two Shanghai addresses, “Laisaosang, 445 Chaoufoong Road” and “Rosamonde, 628 E. Yuhang Road”. In May 1916 the same words travel between Shanghai and “Rosamonde Tokio”.

02 The system and the key

The plaintext is telegraphic English with the spaces removed, cut into ten-letter words. Each letter is replaced by its partner in a table of pairs, so the same table enciphers and deciphers:

a↔u  b↔g  c↔s  e↔o  f↔j  h↔y  i↔r  k↔z  l↔t  m↔w  n↔v  q↔x  · d and p unchanged

You becomes hea and love becomes teno; several clear telegrams of March 1915 to Rosamonde are simply signed “teno”. Names, sums and reply addresses stay in clear, and so do a few words (happy, Inuzuka, iron, trunk). The last word stops at ten letters, which cuts husba[nd] twice in May 1916. On 6 March 1915 the form twice has qq where pp is expected; Brice renders it [pp] and leaves the table as it is.

How the key was recovered. On Brice’s account the letter index of coincidence is 0.062, a natural language and not a code; f and q never occur and hea comes ten times. Simulated annealing over all substitutions, then over reciprocal ones only, scored with an English quadgram model, gave the key; the same search recovers an English control text enciphered with a random key. No crib or outside plaintext was used. The key taken from the 1915 telegrams alone then scores the 1916 forms, transcribed blind before the key was found, far above 2,000 random reciprocal tables (z = 7.5), and Brice’s own blind re-transcription of 1915 likewise (z = 10.9). A telegram in another system stays at chance (z = 0.6). The forms were transcribed twice and independently, by Claude and then by Brice without seeing Claude’s transcription; 89% of the letters agreed.

Checked here. The cipher words of three forms whose scans are in Brice’s repository (20 and 26 February, 7 July 1915: 20 words, 200 letters) were transcribed again from the images and deciphered with the table unchanged. The result agrees with his edition letter for letter, including the letters he corrects and the unreadable xa ins of 7 July; the statistical test in docs/_validate.py passes on these 200 letters. Two small points: the scan gives abon (g, not p) in “[u]pon”, a second slip that his brackets leave out, and the cipher has “for our soul are mingled” where his edition prints “souls”.

04 Who writes to whom

No form names either party; the attribution is Brice’s, built from independent facts that agree. “Rosamonde” (or Rosamond) was Soong Ching-ling’s English name, and in 1916 “Rosamonde Tokio” was a telegraphic address of Sun’s household.

  • The dates. Ching-ling was in Shanghai from 28 October 1914 to 17 March 1915. The Tokyo–Shanghai telegrams run from 17 February to 12 March, and on 15 March one goes by wireless to the steamer Korea, which brought her back (“Are you land Kobe or Yoko?”). She left again on 3 May; the run resumes on 10 June.
  • The relay. Zhu Zhuowen (“Chu”), Sun’s man in Shanghai, wrote on 2 and 4 February 1915 that he had set up an office for Miss Soong beside his own room, out of her mother’s sight, where she could be written to. On 20 February Tokyo wires “Must answer cable to Chu’s yesterday.”
  • The rumour of March 1915. On 9 March clear telegrams from Shanghai ask after Sun’s health, one of them to Rosamonde signed “teno”. Tokyo replies in the cipher, “I a[m] well and sound usual”, and the next day Sun asks Shanghai, in a condensed Chinese code, what rumours are going round about his safety.
  • Yung. A suitor named, with another, in a letter from her father Charlie Soong to Sun of 3 August 1915, published by Yang Tianshi; the telegrams name him five months earlier.
  • May 1916. The messages are signed “devoted husband” and addressed to “my own wife”; the iron trunk, Inuzuka Shintarō, Canton, Zhang Ji and the liner Porthos recur in Chinese-code telegrams of the same days. Ching-ling joined Sun in Shanghai on 19 May 1916.

The 11 March telegram is itself a lesson in the system’s weakness: encipher at home, never in the office, “You don’t use code book, so they can get our secrets by guessing.” Japanese officials copied every form but did not decipher them.

05 What remains uncertain

  • In the eleven complete telegrams of the first 1915 sample, about 45 of 750 letters are corrected (6%), nearly all copying confusions. The brackets in the table show each one.
  • Not deciphered: eight words in the middle of 9 March 1915; one word after “throw of[f]” and the end of “de[vou]t” on 25 February; one group on 7 July (the key gives lovesickxa instorture). The key is complete; these fail on the copies.
  • Probable, not proved: “Chang Ki” as Zhang Ji, “Tai” as Dai Jitao, Ching-ling as sender of 7 July, 8 and 13 May.
  • Unidentified: who stood behind the address “Laisaosang”, and Yung’s Chinese name.
  • Brice found the telegrams neither in the work on the 1916 intercepts nor in the studies of the marriage he checked (below); other works remain to be searched.
  • This project checked three of the twenty-two forms against the scans; the other nineteen rest on his transcription.
  • May 1916: Brice Dudout’s blind transcriptions of the seven forms (made on 15 October 2026, before the key was known), the decryption word by word and the table of every emendation are in the repository folder (dudout/). The emendations fall into a few cursive pairs: n/v 23 times, i/r 18, r/i 7, y/h 6. That makes them copying confusions rather than guesses, but they are still 109 of 627 letters, so the telegrams stay decoded, not read, under the rule that a reading may rest on at most 3% of look-alike letters.

JACAR B03050089000, seven telegrams, 1 - 13 May 1916 decoded, not read

Between Shanghai and 'Rosamonde Tokio' after Sun's return to China, May 1916: his safe landing in the French concession, Canton, and repeated orders to leave the iron trunk in Mr Inuzuka's care. Brice Dudout's readings; no cipher transcription of these forms is in the repository.

A mechanical decode with holes in most sentences: shown so that the transcription and the key can be checked and improved, not as a reading.
