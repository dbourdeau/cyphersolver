    Historical cryptography

    The cipher iceberg

    Everyone has heard of a few ciphers. Under them lie five centuries of secret letters, the people who broke them, and thousands of letters that no one has read yet.

    Scroll to dive ↓

    This is the tip.

    Caesar, Enigma, the Zodiac, the Voynich manuscript: the ciphers everyone has heard of are the part above water. About nine tenths of an iceberg is under it, because ice is about nine tenths as dense as sea water.

    Under the tip are five centuries of diplomatic letters, black chambers that opened the post of whole capitals, and thousands of letters that have never been read. The deeper you go, the fewer people have been there. Click anything on the ice to read what is down there, with its sources.

    Tier 1 · Above the water

    Everyone has heard of these

    Seven ciphers from films, true-crime documentaries and school projects. Each one has a second story under it.

    • The Caesar cipher 1st century BC

      Suetonius says Julius Caesar wrote confidential letters by putting each letter three places further down the alphabet, D for A. Less often told: Augustus used a shift of one and did not wrap around, writing AA for X.

      Suetonius, Divus Julius 56.6 and Divus Augustus 88; David Kahn, The Codebreakers (1967), ch. 2.

      On this site: seal your own letter in a real historical cipher

    • Enigma 1932–1945

      Bletchley Park gets the credit, but the Polish Cipher Bureau broke Enigma first. In December 1932 Marian Rejewski worked out the wiring of the rotors with permutation theory and documents a German spy had sold to France. Poland handed its methods and replica machines to Britain and France at Pyry in July 1939, five weeks before the invasion.

      Marian Rejewski, “How Polish mathematicians deciphered the Enigma”, Annals of the History of Computing 3 (1981); Kahn, Seizing the Enigma (1991).

    • The Zodiac Killer’s ciphers 1969–2020

      The 408-symbol cipher was read within days in August 1969 by a schoolteacher, Donald Harden, and his wife Bettye. The 340-symbol cipher held out for 51 years, until David Oranchak, Sam Blake and Jarl Van Eycke solved it in December 2020. It was a homophonic substitution whose text had been written out in diagonal steps, which is why ordinary solvers failed.

      FBI San Francisco statement, 11 December 2020; David Oranchak’s announcement of the solution, December 2020.

    • Mary, Queen of Scots 1586

      Walsingham’s decipherer Thomas Phelippes read the cipher letters smuggled to Mary in beer barrels, including her reply approving the Babington plot, and added a forged postscript asking for the names of the conspirators. Her cipher was a nomenclator: an alphabet with extra signs for common words and names. She was executed in February 1587.

      John Guy, My Heart Is My Own (2004); Kahn, The Codebreakers, ch. 4.

      On this site: Mary’s cipher letter to Archbishop Hamilton, 1569

    • The Voynich manuscript c. 1404–1438

      The vellum was radiocarbon-dated at the University of Arizona in 2009 to between 1404 and 1438, which, with a letter about the book from 1665 or 1666, makes it very unlikely that Voynich himself forged it in the 1910s. Its text behaves like a language in some tests and unlike any language in others. No proposed reading has been accepted.

      Beinecke Library, Yale, MS 408; René Zandbergen, voynich.nu.

      On this site: statistical tests on the Voynich text

    • The Navajo code talkers 1942–1945

      About four hundred Navajo Marines passed messages in the Pacific in a code built on their language, and it was never broken. They were not the first. In October 1918, Choctaw soldiers of the 36th Division were already passing orders by field telephone in the Meuse-Argonne.

      National WWII Museum; U.S. Army Center of Military History; William C. Meadows, The Comanche Code Talkers of World War II (2002).

    • Kryptos 1990–

      Jim Sanborn’s sculpture at CIA headquarters has four sections. The first three were solved in the 1990s. In September 2025 two writers found the plaintext of the fourth among Sanborn’s papers at the Smithsonian. Sanborn says they did not decipher it, and in November 2025 his Kryptos archive sold at auction for $962,500. The answer has not been published.

      Bruce Schneier, “Part Four of the Kryptos Sculpture” (October 2025); RR Auction, sale of November 2025.

      On this site: the top 25 unsolved ciphers

    Tier 2 · At the waterline

    The documentary layer

    A telegram that helped bring America into a war, a pamphlet about buried gold, a note to a young woman that nobody can read. Famous among people who watch history documentaries, and often told wrongly.

    • “The indecipherable cipher” 1553

      The cipher named after Blaise de Vigenère was published by Giovan Battista Bellaso in 1553, with an easy-to-remember key word. Vigenère’s own invention, in 1586, was the stronger autokey. Charles Babbage broke the repeating-key cipher around 1854 but never published, and Friedrich Kasiski published a general method in 1863.

      Bellaso, La cifra del Sig. Giovan Battista Bellaso (1553); Vigenère, Traicté des chiffres (1586); Kahn, ch. 4 and 6.

    • The Zimmermann telegram 1917

      Germany offered Mexico an alliance against the United States. In Room 40 of the British Admiralty, Nigel de Grey and William Montgomery read it. To hide that Britain was reading American cables, they obtained the copy re-sent in an older code from Washington to Mexico City and showed the Americans that one. The United States entered the war that April.

      Thomas Boghardt, The Zimmermann Telegram (2012); Kahn, ch. 9.

      On this site: Bernstorff’s telegram No. 205, 1917

    • The Beale papers 1885

      A Virginia pamphlet printed three number ciphers said to give the place of buried gold. The second reads if each number stands for the first letter of that word in the Declaration of Independence. The other two have never been read. Decoded the same way, the first gives strings like ABFDEFGHIIJKLMMNOHPP, which most cryptanalysts take as the mark of a hoax.

      James J. Gillogly, “The Beale Cipher: A Dissenting Opinion”, Cryptologia 4 (1980).

      On this site: the top 25 unsolved ciphers

    • The Dorabella cipher 1897

      Edward Elgar sent 87 little semicircles in three lines to Dora Penny, the “Dorabella” of his Enigma Variations, on 14 July 1897. She never read them, and nobody has since. Elgar was a keen amateur cryptographer, so it may well be a real cipher, perhaps with a private joke in it.

      Mrs Richard Powell (Dora Penny), Edward Elgar: Memories of a Variation (1937).

      On this site: the Dorabella cipher, attempted

    • ADFGVX 1918

      Only six letters, chosen because they sound unalike in Morse. Each letter of the message became a pair of them, and the pairs were then shuffled by a key word. The French cryptanalyst Georges Painvin broke a key of early June 1918 after weeks of work in which, Kahn says, he lost some fifteen kilos. The message showed where Ludendorff would attack next.

      Kahn, ch. 10; Lasry, Niebel, Kopal, Wacker, “Deciphering ADFGVX messages from the Eastern Front of World War I”, Cryptologia 41 (2017).

      On this site: twenty-two garbled ADFGVX messages of November 1918

    • The Playfair cipher 1854

      Invented by Charles Wheatstone and named after his friend Lyon Playfair, who promoted it. It enciphers letters two at a time from a 5×5 square, which flattens the letter counts that break simple ciphers. The British used it in the Boer War and the First World War.

      Kahn, ch. 6.

    • The scytale 5th century BC?

      Every textbook calls the Spartan baton the first cipher device: wind a strip round a rod, write along it, unwind. The earliest sources only mention a message stick. The use as a cipher is first described centuries later, and Thomas Kelly argued that the cipher story may be a later invention.

      Thomas Kelly, “The Myth of the Skytale”, Cryptologia 22 (1998).

    • Pigpen 1587

      The grid alphabet sold as the “Freemasons’ cipher” was in use long before eighteenth-century lodges took it up. In 1587 a servant of Walsingham wrote from the fleet sent to relieve Sluys in a three-grid pigpen.

      British Library, Harley MS 287 ff. 39–40.

      On this site: Francis Needham to Walsingham, 1587

    Tier 3 · Shallow water

    Where the enthusiasts swim

    The inventors, the first codebreaking manuals and the people who read other people’s post. Popular books on cryptography usually get this far.

    • Al-Kindi and frequency analysis 9th century

      The oldest known description of how to break a cipher by counting letters is in the Baghdad philosopher al-Kindi’s Risāla fī istikhrāj al-muʿammā, “On deciphering cryptographic messages”. The treatise was found in the Süleymaniye library in Istanbul and published in 1987.

      M. Mrayati, Y. Meer Alam, M. H. at-Tayyan, Arabic Origins of Cryptology, vol. 1 (2003).

    • Alberti’s cipher disk 1466–67

      The architect Leon Battista Alberti described two concentric disks of letters. Turning the inner one every few words changed the cipher alphabet, which is the first polyalphabetic cipher in the West. He also put the numbers 1 to 4 on the disk and used them for code words, then enciphered them with the rest: the first enciphered code.

      Alberti, De componendis cifris (1466–67); Kahn, ch. 4.

    • Trithemius’s angels 1499–1606

      The abbot Johannes Trithemius wrote the first printed book on cryptography, the Polygraphia (1518). His Steganographia looked like a manual for sending messages by summoning spirits, and it was put on the Index. In 1996 and 1998 Thomas Ernst and Jim Reeds showed that the number tables of its third book are themselves enciphered messages.

      Jim Reeds, “Solved: The Ciphers in Book III of Trithemius’s Steganographia”, Cryptologia 22 (1998); Thomas Ernst, Daphnis 25 (1996).

    • The Great Cipher of Louis XIV 1680s–1890s

      Antoine and Bonaventure Rossignol built a code of about 587 numbers for syllables, with traps such as a number that cancels the one before it. Nobody could read the letters written in it for two hundred years, until Étienne Bazeries broke it in the 1890s. His claim that one letter names the Man in the Iron Mask is not accepted by historians.

      Kahn, ch. 5; Bazeries, Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés (1901).

    • The Copiale cipher 18th century

      A 105-page bound book with about 75,000 handwritten characters, abstract signs mixed with Roman and Greek letters. In 2011 Kevin Knight, Beáta Megyesi and Christiane Schaefer found that the plain Roman letters only marked the breaks between words, while the accented letters and the signs spelled German: the initiation ritual of the Oculists, a secret society whose new members had a hair plucked from their eyebrow as a mock eye operation.

      Knight, Megyesi and Schaefer, “The Copiale Cipher”, Proceedings of the 4th Workshop on Building and Using Comparable Corpora, ACL (2011).

    • The black chamber of Vienna 18th century

      In Vienna the post for the embassies went first to the Geheime Kabinets-Kanzlei. The bags arrived at seven in the morning. The letters were opened, copied, resealed and back in the post by about half past nine, and the copies were deciphered at leisure.

      Kahn, ch. 5.

    • Elizebeth Smith Friedman 1920s–1940s

      She broke the codes of Prohibition rum-runners for the Coast Guard. In the Second World War her unit read the Enigma traffic of German spy networks in South America, while J. Edgar Hoover’s FBI took the public credit. Her part was secret until long after her death.

      Jason Fagone, The Woman Who Smashed Codes (2017).

    • Jefferson’s wheel cypher c. 1795

      Thomas Jefferson described a stack of 36 wooden disks, each with a shuffled alphabet on its rim. The idea was forgotten, reinvented by Étienne Bazeries in 1891, and adopted by the U.S. Army as the M-94 in 1922, more than a century after Jefferson.

      Kahn, ch. 7; Library of Congress, Jefferson Papers.

      On this site: Jefferson’s Code No. 11, 1786–1791

    • Venona 1943–1980

      A one-time pad is unbreakable if each page is used once. In 1942 a Soviet factory under wartime pressure printed some pages twice. U.S. Army codebreakers, starting in 1943, used the repeats to read parts of about three thousand messages, which exposed Klaus Fuchs, the Rosenbergs and the Cambridge spies. The programme stayed secret until 1995.

      Robert L. Benson, The Venona Story, NSA Center for Cryptologic History (2001).

    Tier 4 · Deep water

    The cipher that ran Europe

    For about four hundred years, kings, popes and ambassadors used one kind of cipher. Hardly anyone outside the field knows its name: the nomenclator.

    • The nomenclator c. 1400–1850

      A cipher alphabet, often with several signs for each common letter, joined to a list of code signs for names, places and frequent words: the Pope might be 22, the King of Spain a triangle. Nearly every enciphered letter in Europe’s archives from these centuries uses one. Each ambassador usually had his own, and a lost key can leave a whole embassy’s letters unread.

      Kahn, ch. 3; Beáta Megyesi et al., “Historical Cryptology”, ch. 3 of Bernhard Esslinger, Learning and Experiencing Cryptography with CrypTool and SageMath (2023).

      On this site: how cipher keys changed, 1400 to 1949

    • The first homophones 1401

      The oldest known key that gives several signs to the same letter was made in Mantua in 1401 for correspondence with one Simeone de Crema. Each vowel has extra signs, so that counting letters no longer gives the vowels away. Codebreaking by frequency must already have been a threat in Italy by then.

      Aloys Meister, Die Anfänge der modernen diplomatischen Geheimschrift (1902); Kahn, ch. 3.

      On this site: Forlì to Gianfrancesco Gonzaga, 1428

    • Giovanni Soro of Venice 1506–1544

      Venice appointed Soro cipher secretary in 1506, and he is often called the first great Western cryptanalyst. He read the ciphers of Milan, the Empire and the Pope. Venice kept its cipher secretaries in the Doge’s Palace and trained their successors there.

      Kahn, ch. 3; Paolo Preto, I servizi segreti di Venezia (1994).

    • Viète and the sorcery complaint 1590

      François Viète, one of the founders of algebra, read Spanish cipher letters for Henri IV. When Philip II found out, he complained to the Pope that the French could only have done it by black magic. The Pope’s own cipher office had been reading Spanish letters too, and nothing came of it.

      Kahn, ch. 4.

    • Rossignol at Réalmont 1628

      During the siege of the Huguenot town of Réalmont, a young mathematician, Antoine Rossignol, deciphered an intercepted letter the same day. It said the town was out of munitions. The town surrendered and Richelieu took Rossignol into his service. He and his son founded the French tradition of state cryptology.

      Kahn, ch. 5.

      On this site: Cardinal Richelieu to Rancé, 1629

    • John Wallis 1643–1703

      The Oxford mathematician who gave us the symbol ∞ deciphered intercepted letters for Parliament from 1643, and later for William III. When Leibniz asked for Hanoverians to be trained, Wallis held back, so as not to let the art leave England.

      Philip Beeley and Christoph Scriba (eds.), The Correspondence of John Wallis (2003–); Kahn, ch. 5.

    • England’s Deciphering Branch 1716–1844

      For more than a century the decipherers to the King were the Willes family. Edward Willes became Bishop of Bath and Wells and kept the work, and so did his sons. The branch was closed in 1844 after it came out that the post office had opened the letters of the Italian exile Giuseppe Mazzini.

      Kenneth Ellis, The Post Office in the Eighteenth Century (1958).

    • The Argenti, papal cipher secretaries c. 1590–1605

      Giovanni Battista Argenti and his nephew Matteo made the Pope’s ciphers in the late sixteenth century. Matteo left a long manual describing papal practice: alphabets mixed by a key word, nulls, and code words for the names of popes and princes.

      Aloys Meister, Die Geheimschrift im Dienste der päpstlichen Kurie (1906); Kahn, ch. 3.

      On this site: Cardinal Farnese to the nuncio Poggio, 1542

    • The Great Paris Cipher 1811–1813

      Napoleon’s armies in Spain used a code of about 1,400 numbers. George Scovell, an officer on Wellington’s staff, rebuilt it from captured letters, and by the end of 1812 Wellington could read the letters between King Joseph and his marshals.

      Mark Urban, The Man Who Broke Napoleon’s Codes (2001).

      On this site: King Joseph to Napoleon, December 1812

    Tier 5 · The abyss

    Where the archives are

    Letters sealed in the sixteenth century are still being read for the first time, some of them in this decade. The work is slow: find the letter, transcribe every sign, then rebuild a key that no one has seen for four hundred years.

    • The unread letters today

      Nobody knows how many enciphered letters lie in Simancas, the Vatican, Venice, Vienna, Paris and London, because nobody has counted them. Many were never deciphered, or their decipherment is lost. The DECODE database, built up at Uppsala since the late 2010s, is the first attempt to gather them, with images and keys.

      Megyesi et al., “Decryption of historical manuscripts: the DECODE project”, Cryptologia 44 (2020).

      On this site: the catalogue of open ciphers

    • Mary Stuart’s lost letters 2023

      In the Bibliothèque nationale de France, George Lasry, Norbert Biermann and Satoshi Tomokiyo found 57 cipher letters filed as Italian documents. They were Mary’s letters from captivity to the French ambassador Castelnau, 1578–1584. A computer search recovered the key, and hand work did the rest.

      Lasry, Biermann, Tomokiyo, “Deciphering Mary Stuart’s lost letters from 1578–1584”, Cryptologia 47 (2023).

    • Charles V’s letter to Saint-Mauris 1547 / 2022

      A cipher letter of the Emperor to his ambassador in France had sat in the Bibliothèque Stanislas in Nancy. In 2022 Cécile Pierrot and the historian Camille Desenclos deciphered it. It mentions rumours of a plot to murder Charles.

      Inria and CNRS press release, 23 November 2022.

    • A lost solution, found again c. 1330–1370 / 2026

      Penn LJS 51 is a Mamluk secretary’s own account of the cipher verses he traded with colleagues: a message in an alphabet made up for the occasion, then his solution. On the last page, Ibn al-Qabāqibī’s seven lines of 22 signs stand without one, because the book breaks off. This site rebuilt the key from five red letters, a repeated word and the metre: two love verses, one damaged word unread.

      University of Pennsylvania, Kislak Center, LJS 51, f. 106v (OPenn); corrections by Alan Elbaum, 23 November 2026.

      On this site: Ibn al-Qabāqibī’s cipher verses, the full write-up

    • Cryptiana 2000s–

      Satoshi Tomokiyo’s website is one person’s reference work of hundreds of historical keys and decipherments, from Renaissance Italy to the American Revolution. On it, “undeciphered” means that no decipherment of the time survives, not that nobody has read the letter.

      Satoshi Tomokiyo, Cryptiana: Articles on Historical Cryptography.

    • Hill-climbing the past 2018

      Ciphertext-only attacks now use search methods such as hill-climbing and simulated annealing: guess a key, score the decryption against a language model, change the key slightly, keep what scores better. George Lasry’s thesis set this out for transposition ciphers, ADFGVX, the Hagelin M-209, the Chaocipher, Enigma and Playfair. The same approach read the Mary Stuart letters.

      George Lasry, A Methodology for the Cryptanalysis of Classical Ciphers with Search Metaheuristics, Kassel University Press (2018).

      On this site: how each cipher was solved, and the terms used

    • The Chaocipher 1918–2010

      John F. Byrne, a friend of James Joyce, built a cipher device in a cigar box and tried for decades to sell it to the U.S. Navy and State Department, publishing challenge texts no one could break. His family gave the device to the National Cryptologic Museum, and Moshe Rubin published how it worked in 2010.

      Moshe Rubin, “Chaocipher Revealed: The Algorithm” (2010); John F. Byrne, Silent Years (1953).

    • Telegraph codebooks 1870s–1930s

      Telegrams were charged by the word, so merchants bought codebooks in which one word stood for a whole phrase. The wool dealer Frank Primrose sent his agent the code word BAY; it arrived as BUY, and the purchases that followed cost him a large sum. In Primrose v. Western Union (1894) the Supreme Court held the company was liable only for the cost of the telegram.

      Primrose v. Western Union Telegraph Co., 154 U.S. 1 (1894).

      On this site: the Swatow telegram to Sun Yat-sen, 1916

    • The Confederacy’s Vigenère 1861–1865

      The South used the Vigenère cipher with a handful of key phrases, among them MANCHESTER BLUFF, COMPLETE VICTORY and COME RETRIBUTION, and often left the word breaks in. Union telegraphers read much of it.

      Kahn, ch. 7.

      On this site: Johnston to Seddon during the siege of Vicksburg, 1863

    Tier 6 · The trench

    Older than the codebooks

    Tombs, clay tablets, scripture, poems and swords. The first secret writing is older than the word “cipher”, and much of it was not hiding anything from enemies.

    • The tomb of Khnumhotep II c. 1900 BC

      In a tomb at Beni Hasan in Egypt, a scribe wrote part of the inscription with unusual hieroglyphs in place of the ordinary ones. It was meant to impress, not to hide anything, but Kahn calls it the first deliberate transformation of writing, where the history of cryptology begins.

      Kahn, ch. 2.

    • A potter’s secret recipe c. 1500 BC

      A small cuneiform tablet from Seleucia on the Tigris gives a formula for pottery glaze. The scribe wrote the signs in their rarest sound values, apparently to keep a trade secret from rivals.

      Kahn, ch. 2.

    • Atbash in the Book of Jeremiah 6th century BC

      Atbash swaps the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet with the last, the second with the second-to-last, and so on. In Jeremiah, “Sheshach” (25:26, 51:41) is Babel written in Atbash, and “Leb Kamai” (51:1) is Kasdim, the Chaldeans.

      Kahn, ch. 2; standard commentaries on Jeremiah.

    • Secret writing in the Kama Sutra c. 3rd century

      Vatsyayana’s list of the 64 arts includes mlecchita-vikalpa, the art of secret writing. A later commentator describes a substitution in which letters are swapped in pairs.

      Kahn, ch. 2.

    • Ciphers of the Mamluk chancery 14th century

      Ibn ad-Durayhim (1312–1361) wrote a treatise with tables of letter frequency and a worked example of breaking a cipher. It survives because al-Qalqashandi copied it into his chancery manual in 1412. Secretaries of the period also wrote love poems in cipher and set them to each other as puzzles, like the leaf of Penn LJS 51 drifting in the abyss.

      Mrayati, Meer Alam, at-Tayyan, Arabic Origins of Cryptology, vol. 2–3; al-Qalqashandi, Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā.

      On this site: Ibn al-Qabāqibī’s cipher verses, about 1330–1370

    • An antipope’s cipher letters 1399–1403

      During the Great Schism, Benedict XIII, the Avignon pope, wrote to his confidant in Barcelona in cipher. Aloys Meister reported the letters in 1906 without a decipherment. A Barcelona canon, Puig y Puig, deciphered and printed them in 1920, and the book was then largely forgotten.

      Sebastián Puig y Puig, Pedro de Luna, último papa de Aviñón (1920).

      On this site: Benedict XIII’s cipher letters to Francesc Climent

    • Chaucer’s cipher? 1393

      A Middle English treatise on an astronomical instrument, the Equatorie of the Planetis, has short passages in a cipher alphabet. In 1955 Derek Price attributed it to Chaucer, which would make them Chaucer’s ciphers. Kari Anne Rand has since argued convincingly that the author was the monk John Westwyk.

      Kari Anne Rand, “The authorship of The Equatorie of the Planetis revisited”, Studia Neophilologica 87 (2015); Cambridge, Peterhouse MS 75.I.

    • Hildegard’s unknown language 12th century

      Hildegard of Bingen wrote down a Lingua ignota of about a thousand invented words, with its own alphabet of 23 letters. It is not a cipher, but it is one of the oldest constructed languages and secret alphabets known, and its purpose is still debated.

      Sarah Higley, Hildegard of Bingen’s Unknown Language (2007).

    • The sword inscriptions c. 1250–1330

      Medieval swords sometimes carry strings of letters inlaid in the blade that spell no word, like the eighteen gold letters on the River Witham sword in the British Museum. They are often read as the first letters of the words of a prayer. None has a sure reading.

      British Museum, 1858,1116.4.

      On this site: the River Witham sword inscription, attempted

    • Cipher runes c. 800

      The Rök runestone in Sweden carries the longest runic inscription known, and parts of it are in cipher runes: runes replaced by their position in the rune row, and passages written in an older alphabet.

      Rundata, Ög 136; Per Holmberg et al., “The Rök Runestone and the End of the World”, Futhark 9–10 (2020).

      On this site: the cipher runes of the Rök stone

    Tier 7 · The bottom

    What nobody has read

    Writing systems whose language is lost, books in unknown scripts, and the letters in archives that no one has read yet. Some may never be read. One of them was, in 2022.

    • Linear A c. 1800–1450 BC

      The Minoan script of Crete. Many signs can be sounded out with the values of the later Linear B, so the texts can be pronounced roughly, but the language behind them is unknown.

      John Younger, Linear A Texts in Phonetic Transcription; Ester Salgarella, Aegean Linear Script(s) (2020).

    • The Indus script c. 2600–1900 BC

      Several thousand inscribed seals and objects, most of them only about five signs long. There is no bilingual text and no agreement on the language, and some scholars have argued that the signs are not writing at all.

      Asko Parpola, Deciphering the Indus Script (1994); Farmer, Sproat and Witzel, EJVS 11 (2004).

    • Rongorongo 15th century?

      About two dozen inscribed wooden objects from Rapa Nui (Easter Island). In 2024 radiocarbon dating put the wood of one tablet in the fifteenth century, before European contact, which supports an independent invention of writing on the island.

      Silvia Ferrara et al., “The invention of writing on Rapa Nui”, Scientific Reports 14 (2024).

    • The Phaistos disc c. 1700 BC?

      A clay disc from Crete with about 240 signs stamped in a spiral, found in 1908: some of the earliest printing with movable stamps. There is only one such text, which is probably too little ever to read it.

      Yves Duhoux, Le disque de Phaestos (1977).

    • The Rohonc Codex surfaced 1838

      A book of 448 pages in an unknown script, with pictures, given to the Hungarian Academy of Sciences in 1838. Many readings have been proposed and none accepted, and its date and origin are disputed.

      Benedek Láng, “Why Don’t We Decipher an Outdated Cipher System? The Codex of Rohonc”, Cryptologia 34 (2010).

    • Linear Elamite, read 2022

      For a century this script of ancient Iran was counted among the undeciphered. In 2022 François Desset and colleagues published a decipherment built on silver vessels with royal names. Not every detail is accepted, but it shows that “undeciphered” can end.

      Desset, Tabibzadeh, Kervran, Basello, Marchesi, “The Decipherment of Linear Elamite Writing”, Zeitschrift für Assyriologie 112 (2022).

    • The letters no one has read yet now

      This site keeps a ledger of historical cipher letters and what became of them: 689 write-ups so far, and 2,204 vetted ciphers in the catalogue still open. Each glowing point on the sea floor is one of them, waiting in an archive for a reader.

      The catalogue on this site, built from DECODE, Cryptiana, archive catalogues and our own sweeps.

      On this site: pick an open cipher from the catalogue

    How the iceberg was drawn

    Daniel Bourdeau · posted 27 November 2026

    An “iceberg” is an internet picture that ranks the parts of a subject by how far into it you have to go before you meet them. This one ranks fifty-nine stories of secret writing. The tier of each is a judgement, not a measurement. Roughly, the tip is what a general audience knows from film and television, the waterline is what history documentaries cover, shallow water is the ground of popular books such as Simon Singh’s The Code Book, deep water is David Kahn’s The Codebreakers and the specialist literature, and the abyss is current research in the archives. The trench and the bottom are ordered by age and by how little is known, not by obscurity alone.

    Every entry names its sources. Dates are those of the cipher or the event, not of its solution, except where both are given. The scene takes its shape from “The Last Boring Slide”, an interactive three.js talk built with Claude, in which a red line breaks out of a chart and runs through the world; here it runs down the ice. The leaf drifting in the abyss is f. 106v of Penn LJS 51, read on this site; its image is from the University of Pennsylvania, Kislak Center, via OPenn (public domain). It is drawn with three.js, and the sound is made live in the browser with the Web Audio API. Without WebGL the page shows the same entries as a plain list.
