The Leticia cipher telegrams, September 1932

Telegrams from the first weeks of the Colombia–Peru crisis, on gunboats, ammunition and troops, read with a key issued by the Ministry of War itself in 1931.

The telegrams
Seventeen cipher telegrams between the Ministry of War in Bogotá and Gen. Amadeo Rodríguez Vergara, civil and military chief of the Amazonas at Puerto Asís, 9–27 September 1932, after the Peruvian seizure of Leticia on 1 September.
Where
Archivo General de la Nación Colombia, Archivos Privados, Fondo General Amadeo Rodríguez Vergara, Asuntos militares, CO.AGN.AP.GARV..2.2.4, ff. 85–177 (on DataViva).
The cipher
Seven simple-substitution alphabets, one for each weekday, vowels to vowels, with the day name and D (forwards) or R (reversed) in clear at the head. All the telegrams seen are D.
How it was deciphered
With the Sección I key of 31 January 1931, kept in the same fonds (GARV..1.1.4 f. 44r). It scores 0.977 of characters on a held-out telegram against 0.381 for the best of 200 permuted keys.
What it says
Ammunition from Bofors, the gunboats Cartagena, Santa Marta and Barranquilla, the state of the troops (499 men, corrected to 599), the move to Caucaýa, the passage of Güepí, the Napo and the risk of a conflict with Ecuador, Peruvian reinforcements to Loreto, and the censorship of soldiers’ letters.
Still open
Telegram No. 143 (f. 151), about 160 numbers in a numeric system for which no key is in hand, and four unresolved words.
Credit
Daniel Bourdeau transcribed the key and read ff. 149 and 153 first; Brice Dudout (Europe chiffrée), working with Claude Opus 5.5 agents, transcribed and read the other telegrams.

01 The telegrams

The Peruvian seizure of Leticia on 1 September 1932 opened the Colombia–Peru conflict. The telegrams are the traffic of the first weeks between the Ministry of War and the general at Puerto Asís on the Putumayo. Five of them have a contemporary clear text in the same file (ff. 117, 127, 137, 139 and 157), and f. 141 carries a pencil gloss of its last sentence; for these the reading is a verification of the period decipherment, not a new reading. The other eleven have no decipherment in the file. Copies are counted once (ff. 106 = 107, 132 = 125, 152 = 153, 154 = 158).

02 The key and its test

The Ministry’s key sheet (Sección I, “Criptografía”, 31 January 1931) gives the cipher letter of each plain letter for every weekday; its rules are on f. 43r. Daniel Bourdeau transcribed the table on 28 October 2026 and Brice Dudout transcribed it again independently on 4 November: the two tables are identical. Many slips of keying or transmission remain in the telegrams (b/h, i/e/a, dropped letters); f. 134 is headed “Martes D” but is enciphered with the Monday alphabet, and ff. 109, 141 and 170 have no heading.

One pre-registered measurement was made on a held-out telegram (No. 82, 661 letters): 0.977 of characters and 0.854 of words, against 0.381 and 0.039 for the best of 200 permuted keys.

03 The reading

The 787 cipher words give 759 certain, 24 probable and 4 unresolved: 99.5%. The clear words of most telegrams were left out of the transcriptions, so the reading gives the cipher parts. For the numeric telegram No. 143 (Lt-Col. Paz, 25 September) the weekday key does not apply; the Presidency key (GARV..2.2.1 ff. 6v, 8, 10) is a candidate and has not been tried. The Spanish and an English translation are under Full texts.

04 What remains uncertain

  • Telegram No. 143 on f. 151: about 160 numbers from 1 to 181 in another system, with no key in hand.
  • Four unresolved words (zeffe on f. 107, pascama on f. 153, hsla on f. 159, gixhtlo on f. 170). The sentence on f. 153 does not fully construe around “relator … yo”, and f. 159 should be re-read at full resolution.
  • The historiography of the Colombia–Peru war was not searched beyond the catalogue’s searches of 3 November; no edition or study of these telegrams was found. Other files of the fonds (GARV..2.3.1, 2.3.2, 2.4.1, “con traducción”) were not opened.
  • The DataViva images carry no explicit licence and are not reproduced here.

# Reading (Spanish), cipher words only, Daniel's rule

Plain word = certain (mechanical decoding gives the expected Spanish word, or a one-letter keying slip of a kind seen in the held-out test); {x} = probable (two or more letters corrected, or a proper name not attested elsewhere); ‹x› = unresolved. No restorations. Clear words of the telegrams are not counted and are mostly left out; copies are counted once. Telegraphic style, no punctuation in the originals.
