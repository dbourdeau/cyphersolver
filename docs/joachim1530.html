402 proposed signs in nine account entries

London → Francis I · undetermined cipher · 15 March 1530 · not read

Passano's ciphered account from London

BnF Clairambault 331, ff. 156–157. A mostly clear Italian diplomatic letter on English affairs ends in a ciphered account of payments. The account is newly transcribed here, but no defensible decipherment was found.

Daniel Bourdeau · attempted 23 September 2026

not read

Summary. A copy of Giovan Gioacchino da Passano's letter to Francis I, dated 15 March 1530, survives in Clairambault 331. Its discussion of Henry VIII's divorce and dealings with Cardinal Wolsey is largely in clear Italian. An appended account occupies the next leaf, with nine enciphered entries and clear scudi amounts. Tomokiyo noted the cipher as undeciphered. The 402 proposed signs have been transcribed from Gallica images and tested against several language models and cipher hypotheses. An ordinary monoalphabetic substitution is not supported by a controlled test, but dotted and similar-looking signs may have been conflated in the provisional transcription. No sign has a verified plaintext value; the account remains unread.

01 The letter and account

The heading calls this a copy of a letter to the king and an enclosed letter for the Grand Master, dated 15 March. Passano wrote from London. The clear text concerns the king of England, the papacy, the Emperor, and Cardinal Wolsey. Just before the cipher, Passano speaks of recovering payments and quittances, including the eight instalments and payments over four years, with May and November 1528 and May 1529 mentioned. The clear words identify the business of the account, not the wording of its entries.

On the continuation the copyist writes that a page and three lines in cipher follow, then resumes clear Italian. The nine entries each end with an amount in scudi written outside the cipher. The signs themselves run without reliable word spaces.

02 What can be measured

The research transcription in ciphertext.txt has 402 proposed signs in 20 alias classes across both sides of f. 157. Its index of coincidence is 0.0819; 4 occurs 58 times and 3 51 times. These counts depend on visually grouping ambiguous cursive forms. Dots over some signs may be significant, and the transcription is not diplomatic. There is no reliable sign-to-letter key.

03 Tests and limits

One-to-one substitution search was run with period and modern Italian, French, Spanish, Latin, and English models; reversing and swapping adjacent signs did not help. Frequent signs were tried as spaces and nulls, and a many-to-one mapping and Italian word segmentation were tried. None produced connected language. The solver was checked on a same-length, same-entry-structure artificial Italian cryptogram: it recovered all 20 assignments, and recovered 18 and 17 with 10% and 20% random sign corruption. That comparison argues against simple substitution on the present sign inventory. It does not exclude a different cipher, systematic misclassification of glyphs, or missing key material.

The neighbouring cipher in the same volume and the Passano/Sormano cipher in BnF fr. 3096 have different sign inventories. A related Passano report printed by Le Grand and calendared in Letters and Papers Henry VIII no. 6307 has a different date and cannot be used as a parallel plaintext for these entries.

04 What would finish it

All 402 proposed signs are open. The next useful evidence would be an independent copy of this exact account, its contemporary decipherment, or Passano's matching London key. Before a new automated attack, the dotted and lookalike forms must be checked again against the full-resolution manuscript. The clear context is substantial, but it cannot justify guessed words in the cipher.

05 Sources

BnF Clairambault 331, Gallica, ff. 156–157 (digital canvases f148–f150); S. Tomokiyo, French ciphers in the age of Francis I; P. Le Grand, Histoire du divorce de Henry VIII, vol. 3; Letters and Papers no. 6307 (related, not the same letter).
