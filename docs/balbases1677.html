The marqués de los Balbases to Baltasar de Fuenmayor, 1677–78

At a glanceExisting decipherment · partial (70% of the cipher)

The letters
Fourteen ciphered letters from the marqués de los Balbases, a Spanish plenipotentiary at Nijmegen, to Baltasar de Fuenmayor at Copenhagen, 1677–78.
Where
Archives générales du Royaume, Brussels, SEG 2559. DECODE calls them “partially decrypted”, with the solution in the margin.
The cipher
Single letters and consonant-vowel syllables in numbers, disguised and reversed letter pairs, about twenty word codes and nulls.
How it was read
No key survives and none of the eight Brussels keys fits. An alignment program, started from cribs, rebuilt the table from the ten margin decipherments and applied it to the four letters without one.
What it says
The Imperial protest over the dissolution of the Bremen assembly, and the fear after the fall of Ghent: “con la pérdida de Gante…”
Still open
A handful of rare name codes, and homophones blurred by 4/9-type transcription slips.

01 The letters

Fourteen letters, all from Nijmegen, dated from the clear parts:

  • With a margin decipherment (ten): R988 (29 June 1677), R987 (18 June), R985 (3 August), R986 (28 August), R991 (5 October?), R990 (19 October), R989 (21 December), R993 (28 December 1677), R995 (26 January 1678), R994 (15 February).
  • With no decipherment (four): R992 (19 September 1677), R996 (1 March 1678), R997 (8 March), R998 (22 March).

Each letter opens with courtesies in clear, then gives a cipher block. Two letters (R985, R986) enclose a French clear paper on the Lorraine propositions.

02 The key

This is a syllabic nomenclator with homophones. Groups are separated by spaces.

  • Letters.
    • Vowels: a 21, 3+; e 2+; i 22, 67; o 18.
    • Consonants: b 10, n; c 9; d e; f x; g 16; j u+; l 8, 19; n y, 24, 20; p 26; r 23, 31; s 28, B; t 27; y 25; z h.
    • The closing consonants s, n and r are the three commonest groups.
  • Syllables in numbers.
    • de 46, la 55, en 76, ra 65, re 66, te 71, do 48, ta 70, lo 58, no 63, to 73, da 45, ti 72, di 47
    • le 56, ni 62, vi 42, ne 61, in 77, ve 41, va 40, na 60, on 78, an 75, li 57, ga 50, go 53, tu 74
    • vo 43, ar 80, gu 54, un 79, er 81, du 49, nu 64, ro 68, tr 188
  • Letter pairs: a syllable with the vowel kept and the consonant disguised.
    • c is written x, d or l: xo, do and lo all stand for co; the same holds for ci, ca, ce and cu.
    • f is written n: no fo, ni fi, ne fe, na fa, nu fu.
    • y is written r: ro yo, ra ya.
  • Vowel-first pairs: the syllable reversed, with the consonant disguised.
    • s is written f: ef se, if si, af sa, of so, uf su. Also ye es, ya as, yo os.
    • p is written d: ad pa, ed pe, od po, ud pu.
    • m is written c: ac ma, ec me, ic mi, oc mo, uc mu.
    • h is written q or g: aq/ag ha, eq/eg he, og ho.
    • Others: en em, ot ch, as za.
  • Word codes.
    • que: d+ (struck d), b+. de: p+, g+.
    • V.S.: H. para: 05. por: 15, is. sobre: 184. quando: 148. alguno: 100. congreso: 120.
    • ministro: des. materia: dus. duque: ep. embax[ador]: §. -mente: dan. nuestro: fe.
    • Flandes: ban. Francia: bef. franceses: bin. Suecia: 163. Dinamarca: or. -bourg: 105.
  • Nulls. 13+ and most 13s, 300, 200, 700, 07, 01, 18+, 16+, 35. Many of these mark the start or end of a cipher block.

A few examples:

  • 26 67 y di ed 28 = pr·i·n·ci·pe·s
  • xo y no 23 ic 45 e = co·n·fo·r·mi·da·d
  • xo 20 41 62 76 = co·n·ve·ni·en[te]

03 The four letters without a decipherment

Decoded, not read (reading audit, 22 October 2026): the key decodes these letters group by group into syllable streams that do not yet build sentences throughout. On 22 October each stream was divided into Spanish words token by token (read/R992_reading.md and the others); these partial readings have unread groups ([...]), unidentified codes ([85], [189]) and words only partly carried by the figures ([?word]) in most sentences. What follows is a summary of what can be made out, not a reading; the quotations are from the partial readings, holes kept. The group-by-group text is in targets/balbases1677/read/.

  • 19 September 1677 (R992).
    • Balbases has seen the protest the Imperial ministers published over the dissolution of the Bremen assembly. “pues de nuestra parte no nos [85] que hacer, sino es [...] por nuestra desgracia [...] temer” a violent resolution of the confederates.
    • After Charleroi they may insist at the union that the conquests be shared out before anything positive is settled.
    • A rumour about the Danish chancellor and the Duke of Celle. He gives it no credit, “pues V.S. no lo ha penetrado ni me lo avisa[ra]”.
    • The claim that the duke would take his share of his father’s conquests, the duchies of Bremen and Verden.
  • 1 March 1678 (R996).
    • Nothing in Sweden could undo “la desconfianza que arguimos hacia nuestros aliados”. What causes it is “la miserable postura en que [182] permanecen nuestras cosas”.
    • The Brunswicks’ proceedings rest on solid suspicions.
    • The ministers of the northern princes had thought of writing a protest about treaties of peace that would leave out their participation. He praises Fuenmayor’s diligence in avoiding that step.
    • Strasbourg’s lack of constancy.
    • A French gentleman has come back from France “satisfecho del Re[y] [...]ísimo”.
  • 8 March 1678 (R997).
    • In the business of the Brunswicks’ claim to the treatment of ambassadors, the Danes have conceded nothing. No formal act was made in writing at the first step.
    • He comments on the Duke of [?Hannover] and his brothers: “Todo me parece muy bien pesado en la gran prudencia de V.S.; pero yo creo que nada de estas [?intrigas] [?llegará] a fin”.
    • “los incesantes riesgos de Flandes, en que excuso discurrir por no crecerme la melancolía”.
  • 22 March 1678 (R998).
    • The dependencies between the princes of the north, “de suficiente calibre para tenernos embarazados”.
    • The evils “que nosotros estamos padeciendo”.
    • The loss in Flanders: “la pérdida de Gante estamos temiendo la de Ipre[s] [...] otras importantes plazas”. Ghent had fallen to Louis XIV on 9 March.
    • They are still waiting on the others’ long delays while “la enfermedad” advances.

04 The ten letters with a margin decipherment

These were deciphered at the time. The key reproduces their margins, which recover illegible margin words and confirm the doubtful ones. Nine of the ten reach the 95% bar; R986 (28 August) builds coherent sentences but reads at 94.6%, under it. Their subjects:

  • Amerongen’s hopes for a guarantee to the town of Bremen, 3 August.
  • The dissolution of the Bremen conference, 18 June.
  • Villahermosa, Temple’s recall and the Danish setback at Malmö.
  • The Bishop of Strasbourg’s minister and Orange’s journey to England, 19 October.
  • The loss of Saint-Ghislain, December.
  • The Brunswick claim to ambassadorial rank, which runs through the winter letters of 1678.

The margin text is transcribed in targets/balbases1677/tx/ and written out as running Spanish, spelling kept, in read/R985_reading.md and the others; the key decryption carries it group by group. In R991 a margin word left unread was filled from the figures (“de estos discursos”; 23 October 2026).

05 Method

  1. The images were fetched from DECODE.
  2. Two agents transcribed every cipher line and margin line by page into tx/. They corrected DECODE’s transcriptions, which drop lines, confuse 46/96 and 48/98, and garble the margins.
  3. Repeated n-grams across letters were set against the margin text at the same relative position to find cribs: Brunsuic, principes, conformidad, novedad, conveniente.
  4. An alignment program (align2.py; hard expectation-maximisation that keeps the order of the text) was started from those cribs and run over the ten letters with margins. Each group emits a substring of the margin text, with nulls and skips allowed.
  5. Two rounds brought out the table. A frozen key then decoded all fourteen letters.

06 What remains uncertain

  • 3% of groups (243 of 8,150) are unassigned. They are mostly rare 3-digit name codes: 82, 83, 150, 161, 182, 187, 189, 800.
  • Several homophones carry a second value. The cause is transcription slips (4/9, in/ot), not the key: 41 gives di beside ve, 77 in beside ot, 61 ne beside ri.
  • The four letters without a margin are decoded, not read: their partial readings have holes in most sentences, from rare codes with no value (fol, 85, 150, 182, 187, 17, 189, bon, i8 and others, once or twice in all fourteen letters, none in a margined letter) and from stretches whose homophones do not divide into Spanish. The doubtful (struck, overwritten) figures cannot be settled on the 1616-pixel greyscale DECODE scans; the originals are at the AGR in Brussels. None of the four has a check text.
  • R992 is the least certain transcription. Its pages are heavily marked up.

contenido nos sirviesse de luz y de advertencia en lo que por aca se ofreciesse, siendo cierto que cada dia vamos

# R994, Balbases to Fuenmayor, 15 Feb 1678 (cipher): English translation

Everything Your Lordship has been suggesting about the Brunswickers' claim to the rank of ambassadors that they want for
their ministers is very much in keeping with the policy and the manner in which we here considered the way of handling
this matter to be right; but I am not surprised to see now in Your Lordship's letter of the 8th that the Danes have
given way on that point, prostituting themselves to the declaration that Your Lordship suggests they have made in favour
of the Brunswickers; for since they are of so faint-hearted a nature, at any adverse event it will already have seemed
to them that that of [?Nugen], over which we truly have much to regret, brings them very low if they do not bring
themselves to humour the House of Brunswick a great deal. Here we have tried skilfully to learn from those Danish
ambassadors what there was in the matter, but they not only profess to know nothing of such a thing, but rather deny it,
thinking it impossible that their King should have done such an act; and so I would be grateful if, should it be easy
for Your Lordship to obtain a copy of it, you would be pleased to send it to me, so that its contents might serve us as
light and warning in whatever may arise here; for it is certain that every day we find new reasons to suspect that the
inner mind of the Brunswickers has put on less good intention towards our alliance than would be fitting. I thank Your
Lordship for the copy you send me of the letter that the Swedish ambassador in Paris wrote to Count [?] Brahe, senator
in Stockholm, suggesting to him that the Most Christian King's intention is to offset our losses in Flanders with those
of Sweden in Germany; and I do not know whether the indifference that the Danish minister who sent Your Lordship that
paper has shown on this point is artful, for I hear that in Vienna too this kind of talk has been spread by the
minister of Brandenburg, whereas in reality the allies cannot have the least suspicion of our side, since matters have
not come to the point of such discussions, when ill fortune has willed that in everything and for everything we depend,
in these peace talks, on what England may do and Holland support. This is still unknown up to now, and so we cannot
speak with any foundation, it being necessary to go on humouring everyone until we reach clarity about what is to
happen. Here the rumour runs that the London Parliament opened on the 7th, beginning its first speech by pressing for
the break with France, and that the Most Christian King set out that same day for the frontiers; but I have no letters
that oblige me to give sure credit to either of these things, so I refer to what Your Lordship will learn from other
quarters.

# R995, Balbases to Fuenmayor, 26 Jan 1678 (cipher): English translation

I confess to Your Lordship that for some days now the way the Dukes of Brunswick behave in all their negotiations has
obliged me to enter into some suspicions, and that what they did in the matter of the winter quarters, and what they
now press for in having their ministers treated here as ambassadors, make me suspect that they have formed some
concealed cabal of agreement with the French; for were it not so, the arrogance and effrontery with which they press for
things without foundation and without precedent would seem excessive — seeking to get this declaration from the Danes
there in advance of their agreements, and pressing the Duke of Villahermosa in Flanders so that we, the ambassadors of
the King our lord, should make a positive commitment to give them this treatment. Your Lordship acted entirely in
keeping with your prudence in the cautions you gave the Danish ministers on this point, for the positive declaration of
one crown could be of great inconvenience to the others, besides which it would bring pernicious precedents with regard
to so many other princes of the Empire, of greater and lesser rank than those of Brunswick, who would want the same
treatment; the more so as the Dukes of Lorraine and Neuburg, who take precedence over the Brunswickers — one the
brother-in-law and the other the father-in-law of the Emperor himself — have not pressed such a claim, nor could they
bear its being granted to those of Brunswick. And it is true that the decision of matters concerning the princes of the
Empire belongs properly to the deliberation of the Imperial Diet; in the present case it is a point reserved solely to
the ministers attending the congress, where the form of treatment to be observed is settled by agreement among all; so
the Brunswickers ought to apply here on this point. This is in line with what I answered the Lord Duke of Villahermosa:
he could meet the requests they were making to His [?] so as to free himself of them, and not throw himself into [?]
that would be impossible to fulfil, nor into a state of [?] the Princes of Brunswick with greater distrust; for [?] they
can be assured that the ministers of the King our lord will not be the least forward in seeking all the satisfaction
their Highnesses desire. I thought I should inform Your Lordship of this in detail, so that you may know the answer I
have given, in case there should be occasion to speak of the matter there. The reports arriving these days seem to
cloud somewhat the hopes towards peace that we had entered into in past days, for France's obstinacy persists in denying
what was visibly its own glory and advantage; but we still remain waiting on what the British King will finally resolve
to counterbalance these doubts, in which France will leave us not a little to fear if it rushes into wanting war with
England as well.
