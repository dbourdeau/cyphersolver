The Duke of Guastalla on a “lettera reversale”, 24 September 1717

Go to the full text ↓

A partly ciphered Italian letter of the Duke of Guastalla, catalogued as dated July and as unread, was deciphered with a key that Prabal Gupta reports finding from the letter alone: the duke refuses a condition that would give up his rights.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 8 November 2026

Method: key recovered from ciphertext-only · Extent: partial (90% of the cipher)

Contributed by Prabal Gupta (solution)

01 The letter

Four pages in the Autografoteca Campori of the Biblioteca Estense in Modena (Lettera A, “Antonio Ferdinando, duca di Guastalla”, cc. 15–16), in Italian: a clear opening and closing, and a cipher of numbers between them. The signature is “Ant. Ferd. Gonzaga”, Antonio Ferdinando Gonzaga, Duke of Guastalla; the correspondent is not named. The Estense record calls it “Lettera parzialmente cifrata”, mentions no decipherment and dates it 24 July 1717. The dateline of the letter reads “Guastalla 24 7bre 1717”, and in letters of the time “7bre” means September, so the date here is 24 September 1717.

The ledger listed the letter with two other Campori letters (Augustoni, 1612; Olivazzi, 1725) in one entry; they use other ciphers and stay there. The contributor, Prabal Gupta, read the letter from the scans; the project could not reach them (the Estense images are licensed CC BY-NC-SA) and worked from his files.

02 The key and its test

Letters are two-digit numbers in alphabet order from a = 30 to z = 67 (b = 25 is an exception). Some letters have a twin (e is 37, 77 or 38; a is 30, 70 or 54), and the writer’s 3 and 7 in the first digit are hard to tell apart, so the transcription writes 3. A word may end in one single digit for its last vowel: 5 a, 6 e, 7 i, 8 o, 9 u. A few short numbers stand for whole words (2 che, 18 non, 1 e, from context), and about ten other code groups, probably names and titles, stay unread: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 20, 26, 92 and 93.

The contributor reports that he found the key from this letter alone, with no other letter, key or crib, by count matching and a fit on Italian letter counts. His own tests, which the project did not repeat: a key found from the first half alone agrees with the full key on 98.3% of the second half; a blind reader called the text Italian and translated about 85% of the sense.

Checked here, with an own script: decrypting the cipher groups again with key.tsv gives 1,668 letters; a sequence match against the contributor’s decryption (1,600 letters) gives 95.2%. A word-list test, about 130 common Italian words against 300 shuffled versions of the two-digit key, gives 35.2% of the letters in listed words for the key, a mean of 17.7% and a best of 34.5% for the shuffles. That separates the key from the average shuffle but not clearly from the best one, so it is a weak control. The project relies instead on the decryption reading as Italian sentences.

03 The reading

The cipher part is built into 23 clauses (reading.md, with an English translation). It opens, in the contributor’s reading: in sostanza vedo [che] era ritornato da’ suoi beni il […] Trauston [?Trautson] (“in substance I see that Trautson had come back from his estates”); Starhemberg and Trautson were expected the following week. The duke cannot answer until he is told whether the claim of a “lettera reversale” has been dropped: that letter has the same effect as the eleventh article of the declaration, and worse, “una tacita abdicazione delle mie ragioni, alla quale non sarò mai per aderire” (“a tacit abdication of my rights, to which I will never agree”). He will accept the land only in the form of the old investitures usually granted to his forebears, with no harm to himself or his heirs; the income of the territory is too small to justify any loss of rights; whatever he does will be out of regard for the high merit of a person written in code groups 92 and 93, perhaps the Emperor (a guess). The clear closing wishes the correspondent’s son, “il Signor Contino”, a return to health.

Trautson and Starhemberg are the contributor’s reading of names in the cipher; he matches them to ministers of the imperial court in 1717 and has not checked that in print, and neither has the project. He also reports that Ireneo Affò’s history of Guastalla (vol. IV, 1785) tells of talks that led to an imperial draft of January 1718, which fits September; Affò does not quote or decipher this letter.

04 What remains uncertain

  • Ten code groups (10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 20, 26, 92, 93) are unread or doubtful. Counting the cipher part of the reading as 356 units, with a code group in brackets counted as one, 34 carry an unread code group or a question mark, so 322 of 356 (90.4%) read.
  • Doubtful passages marked [?] in the reading: page 1 line 15, page 3 line 4, page 4 line 9 and a few single words, where the shapes of 3 and 7 or other digits are ambiguous and only the sense chose between readings.
  • The scans were not seen by the project, so the transcription (two passes agreeing on 92.0% of 3,064 digits, by the contributor’s count) has not been compared with them (the transcription file itself holds 3,095 digits), and no human has repeated the check.
  • The recipient’s papers, the Gonzaga of Guastalla papers in Parma and Mantua and the imperial archives in Vienna were not searched; a draft or clear copy may survive there, and the letter may be known to historians.
  • No statistical validation is recorded for the key.

11. You have written to me that nothing more is claimed than what is commonly practised in [the investitures?] of the other fiefs.
12. I reply to you that, if [the investiture?] that is meant to be granted to me follows the form [?] of the old investitures usually granted to my forebears, the owners of the territory that is to be ceded to me,
13. and so without the least harm to me and to my posterity, I will have no difficulty in accepting it.
14. But if they wanted to attach new conditions to it, I could put my thoughts on them to you with all the greatest care [?].
15. [Passage not fully read.] It will be to my advantage [?], and I could add nothing more on the matter [?].
16. It suits me to wait from you for the precise answers and decisions on the difficulties raised above.
17. I will not lose a moment in answering them, and I will not fail to put them up again for consideration.
18. The revenue of the territory named to me in the list you sent is so small and poor, compared with the whole [value?] of [?],
19. that it never deserves that I let myself be tempted into the least harm to myself.
20. I declare again that whatever I do, provided that no harm to my rights comes of it,
21. will always be out of regard for the high merit of [code 92 93: the Emperor?],
22. for whom I will always have the most dutiful veneration,
23. in the hope that, in time, I will enjoy the acts of that most generous munificence and [?] justice.

## Clear closing

I hear with much regret of the bad health of the young Count, your son. I understand the distress of mind that you feel. I pray the Lord to give him back his former health, and to you every true good. Guastalla, 24 September [written "7bre"] 1717. Antonio Ferdinando Gonzaga.

## Gist

The duke writes to an agent at the imperial court, or in touch with it. The court has offered the duke a territory. The duke refuses to sign a "reversal letter" that would give up other claims. The duke will accept the territory only on the old terms of investiture. The territory's income is too small to justify any loss of rights.
