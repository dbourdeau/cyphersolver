Barlow to Monroe and to Bassano, autumn 1812: two passages in the Paris legation code

Two short coded passages in the last despatches of the American minister in Paris, on indemnities for seized ships, deciphered in part with a key rebuilt from ten other letters that were deciphered at the time.

Daniel Bourdeau

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · partial · 47 of 60 coded words

Joel Barlow, US minister to France, to James Monroe, Secretary of State, Paris, 25 September 1812, with 64 code numbers in one paragraph; and the copies sent to Monroe of Barlow’s note of 25 October 1812 to the Duke of Bassano, the French foreign minister, with 46 numbers in one copy and 47 in the other. No decipherment of either is on the film.

01 The letters

The first is Barlow’s despatch to the Secretary of State of 25 September 1812, a secretary’s fair copy signed by Barlow. It acknowledges Monroe’s letters and reports on the negotiation with France. Only its middle paragraph is in code. In that paragraph a comma joins the numbers of one word and a dash separates words.

The second is Barlow’s note of 25 October 1812 to the Duke of Bassano, in two copies that were enclosed to Monroe (copy A on frames 0240–0241, copy B on 0246–0247). They stand in the volume beside copies of Bassano’s French letter of 11 October 1812 from Wilna, which the note answers. Two sentences of the note are partly in code in both copies. The contributor takes it, as an inference, that this was done for the copies sent home and that the note Bassano received was in clear.

All 296 frames of the roll were surveyed and neither passage has a decipherment there. A print check of 21 Nov 2026 found neither in print: the despatch of 25 September is not printed in American State Papers, Foreign Relations vol. 3, Wait’s State Papers (1819), Todd’s Life and Letters of Joel Barlow (1886) or The Papers of James Monroe vol. 6, and the note to Bassano is printed only as an extract that stops at “the convention of indemnities”, before the coded sentences. Four later books were covered by phrase searches only, so a paraphrase in them is not excluded.

02 What they say

Words written in code are in italics. [?word] is a word in which one number has no value from a decipherer of the time (or, for [?the], is miswritten) and which is completed from the sense: a proposal. [163] is a number with no value. Letters in curly brackets are supplied.

To Monroe, 25 September 1812. “The great object of [?indemnities] for past {+s}poliations is under discussion and is [?making] [?progress] on a footing more favorable than I ever {+exp}ected. The views in which the principle is presented in my note of the [163]th June have convinced every person in the imperial confidence here; and their arguments aided by the attitude now taken by [?the] United States will probably bring the Emperor to their opinion. His decision is expected in a few days, and my hopes in a result acceptable, if not satisfactory, are now stronger than they have been. The Treaty will suffer no difficulty when once the mod{+e} of [?indemnity] is agreed upon.”

To Bassano, 25 October 1812. “And if the present project (whose execution would really cost [?little] or nothing to the imperial treasury and by which it would immediately gain great sums and secure a permanent and increasing gain) should be rejected, the President can see in it nothing but a [?determined] refusal of justice on the part of his Imperial Majesty, which must greatly embarrass the Government in its operations in [?regard] to England as wel{+l} as France.”

The indemnities are the American claims for ships and cargoes seized by France. In the note Barlow accepts Bassano’s invitation to Wilna to finish the negotiation, says that the treaty of commerce and the convention of indemnities are ready, and puts the sharpest words in code. Irving Brant (1958) describes from the French archives the proposal behind the first coded clause: reparation was to be raised by French licences to eighty American merchant vessels, which is why its execution “would really cost little or nothing” to the treasury. Barlow died on the road back from Wilna on 26 December 1812 and nothing was signed (so the contributor’s reading notes; not checked here).

The reading of the note follows copy A for the clear text. The coded words follow copy B in three places where copy A is short: copy A has no “of” before “justice”, spells “justice” with a number that has no value, and writes “Imperial” before “Majesty” without its third syllable. “Treasury” follows copy A, which alone has its third syllable.

03 The code and the key

The code writes words and syllables as numbers from 7 to 1700 among clear words. Common words have their own number (the 1700, of 631, and 513, to 494, in 1114). Other words are spelt from syllables: in/dem/ni/ti/es, re/fu/sal, ma/i/est/y. I and J are one letter, and so are U and V. Several syllables have more than one number. Barlow used it, Monroe and Madison used it in writing to him, and his nephew Thomas Barlow used it after his death.

No code book or key sheet of it is known. The key here, key.tsv, holds 644 numbers: 623 with a value, and 21 seen only inside a word that could not be divided. Every value is one that a decipherer of 1811–1813 wrote, or that follows from a word he wrote. Each carries a grade that says how it is known:

Ldoubtful occurrences only (poor film, a blot, a number the second readers did not confirm)14

The ten letters behind the key:

  • Barlow to Monroe, Paris, 29 September 1811 (roll 16 frame 11), with a separate decipherment by the State Department on frames 15–16.
  • Barlow’s despatches 19 and 20 of 20 and 25 October 1812 (frames 242 and 244), his letter from Wilna of 23 November 1812 (frames 249–250), Thomas Barlow’s letter of 10 February 1813 (frames 291–293) and a letter of his of 1813 marked “Duplicate” (frame 295), each with a clerk’s decipherment between the lines.
  • Monroe to Barlow, 23 April 1812 (the last page, from a sale photograph) and 16 June 1812 (Library of Congress, Monroe Papers); Madison to Barlow, 11 August 1812, and Barlow to Madison, 26 September 1812 (Library of Congress, Madison Papers), each deciphered between the lines at the time, the last by Madison.

It is not the code that John Quincy Adams used at St Petersburg, in which the is 1385 and of is 1576, nor Armstrong’s code of 1804–1810, in which the is 972.

04 How it was deciphered

  1. The despatch volume (19 Oct 2026). All 296 frames of the roll were surveyed. Barlow’s despatch of 29 September 1811 (503 numbers, nothing written over them) has a separate decipherment four frames on, and the two were aligned word for word. Five later despatches have a clerk’s decipherment between the lines. Together they gave 436 numbers, which gave a value to 78% of the numbers of the three passages without a decipherment.
  2. Four letters outside the volume (20 Oct 2026). The letters of Monroe, Madison and Barlow listed above, each deciphered between the lines, brought the table to 655 numbers.
  3. A second reading of every source (21 Nov 2026). Each source was transcribed again by two AI readers who were given only the images. The pass marks were written down first: each reader to agree with the first transcription on at least 95% of the numbers, and at least 98% to be matched by one reader or the other. Of 3,297 numbers compared, 56 had been misread and were corrected, and 22 doubtful occurrences were set aside. Nine of the fourteen texts compared (the key letters and the three passages here) missed a mark. The table after correction has 644 numbers (key.tsv): the 623 with a value and the 21 seen only inside a word. The nine numbers of the two passages that have no value occur in none of the key letters.
  4. Transcription and decryption. The two letters were transcribed in full. The code numbers were transcribed four times by AI readers; all four agree on 56 of the 64 numbers of the September letter, and every difference is listed in the notes with what the frame shows. The decryption files give each number with the value of the key and nothing repaired: 59 of 64 numbers have a value in the September letter, 42 of 46 in copy A of the note and 44 of 47 in copy B.
  5. The readings. Each letter was written out whole as sentences. Counted over the coded words only, a word counting when the key alone gives all of it, 25 of 34 words are given in the September letter (0.735) and 22 of 26 in the note (0.846): 47 of 60 together (0.783), under the bar of 95%, so the extent is partial.
  6. Reviews. An AI agent of the contributor that had made none of the readings read all 157 code numbers from the frames before opening the folder and agreed on 153. For the maintainers, an AI agent read both readings as sentences against the unedited decryptions on 24 Nov 2026 and graded both B: coherent English, with holes in key words. No person has read the numbers or the readings against the images.

05 How far the key is checked

The key is not validated by a held-out test. All ten deciphered letters went into the table, so there is no letter in this code, deciphered at the time, on which the finished key was tried afterwards.

The project’s statistical validation (24 Nov 2026) gave the verdict underdetermined. It took the 110 numbers of the September letter and of copy A of the note, of which 101 have a value in the key (68 different numbers, 251 letters). On a letter model of English the reading scores −2.064 per letter, and random keys of the same structure score −2.859 on average (best −2.603). But a key fitted freely to the shuffled text scores −1.901 (best −1.879), better than the reading, and so does the same fitting run on the real text (−1.827). Underdetermined means here that the reading is far better than a random key would give, yet on a text this short, in which most numbers occur once or twice, a freely fitted key does better still, so this test can neither confirm nor contradict the key.

What supports the key is where its values come from, the decipherers of the time, and the second reading of every source. That second reading checks that the numbers were transcribed as the images show them, though nine of the fourteen texts still missed a pass mark (section 04). It does not show that a value is right. 290 of the 623 values rest on a single occurrence, on one decipherer’s word. In the two readings these words lean on such a value: confidence (831), imperial (1014), justice in copy B (1017) and spoliations (1646); the full stop after “opinion” (177) is known from the alignment of 1811 only.

The surviving State Department sheet (24 Nov 2026). 584 of the 623 values are entries of the State Department code sheet that survives at the National Archives (M97 roll 13, the code of Anthony Morris’s despatches), and short runs stand in the same order: 133 ti, 137 tin, 138 tion here are 560, 564, 565 there. No number has the same value in the two codes, nor in the other American tables held in the repository. So this code was numbered over a word list of the same family, and the place of a number in that list can be compared. A test of the nine open numbers by their place was planned before it was run, and it did not reach its marks: with each of the 571 valued numbers that match one entry of the sheet left out in turn, only 47 could be predicted by the rule, where the plan asked for 100, and 43 of them were right, where it asked for 95%. No value is taken from it. What the order suggests for each open number is given under the next heading, as a remark only.

06 What remains open

Thirteen of the 60 coded words are counted as holes: seven completed from the sense because a number has no value, [?the], the unread day in June, and four words with a letter supplied. The eight proposals are inferred from the sense (grade I in the project’s scale) and none is counted.

Each “by its place” sentence below is a remark only. The test by position failed its control (section 05) and made no prediction for eight of the nine numbers; for the ninth, 531, its one interpolated value is not the word the sense needs. Where the neighbours below and above point to different entries of the sheet, both are given.

  • indemnities, indemnity (1114 703 815 1156 and 1114 703 815): in, dem and es are in the key; 815 is not. The proposal is nity. Monroe’s letter of 16 June 1812 spells the word another way, in/dem/ni/ty/es. By its place 815 may fall on nity, counting from 812 (ning) below: the sheet runs ning, ninth, nish, nity, nk, and 814 and 816 are found in “Spanish” and “think”. Counting from the neighbour above, 818 (no), it would fall on fix.
  • making progress (1511 1567 1115 and 574 1404 761): 1567, written above the line, and 1404, whose third digit is blotted, have no value. The proposals are k and gres. By its place 1404 would be gre counting from 1405 (great) above, which gives the same word, and ged counting from 1403 (ge) below.
  • the [163]th June: 163 has no value and none is proposed. In his private letter to Madison of the next day Barlow refers twice to a note of June with the same pair 939 1126, which Madison deciphered once as “11th June” and once as “fifth June”. The Madison edition identifies the note of the fifth as his letter of 5 June 1812 to the Duke of Dalberg, and says that no note of 11 June has been found. By the order of the word list (a remark only) 939 falls among the words in fi-, so the despatch may mean the note of 5 June. Nothing gives 163 a value.
  • [?the] United States: the number is written like 1780, and the is 1700. It is a whole word, so no neighbour can confirm the digit.
  • little (624) in “cost little or nothing”: no value. By its place 624 stands between like and live, where the sheet has ling and little.
  • determined (706 1059 1427 950): er, min and ed are in the key; 706 is not. The proposal is det. By its place 706 comes directly after den, where the sheet has dent, dep and other entries before det, so the proposal is in doubt on that side. No other word was found for it, and both copies have 706.
  • regard (1114 841, then 531 in copy A and 537 in copy B): neither number has a value. One of the two copies has miscopied the number, and the contributor’s notes leave open which. By its place 537 may fall on ard counting from 538 (are) above, or on arch counting from 535 (ar) below. For 531 the rule gives one value, aph, which is not ard. The test failed its control, so this does not settle which copy is wrong.
  • justice is counted as deciphered, from copy B (i/u/st/ice). In copy A it is written with 103, which has no value and stands where the word needs ti. By the sheet’s order 103 would be dist (from 102 below) or dom (from 105 above), not ti; 133 is ti in the key, so 103 may be a slip for 133.
  • {+s}poliations, {+exp}ected, mod{+e}: one number in each is blotted or miswritten on the film (the group after 745; 1353 written like 1553; 750) and is taken from the word. The key was rebuilt and is not a surviving key of the time, so the project’s rule for a one-digit slip does not apply, and each word is a hole.
  • wel{+l}: the key gives “wel” for 896 and the second l is supplied.

The nine numbers without a value (815, 1567, 1404, 163, 624, 706, 103, 531 and 537) occur nowhere in the ten key letters. On 24 Nov 2026 the fifteen Madison and Barlow letters of 1811–1812 on Founders Online were searched for the editors’ notes of code: two have it, both already used. Tomokiyo’s Cryptiana article on the State Department code names this code (THE=1700, key not found) and gives no table. Material that could add occurrences or test the readings exists but is not online: Eustis to Barlow of 25 September 1812 (Clements Library, catalogue 770); the note as Bassano received it, presumably in clear, which would test the reading of the second letter (archives of the French foreign ministry, Correspondance politique, États-Unis, vol. 69, cited by Brant); and the Princeton copy of Monroe to Barlow of 16 June 1812 and the French decipherment of Barlow’s letter of 26 September 1812 (vol. 68). Those two are copies of letters already in the key, which contain none of the nine numbers, so they could only check its values.

Not checked: no person has compared a number or a reading with the images; the clear text was transcribed once; Weber’s printed code tables, other than the sheet and rows held in the repository, were not tested against this key; the four later books of the print check were not read page by page.
