A codebreaker’s file: the Italian intercepts in TNA SP 106/10

The National Archives, State Papers 106, box 10 · DECODE R657, R660, R664, R704, R720, R721, R722, with the key record R725 · catalogued as seven separate anonymous ciphertexts, “1558/1625”, sender and recipient unknown

They are one man’s working file · the key sheet he wrote out survives two records away from them · its vocabulary dates the whole group to the Spanish Match and the Palatinate, 1623–24

Summary. DECODE holds seven records for TNA SP 106/10 as separate ciphertexts by an unknown sender to an unknown recipient, dated only by the volume’s span, 1558 to 1625. They are not seven problems. They are the working file of one English codebreaker: intercepted Italian letters with his own solutions written between the lines, his scratch worksheets, and — catalogued three records away, as a key rather than a ciphertext, and so outside the target list — the fair copy of the cipher he reconstructed. That sheet, DECODE R725, is transcribed here in full. It explains the system in the codebreaker’s own words: two-figure groups that are read as a single letter, or as a word from one list, or as a word from a second list, according to whether the figure carries no mark, a bar or a dot. Its recovered nomenclature — Palatinato, Il Principe, Re d’Inghilterra, Papa, Nuncio, C. Barberino, Imbassiatore di Venetia and above all Marques Inichiosa — dates the file to 1623–24, sixty years narrower than the catalogue. Working through all twenty-two ciphertext pages then shows that the file contains three different ciphers, not one, and that DECODE has one worksheet filed with the wrong letter. Of the second cipher, a three-figure syllabic code, about forty-five values were rebuilt by aligning the codebreaker’s interlinear syllables against his groups, enough to read phrases straight off the page. Status: the case is explained and dated, the key of the first cipher transcribed, the second read in fragments. No sender or recipient is yet named, and neither of the two long letters is yet read through.

01What the catalogue says, and what is actually there

The seven records are all headed TNA_SP106/10_UND_(…) — UND for undated — and all carry the same span, 1558 to 1625, which is simply the date range of the volume. The cataloguer had, however, already seen what the file was, and said so in a note on the first record:

“This is clearly the worksheet of a codebreaker. This can be derived, not only from the fact that the lines of code are only partly decrypted, but also from the various code-sequences scribbled in the margin with identical codegroups in the middle. The handwriting seems to indicate that this document is probably late Tudor or early Stuart in origin, but except for Thomas Phelippes who worked for Walsingham, no other codebreakers from this period are known.”

R725 is catalogued as a key, not a ciphertext, which is why it falls outside the seven and why the seven look unsolvable on their own. It sits eleven folios after the last of them in the same volume, in the same hand, and it is the answer to them.

02The key sheet, and the cipher in the codebreaker’s own words

The third page of R725 is a fair copy in four headed columns. The first is “Alphabet of Single Letters”:

A 10, 11 · B 31 · C 41 · D 25 · E 20, 90 · F 21 · G 51 · H 71 · I 30 · L 81 · M 91 · O 40
P 42 · Qu 72 · R 22 · S 94 · T 92 · V 50 · Z 93 · (X blank)

The rougher draft on p. 1 of the same record gives D as 29, V as 90, and adds the line “X. Z. & serve to bee Nulles”; p. 3 is the corrected state.

The second column, “Alphabet of letters that are woordes”, is a second device of the same cipher: a written letter, not a figure, standing for a whole word.

a = per · c = di · d = da · f = dal · g = il · h = in · n = ma · p = se
q = et · r = con · t = non · v = chi · x = si · z = che

The third column is headed Literarum resolutio, and under it the codebreaker writes the sentence that is the whole solution of the system:

“It seemes there are numbers of woordes expressed by this difference of — over the figures whereof in this Cifar.”

That is: a bar over a two-figure group makes it a word rather than a letter. The values he had recovered for the barred list are 11 tanto, 16 lettere, 18 relatione, 20 spedditione, 26 V. Signoria, 27 V. Paternità, 29 Sua Signoria, 30 uno, 37 Re d’Inghilterra (the draft writes Re di Britania), 42 Padre, 52 liga.

The fourth column is headed “An Alphabet of Characters thus marked 10′ 11′ etc. In these letters encountred” — a third reading of the same two figures, marked this time with a tick:

22 Il Principe · 23 Marques Inichiosa · 24 Imbassiatore di Venetia · 28 Imbassiatore · 32 Roma · 34 Palatinato
49 Cattholico · 50 Stato · 57 Negocio or Occasione · 66 acciocche · 67 C. Barberino · 69 Governo
71 havere · 73 habuto · 77 mento · 80 perche · 82 in cio · 84 questo or questa · 85 quello or quella · 89 tutte · 90 Papa · 91 Nuncio

So one two-figure group has three possible meanings, selected by a diacritic: bare, it is a letter; barred, a word of the first list; ticked, a word of the second. It is a compact and genuinely awkward system, and the codebreaker worked it out from the intercepts alone.

03The vocabulary dates the file to 1623–24

DECODE dates these records “1558–1625”, which is no date at all. The words the codebreaker recovered date them closely, because they are the words of one year:

  • Marques Inichiosa — the Marqués de la Inojosa, Spain’s ambassador extraordinary in England from the spring of 1623 until he was recalled in the summer of 1624.
  • C. Barberino — Francesco Barberini was made cardinal in October 1623, the nephew of the Pope elected that August.
  • Il Principe and Re d’Inghilterra — Prince Charles and James I, with the Prince distinguished from his father, as he was in every despatch of the Spanish Match.
  • Palatinato, liga, Papa, Nuncio, Imbassiatore di Venetia, Stato, Governo, Cattholico.

That is the London of 1623–24: the Prince back from Madrid, the Spanish embassy under suspicion, the Palatinate unrecovered, the papal dispensation and the Venetian ambassador at the centre of the traffic. The intercepts are Italian letters passing through or out of that embassy world, and the file is the English government’s attack on them. That also puts the codebreaker outside the frame the DECODE note suggested: Phelippes died in 1625 and had long been out of favour; this is his successors’ work.

04The file holds three ciphers, not one

Going through all twenty-two ciphertext pages shows three distinct systems, which the catalogue does not separate:

B. three-figure syllabic codeR660, R664; worksheets R720533 359 515 …, inline with clear Italian

One correction to the catalogue follows. DECODE files R720 as “worksheets of a codebreaker, possibly related to the intercepted letter in R704”. R704 is written in system C, with letter-and-figure groups; R720’s worksheet is covered in three-figure groups and trial Italian word-lists. R720 belongs with R660 and R664, not with R704.

05System B read in part

R660 and R664 are letters written half in clear and half in code — the writer enciphers what matters and leaves the Italian connective tissue open, which is exactly what let the English reader in, and what lets a reader in now. One whole line of R664 stands in the clear, and it is about the cipher itself:

“potrete continuare nel modo cominciato, osservando però il [mutamento … alle volte … col … numeri suppositi]”

The codebreaker wrote his solutions above the groups, syllable by syllable. Aligning those syllables against the figures gives the code back. About forty-five values were fixed this way, each anchored on a word that admits no other division:

06Method, and the check that the alignment is sound

The whole of this rests on reading the codebreaker’s interlinear syllables against the right groups, and a one-group drift would produce plausible nonsense. The alignment produced one apparent contradiction and then resolved it, which is the reason to trust the rest: group 252 read sen in one line and se in another, and 359 read za in one line and n in two others. Both resolve if the word senza runs over the end of line 41 — 252 se + 359 n closing the line, 560 za opening line 42 — which is what the manuscript shows. After that correction no value in the table above conflicts with any other across lines 34–45.

The images came from DECODE with the project’s login; all of them are written across the page and had to be rotated before anything could be read. No solver was used and none was needed: this is a case where the archive did the cryptanalysis four hundred years ago and the work is to find the sheet on which it is written down.

07What is uncertain, and what remains

  • The letter values of system A are C (from the codebreaker’s own fair copy, and internally consistent); the draft’s D 29 / V 90 against the fair copy’s D 25 / V 50 is the one place he corrected himself, and the fair copy is followed here.
  • The system B values listed above are C where a whole word fixes them (della loro sicurezza, senza, quelle de nuovo, che, et, volte, lettere, vostre) and M for the single-syllable values seen only once (245, 316, 344, 356, 357, 459, 544).
  • Groups seen but not valued on R664 f. 1: 233, 312, 367, 439, 477, 534, 551, 556, 560 in its second sense, 566. Several dozen more stand on ff. 2–3.
  • The dating is C: it rests on Marques Inichiosa together with C. Barberino, and no single one of the names would carry it alone.
  • Not done: the gloss-alignment pass over R664 ff. 2–3, R660 and the R720 worksheets, which should carry system B to a full reading of both letters; the same treatment of system C on R704, R721 and R722; R657’s nine pages against the R725 key; and the identification of the correspondents and of the codebreaker.
