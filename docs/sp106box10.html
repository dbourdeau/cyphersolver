A codebreaker’s file: the Italian intercepts in TNA SP 106/10

The National Archives, State Papers 106, box 10 · DECODE R657, R660, R664, R704, R720, R721, R722, with the key record R725 · catalogued as seven separate anonymous ciphertexts, “1558/1625”, sender and recipient unknown

They are one man’s working file · the key sheet he wrote out survives two records away from them · its vocabulary dates the whole group to the Spanish Match and the Palatinate, 1623–24

Summary. DECODE holds seven records for TNA SP 106/10 as separate ciphertexts by an unknown sender to an unknown recipient, dated only by the volume’s span, 1558 to 1625. They are not seven problems. They are the working file of one English codebreaker: intercepted Italian letters with his own solutions written between the lines, his scratch worksheets, and — catalogued three records away, as a key rather than a ciphertext, and so outside the target list — the fair copy of the cipher he reconstructed. That sheet, DECODE R725, is transcribed here in full. It explains the system in the codebreaker’s own words: two-figure groups that are read as a single letter, or as a word from one list, or as a word from a second list, according to whether the figure carries no mark, a bar or a dot. Its recovered nomenclature — Palatinato, Il Principe, Re d’Inghilterra, Papa, Nuncio, C. Barberino, Imbassiatore di Venetia and above all Marques Inichiosa — dates the file to 1623–24, sixty years narrower than the catalogue. Working through all twenty-two ciphertext pages then shows that the file contains three different ciphers, not one, and that DECODE has one worksheet filed with the wrong letter. Of the second cipher, a three-figure syllabic code, about forty-five values were rebuilt by aligning the codebreaker’s interlinear syllables against his groups, enough to read phrases straight off the page. A further catalogue entry (62) held seven more records of the same box; they are his worksheets, and one of them is his own grid of the three-figure code of two of the letters, the code another sheet calls “the Venetian Cifer”. It rebuilds that code as a systematic syllabary of 117 values and corrects four of the values first rebuilt here. Status: the case is explained and dated, the key of the first cipher transcribed, the second read in fragments. No sender or recipient is yet named, and neither of the two long letters is yet read through.

01What the catalogue says, and what is actually there

The seven records are all headed TNA_SP106/10_UND_(…) — UND for undated — and all carry the same span, 1558 to 1625, which is simply the date range of the volume. The cataloguer had, however, already seen what the file was, and said so in a note on the first record:

“This is clearly the worksheet of a codebreaker. This can be derived, not only from the fact that the lines of code are only partly decrypted, but also from the various code-sequences scribbled in the margin with identical codegroups in the middle. The handwriting seems to indicate that this document is probably late Tudor or early Stuart in origin, but except for Thomas Phelippes who worked for Walsingham, no other codebreakers from this period are known.”

R725 is catalogued as a key, not a ciphertext, which is why it falls outside the seven and why the seven look unsolvable on their own. It sits eleven folios after the last of them in the same volume, in the same hand, and it is the answer to them.

02The key sheet, and the cipher in the codebreaker’s own words

The third page of R725 is a fair copy in four headed columns. The first is “Alphabet of Single Letters”:

A 10, 11 · B 31 · C 41 · D 25 · E 20, 90 · F 21 · G 51 · H 71 · I 30 · L 81 · M 91 · O 40
P 42 · Qu 72 · R 22 · S 94 · T 92 · V 50 · Z 93 · (X blank)

The rougher draft on p. 1 of the same record gives D as 29, V as 90, and adds the line “X. Z. & serve to bee Nulles”; p. 3 is the corrected state.

The second column, “Alphabet of letters that are woordes”, is a second device of the same cipher: a written letter, not a figure, standing for a whole word.

a = per · c = di · d = da · f = dal · g = il · h = in · n = ma · p = se
q = et · r = con · t = non · v = chi · x = si · z = che

The third column is headed Literarum resolutio, and under it the codebreaker writes the sentence that is the whole solution of the system:

“It seemes there are numbers of woordes expressed by this difference of — over the figures whereof in this Cifar.”

That is: a bar over a two-figure group makes it a word rather than a letter. The values he had recovered for the barred list are 11 tanto, 16 lettere, 18 relatione, 20 spedditione, 26 V. Signoria, 27 V. Paternità, 29 Sua Signoria, 30 uno, 37 Re d’Inghilterra (the draft writes Re di Britania), 42 Padre, 52 liga.

The fourth column is headed “An Alphabet of Characters thus marked 10′ 11′ etc. In these letters encountred” — a third reading of the same two figures, marked this time with a tick:

22 Il Principe · 23 Marques Inichiosa · 24 Imbassiatore di Venetia · 28 Imbassiatore · 32 Roma · 34 Palatinato
49 Cattholico · 50 Stato · 57 Negocio or Occasione · 66 acciocche · 67 C. Barberino · 69 Governo
71 havere · 73 habuto · 77 mento · 80 perche · 82 in cio · 84 questo or questa · 85 quello or quella · 89 tutte · 90 Papa · 91 Nuncio

So one two-figure group has three possible meanings, selected by a diacritic: bare, it is a letter; barred, a word of the first list; ticked, a word of the second. It is a compact and genuinely awkward system, and the codebreaker worked it out from the intercepts alone.

03The vocabulary dates the file to 1623–24

DECODE dates these records “1558–1625”, which is no date at all. The words the codebreaker recovered date them closely, because they are the words of one year:

  • Marques Inichiosa — the Marqués de la Inojosa, Spain’s ambassador extraordinary in England from the spring of 1623 until he was recalled in the summer of 1624.
  • C. Barberino — Francesco Barberini was made cardinal in October 1623, the nephew of the Pope elected that August.
  • Il Principe and Re d’Inghilterra — Prince Charles and James I, with the Prince distinguished from his father, as he was in every despatch of the Spanish Match.
  • Palatinato, liga, Papa, Nuncio, Imbassiatore di Venetia, Stato, Governo, Cattholico.

That is the London of 1623–24: the Prince back from Madrid, the Spanish embassy under suspicion, the Palatinate unrecovered, the papal dispensation and the Venetian ambassador at the centre of the traffic. The intercepts are Italian letters passing through or out of that embassy world, and the file is the English government’s attack on them. That also puts the codebreaker outside the frame the DECODE note suggested: Phelippes died in 1625 and had long been out of favour; this is his successors’ work.

04The file holds three ciphers, not one

Going through all twenty-two ciphertext pages shows three distinct systems, which the catalogue does not separate:

One correction to the catalogue follows. DECODE files R720 as “worksheets of a codebreaker, possibly related to the intercepted letter in R704”. R704 is written in system C, with letter-and-figure groups; R720’s worksheet is covered in three-figure groups and trial Italian word-lists. R720 belongs with R660 and R664, not with R704.

05System B read in part

R660 and R664 are letters written half in clear and half in code — the writer enciphers what matters and leaves the Italian connective tissue open, which is exactly what let the English reader in, and what lets a reader in now. One whole line of R664 stands in the clear, and it is about the cipher itself:

“potrete continuare nel modo cominciato, osservando però il [mutamento … alle volte … col … numeri suppositi]”

The codebreaker wrote his solutions above the groups, syllable by syllable. Aligning those syllables against the figures gives the code back. About forty-five values were fixed this way, each anchored on a word that admits no other division:

06Method, and the check that the alignment is sound

The whole of this rests on reading the codebreaker’s interlinear syllables against the right groups, and a one-group drift would produce plausible nonsense. The alignment produced one apparent contradiction and then resolved it, which is the reason to trust the rest: group 252 read sen in one line and se in another, and 359 read za in one line and n in two others. Both resolve if the word senza runs over the end of line 41 — 252 se + 359 n closing the line, 560 za opening line 42 — which is what the manuscript shows. After that correction no value in the table above conflicts with any other across lines 34–45.

The images came from DECODE with the project’s login; all of them are written across the page and had to be rotated before anything could be read. No solver was used and none was needed: this is a case where the archive did the cryptanalysis four hundred years ago and the work is to find the sheet on which it is written down.

07A fourth letter: the “Venetian cifar” dossier (R703 and six siblings)

Catalogue entry 66, DECODE R703 (ff. 184–185), turned out to belong to this file, and it is not a letter. Its second page is a positional index headed linea: every group from 200 to 399 with the lines where it stands (280. 2.6.9.20) and notes such as “201 is a finall sillable after an entire word”. Its first page, half upside down, carries glossed fragments (l.2 280 367 464 432, l.7 245 510 Almeno, l.14 297 506 395 e che quello, l.46 540 464) and grids of trial syllables.

The fragments come from a three-figure letter of 46 numbered lines that the first two sessions took to be missing from the DECODE images; it is R722 (section 08b). It is not R664 or R660: in R660, 540 is Spagnoli, and the index does not list the lines where R664 uses 211. The same lost letter is worked on six more records that DECODE lists separately: R661, R667 (tally grids of every group 200–599), R684, R701, R702 (with an Italian–English word list copied from a dictionary) and R720. R701 names the system in the codebreaker’s hand: “Wordes marked wth letters in the Venetian Cifar” (369º nostro, 467º questo, 420i altri, 233r esser). System B is therefore the Venetian cipher, and a superscript letter on a group marks it as a whole word.

His partial key for the letter is on R701 p. 3. It is a syllabary in which the last digit steps through the vowels:

209 va · 210 ve · 211 vi · 212 vo · 213 vu · 231–235 sa se si so su · 244 ra · 245 re
333–336 cha che chi cho · 366–370 la le li lo lu · 375–378 da de di do

This key does not carry the fragments to a reading. It values four of the 28 groups quoted on R703, and the codebreaker’s own glosses contradict it (R702 glosses 244 as ta). He had not broken this letter when the file stops. R703 is explained, not read. The intercept itself was found on 21 September 2026: it is R722 (below).

08The rest of the file: the codebreaker’s own table of “the Venetian Cifer”

DECODE lists seven further records from the same box as another set of anonymous ciphertexts (R658, R661, R663, R667, R684, R701, R702; catalogue item 62). None is an intercept. All are in the same hand as the rest of the file: count grids, syllable drills, Italian–English dictionary trawls, and one English political draft in clear (on cutting off “that supply of treasure that the K. of Sp. hath for the Indies”) reused as scrap paper. Two of them matter. R701 heads a list of word groups “Wordes marked with letters in the Venetian Cifer”: 369° nostro, 467° questo, 430a passata. The same words stand in the side column of R658, and R658 is the codebreaker’s table of the three-figure code of R660 and R664: every group from 136 to 565, with a count of its occurrences below it and his value above it.

The grid shows how the code was made. Each consonant takes five consecutive rows in the order a e i o u, and each hundreds column a different consonant: 151–155 ra…ru, 251–255 sa…su, 351–355 sca…scu, 551–555 sta…stu; 260–264 ta…tu, 360–364 tra…tru, 460–463 va…vo, 560–564 za…zu; 433–437 la…lu, 142–146 na…nu, 242–246 pa…pu, 442–446 pra…pru, 542–545 qua…quo; the row x56 holds the bare vowels, x57–x59 single consonants (357 l, 359 n, 459 r), and a handful of groups are whole words (339 liga, 340 lettere, 439 per, 165 che, 465 quel), their ending given by a superscript letter. With the table, the code is rebuilt at 117 values, 102 of them written by the codebreaker and 15 predicted by the pattern.

The check is R702, where he wrote out group strings with his own glosses. Every one of its 46 groups is in the table, and every string decodes to his gloss:

356 318 436 143 435 → i·co·lo·ne·li “i colonelli” · 341 433 436 154 → del·la·lo·ro
252 359 518 253 → se·n·do·si “sendosi” · 167 252 459 253 → con·se·r·si “conservarsi”
165 245 556 253 156 526 445 534 261 459 → che·po·s·si·a·mo·pro·me·te·r “che possiamo prometter”

The same sheet glosses di 24 di maggio, del campo, per nome suo, sono capitate and efficacia: the letter he was working on was written in late May and spoke of a camp and of colonels. The table also corrects four values of section 05, which came from aligning glosses on R664 alone: 260 is ta, 262 ti, 263 to and 459 r (they had been read as mento, to, ti and v), and 316 ta does not fit the ca…cu run it stands in.

This is a different table from the one the codebreaker was building for the lost 46-line letter of section 07 (R701 p. 3: 244 ra, 245 re, 366–370 la…lu; on R658 244 is pi, 245 po, 433–437 la…lu), though it is built the same way. The words R701 lists under “the Venetian Cifer” are the words of R658’s side column, so on the sheets’ own evidence the name belongs to the code of R664 and R660; the lost letter is in a second table of the same kind, perhaps a later key of the same correspondence. R701 also carries a five-line text in a letter cipher (Esg frp rohxog x rygo fa ombhpeip …), which a substitution solver run against five languages did not break.

08bThe “lost” letter was imaged all along: R722, a Venetian secretary’s letter of 1625

Catalogue entry 58 grouped four more anonymous SP 106/10 records (R656, R662, R722, R931). R722 had been filed with the letter-plus-figure records of system C, following DECODE’s pointers, and nobody had looked at it. It is in fact the 46-line letter of section 07: its first line ends 540 212 una di questi matine, the fragment R703 quotes as l.1 540 212 una che, and five more worksheet fragments stand in it verbatim (280 367 464 432, 340 367 misciandosi, 245 510 Almeno, 297 506 395 … e che quello, 313 541 377 238 254). It has 48 numbered lines, half in clear Italian and half in three-figure groups, with the codebreaker’s trial syllables between the lines. The endorsement reads “Cypher of a Venetian Secretary to the Embassador of that nation in …”, with “1625” on the fold.

The clear passages give its matter: Parmi che …, Io mi credo che questi siano …, Ho visto chiaramente che V. E. …, proposito che ella procurasse, di satisfattione con honor di …, il Spinola sotto Breda, di Bruselles si è inteso …, di Roma. Spinola besieged Breda from August 1624 until 5 June 1625, so the letter is of the first half of 1625, from a Venetian secretary to the Venetian ambassador in England. R722 is read in part: the clear text and the endorsement, not the code, which neither the codebreaker nor this project has broken yet. With the whole intercept in hand, every fragment on the worksheets can now be checked against its line.

The other three records: R656 is the back of an English Exchequer draft (a sheriff’s answer naming Nosse, Rowley, Sherborne and Franke) used by the codebreaker for more line fragments of R722, e.g. l.12 313 541 377 238 254. R662 is a torn strip of his English memorandum on how the code is built (“series of sillables beginning …”, “the vowels a.e.i. for every consonant”, “the most frequent … di”). R931 does not belong to the case: it is an English royalist letter of September 1645 in a numerical code of about 260 values (“(1) septembre 1645”, “earnestly”, “reasons” in clear). None of George Lasry’s 1645–46 SP 106 keys, the Goring key of the next record, or the Charles I–Culpeper key of 1645 fits it, and it stays open.

09What is uncertain, and what remains

  • The letter values of system A are C (from the codebreaker’s own fair copy, and internally consistent); the draft’s D 29 / V 90 against the fair copy’s D 25 / V 50 is the one place he corrected himself, and the fair copy is followed here.
  • The system B values listed above are C where a whole word fixes them (della loro sicurezza, senza, quelle de nuovo, che, et, volte, lettere, vostre) and M for the single-syllable values seen only once (245, 316, 344, 356, 357, 459, 544).
  • Groups seen but not valued on R664 f. 1: 233, 312, 367, 439, 477, 534, 551, 556, 560 in its second sense, 566. Several dozen more stand on ff. 2–3.
  • The dating is C: it rests on Marques Inichiosa together with C. Barberino, and no single one of the names would carry it alone.
  • The Venetian-code table (section 08) is H for the 102 values the codebreaker wrote and M for the 15 predicted from the pattern; 440 Spagn… and the value of 556 (s in R702, unclear on the grid) are M. Which of the two three-figure tables R701’s heading names is C.
  • Open from catalogue 62: the short letter cipher on R701 and the English draft on R661 (not identified).
  • Not done: the gloss-alignment pass over R664 ff. 2–3, R660 and the R720 worksheets, which should carry system B to a full reading of both letters; the same treatment of system C on R704 and R721; the second three-figure code of R722, now that the intercept is found; R931 (1645), for want of a key; R657’s nine pages against the R725 key; and the identification of the correspondents and of the codebreaker.
