Lord Cobham to Walsingham, 1588

→

British Library, Harley MS 287 ff. 80–81, 88, 92–93 and 96–97 (DECODE R8490, R8492, R8495, R8496). Four letters from the English commissioner at the Bourbourg peace talks. The catalogue entry pointed to the wrong key record. The right key sits in Cobham’s own earlier letters in the same volume, where the cipher was deciphered between the lines at the time. Most of the alphabet was rebuilt from those glosses, but the four letters were read only in fragments.

Summary. DECODE catalogues four letters of Lord Cobham to Walsingham from the spring of 1588 as undeciphered. They are clear English with passages in Greek-like signs. The key record the catalogue suggested is Bodley’s 1590 cipher and does not fit. The right key comes from Cobham’s own April letters in the same volume, which were deciphered between the lines at the time. About sixty glossed words, aligned at full scan resolution, give a sign inventory (signs.tsv) and the code numbers: 7 is the Queen, 10 the Lord Admiral, 15 Parma and 16 the King of Spain. The system turns out to be polyphonic, with one sign for s, t and a, and another for r and y. So a small solver (solve.py) gives each sign its set of letters and matches the cipher words against a word list drawn from the Calendar of State Papers Foreign for 1588. Rereading the runs line by line against its candidates reads about a third of the cipher words: “in all armadas”, “so forwarde in the action that he promised Q[ueen]”, “have promised the haven of Rain”, “Italians arriving … Antonio”, “within France”. The consolidated reading, with a grade on every word, is in cobham1588/READING.md. The rest stays unread where signs could not be resolved from the scans.

01 The letters

William Brooke, Lord Cobham, was one of Elizabeth’s commissioners sent to treat with Parma’s deputies in the spring of 1588. The talks moved from Ostend to Bourbourg while the Armada was fitting out. The Calendar of State Papers Foreign (vol. 21 pt 4) calendars his letters in SP 77, but not these Harley copies. No printed decipherment of them was found.

02 The key, from the glossed siblings

On f. 75 (Ostend, 9 April 1588; R8488) the decipherer wrote the plain text over each cipher word: “cardinal is coming”, “upon our arrival”, “willing to harken”, “by the clergy they made means”, “their great desir of”, “the Duke of Parma his bastard son”. On f. 89 (R8493) the glosses read “what may be”, “further hoped for at their hands” and “our abode in this towne is so advantagious”. Aligning them gives the following table. Not found: j, q, x and z. Several signs on f. 88 (shapes like K, υ, ρ and W) are not in the glosses and may be code signs. A second session working on Cobham’s March letters (R8477–R8487) reached the same values independently from its own cribs, and read those letters: see Cobham at Ostend, March 1587/8 (key in harley287/key.tsv). Its table separates s (a narrow ∧) from t (a wide one), which may resolve the s/t ambiguity here.

03 The sign inventory, and what reads

A third pass worked from the scans at full resolution (7,200 pixels wide), aligning about sixty glossed words on ff. 75, 79 and 89 sign by sign. The result is cobham1588/signs.tsv. The families that look alike at reading size separate cleanly. p is a loop with its tail swept down-left (“upon”, “popes”, “perswade”). l is a circle crossed by a straight stem (“arrival”, “holding”). h is a stem with a bowl to the right (“harken”, “thought”). m is a horizontal S with a loop (“made”, “commission”), and w a dot and stroke (“willing”, “sworne”). The glosses also show that the system is more polyphonic than any simple key allows. Λ is s and t (and probably a), γ is r and y, c is o and once a, and the l sign once stands for t. The code numbers are glossed too: 7 = her Majesty, 10 = the Lord Admiral, 15 = the Duke of Parma, 16 = the King of Spain.

  • f. 80r: “as usuall in all armadas in numbers, that at the armada … which was the 20”. The word ΛV∽ΛŧΛ gives ARMADA with m glossed, if the wide Λ is a and ŧ is d. Also “hoping them in the □pances”, probably “expences”; a run with vertical word dividers ending “to these termes”; and “22 is … in the action … that he promised Q[ueen]”. (M)
  • f. 88r: “15:” is the Duke of Parma. The first cipher run probably begins “captain …” before a name, “with 40 men of …, [who] have promised the …”. The rest turns on signs no gloss shows. (M/I)
  • f. 92r: “4 sent by …”, perhaps “the Portugals” (I), and “of letters” (M).

04 What stays open

The sign inventory is now fixed, so the obstacle is no longer the image. Several signs are polyphonic and a few (an L-shape and a K-shape on f. 88, the square on f. 80, the codes 4, 22 and 27) are unglossed. The next step is mechanical: transcribe the runs sign by sign from the full-resolution crops, give each sign its set of possible letters from signs.tsv, enumerate each cipher word’s readings against a word list of the 1580s, and let the clear context choose.

05 The reading, letter by letter

  • f. 80r (5 May): “hoping them in the □pances [expences]”; “it will not be … the haven is …”; “to these termes as usuall in all armadas in numbers”; “22 is so forwarde in the action that he promised Q[ueen] to …”.
  • f. 88r: “captain Montgomery of late gone into Scotland … with 40 men of … have promised the haven of Rain … spoil … there to 15 [Parma]”; “deliverd of the … to us”; “Italians arriving … Antonio”; “other Scottishmen, divers … soldiers … away the number of …”.
  • f. 92r (27 May): “4 sent by the Portugals(?)”, “imprisoned in Bruges(?)”, “they have charge”, “of letters”.
  • f. 96v (9 June): “passed …”, “within France”, “his servants … priests”, “seventy”.
