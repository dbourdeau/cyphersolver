Beatrice d’Aragona to Eleonora, Pozsony, May–June 1482

Two letters of the Queen of Hungary to her sister at Ferrara, whose cipher passages were printed deciphered in 1877; the prints were matched to the manuscript images and no key was applied.

At a glanceExisting decipherment · complete

The letters
Five records of letters, four to Beatrice’s sister Eleonora and one to her nephew Ippolito, 1482–1505 (catalogue item 160).
Where
Eighteen photographs; the last shelfmark is b. 2/7, 11. DECODE R1154 is not encrypted and is dated 29 May 1505.
How it was read
Not re-solved: the printed correspondence settles it. The letters of 31 May and 8 June 1482 are nos. 10 and 12 in Magyar Diplomácziai Emlékek III (1877), the 8 March 1486 passage is in the 1878 supplement, and 2 May 1486 is III no. 75, with decipherment slips beside the cipher.
What it says
Matthias offers an intervention against Venice with ten thousand Swiss and six thousand cavalry, provided the league sends money to Milan.
Still open
The printed March decipherment keeps some unexplained editorial markers, left in.

Both are to Beatrice’s sister Eleonora d’Aragona, Duchess of Ferrara. DECODE gives R1141’s holder as “Cancelleria, 1523”, its record name says 1623.

02 31 May 1482: troops against Venice

The edition gives the plaintext of the cipher: Matthias offers an intervention against Venice, provided the league sends money to Milan. The proposed force comprises ten thousand Swiss and six thousand cavalry; five hundred more cavalry, including a hundred hussars, also need a safe route. He does not intend to declare himself openly against Venice before his relationship with the league is settled.

uno expediente optimo et necessario per lo favore et salute de tuta la lega

Dece milia Svizari et sei milia cavalli del Signor Re di Hungaria.

These are excerpts of the 1877 decipherment. The manuscript’s cleartext and cipher boundaries match the edition’s roman and italic sections. The editorial note states that the italic words were written in secret signs.

03 8 June 1482: money and effective cooperation

Beatrice relays Matthias’s criticism of the league’s reluctance to spend. The king repeats the troop offer, requires payment at Milan, and asks for decisive cooperation. The published text has the proverb:

ali avari che per sparagnare uno grosso perdeno lo ducato

Misers save a grosso and lose a ducat. Near the end the decipherment describes endless negotiations as “uno Jocare de balla cum retornare sempre ali principij”—playing ball and always returning to the starting point. The printed page has balla; the OCR text gave holla. The edition and manuscript both date the letter 8 June, despite a secondary citation giving July.

04 What was verified

The evidence is the agreement of dates, salutations, clear passages and cipher boundaries with the edition. No full transcription of the symbols was made, no key was recovered and no percentage was measured. The method is existing decipherment: printed decipherments found and matched to the images. The letter-by-letter accuracy of the older decipherment has not been tested. The 1482 alphabet looks different from those of the 1486 letters.

Tomokiyo’s Cryptiana index has no article on Beatrice; his other publications were not searched. Archive photographs remain local because DECODE states that their publication requires archive permission.

Beatrice of Aragon writes to her sister Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara, during the War of Ferrara: for 100,000 ducats sent to Milan, King Matthias would raise 16,000 men (10,000 Swiss and 6,000 cavalry) in the Brescia and Verona districts against Venice, but will not declare himself openly before his relations with the league are settled. # R1140, Beatrice of Aragon to Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara, Pozsony, 31 May 1482: English translation

Most Illustrious Lady, most honoured Sister, after due recommendation. I am writing more fully on behalf of His Majesty
the King my consort to His Majesty the Lord King our father about the matters below, for fear these letters might
miscarry, the road being dangerous; and I shall leave long words aside and come to the conclusion. Having understood the
danger you are in, the Lord King of Hungary, notwithstanding the great business he has in being at war on two sides, one
against the Turk, the other against the attack that the Emperor with the whole Empire seeks to make on him, has
nevertheless decided on an excellent and necessary expedient for the favour and safety of the whole league. His Majesty,
after he understood the preparations of the Pope and of the Venetians against the league, and above all against your
state, sent to the Swiss, who are his confederates, and an answer has come with some captains of the said Swiss, and [?]
and the matter arranged, that if the league is willing only to spend a hundred thousand ducats and send them to Milan,
and the Lord King of Hungary to bear the rest of the expense, and that it be soon, he will bring it about that by the end
of the month of August there will be sixteen thousand fighting men in the territories of Brescia and Verona against the
Venetians: ten thousand Swiss and six thousand horse of the Lord King of Hungary. Furthermore His Majesty holds 500 horse
ready to send you, among them a hundred hussars; but he must be told by what route he is to send them to you: you must
send us a commissary of yours. Besides that, the Lord King of Hungary is sending Megrebal [Magyar Balázs] to the Croatian
border to raise men, likewise in your favour; but he declares to you that, not being otherwise considered in your
league, he does not wish to declare himself openly an enemy of the Venetians, but that the said Swiss should make war as
soldiers of the league, and he would gladly have Don Federico as their captain; and when the King of Hungary has entered
your league, he intends to show himself openly an enemy of the Venetians. And beyond this he further offers that, if the
league will help him with only a hundred thousand ducats more, His Majesty will spend on his side a much greater sum, and
will raise another army and fall upon the Venetians from the other side, so that everyone would see that the way would
be good. So that when the league is minded in earnest to help itself, it will find the right way not only to divert the
Venetians from attacking it, but not to be attacked by them.

Encourage the Most Illustrious Lord Duke to do his diligence and duty about the above, and meanwhile to resist
steadfastly; for, as I said above, when the league is willing to make the said expense, whatever it wishes will be easy
for it. The Lord King of Hungary is willing to put in much more than as much again, to make the Venetians feel more
damaged than they think to damage others. All I write above is by the will and order of the Most Serene Lord King my
consort and your brother, with compassionate and affectionate help, and to put it into effective execution. Pozsony, the
last of May 1482.

Your Most Illustrious Ladyship's most devoted and obedient sister, the Queen of Hungary.

To the Most Illustrious Lady Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara etc., Lady and most honoured sister.

Pozsony, 8 June 1482

Beatrice reports Matthias's displeasure at the league's unwillingness to spend, repeats the Swiss and cavalry offer, asks for money to be sent to Milan and calls for action rather than negotiations that keep 'returning to the beginning' like a ball game. # R1141, Beatrice of Aragon to Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara, Pozsony, 8 June 1482: English translation

Most Illustrious Lady and my most honoured sister. I have understood what Your Ladyship [?writes] [?to] me in your
letter of the 14th of last month, and with great displeasure; all of which I have made known to the Most Serene Lord King
my consort, who has had no less displeasure at it; but in part he lays the blame on the whole league, which, had it been
willing to spend, would have had plenty of help at small cost, and such that it would not only have taken [them], but
would have reduced them to scarcity in defending themselves; and he rightly says that so it usually happens to misers,
who to save a groat lose the ducat. [?Now], things standing thus, and His Majesty finding himself with two armies, one
against the Turk, the other against the Emperor and the Germans, he has thought of the expedient that I write in the
other letters enclosed here, which is prompt and excellent. It remains for the league to make diligence in sending money
to Milan; and when His Majesty is assured of it, so as not to lose time, he will order some captains of the Swiss to
come with a commissary of His Majesty to take the payment for the 10,000 foot and six thousand horse; for he does not
intend to make or send a smaller army, so that whatever event or opposition may arise, it may be sufficient by itself.
His Majesty has been waiting for the ambassadors of the Most Serene League, who it has been said for a long time, and
confirmed more from day to day, were coming; which has been the reason for keeping Messer Francesco Fontana from being
[?despatched]. When the league is willing to [?help] itself with the Lord King of Hungary, and do what is fitting and
what reason requires, we believe, Your Most Illustrious Ladyship, that he alone will be enough to save the state of the
league and to punish the Venetians. But that league must come to a different understanding with His Majesty, and the
conditions be brought to their true duty; for if it should be necessary to make an army of a hundred thousand persons,
being helped with money in no too great a sum, he will do it quickly, and with men of his life. When the league comes to
an understanding with His Majesty in earnest, His Majesty will find a good way to settle the wars he has here, so as to
join with the league in the oppression of the Venetians; but he means to go into it in deed and not with negotiations;
and so His Majesty will put his kingdom and his life at risk, once and for all, to remove such [?rivals]; for otherwise,
he says, it is a game of [?ball] that always returns to the beginning. I always commend myself to Your Ladyship. Pozsony,
8 June 1482.

Your Most Illustrious Ladyship's obedient sister, the Queen of Hungary etc.

To the Most Illustrious Lady Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara etc., Lady and most honoured sister.
