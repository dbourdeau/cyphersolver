“L’Estat du Roy de Navarre et de son party en France”

BL, Harley MS 1582 ff. 263–264 (DECODE R8505). Four sides of French political memoir on Henri de Navarre’s standing, his places, his gentlemen and his alliances, with long runs of cipher written inline in the same hand, in mid-sentence.

The document is identified and transcribed here for the first time — about 3,630 signs — and the cipher is not broken. The second record of the same catalogue item, R8501, proved to be one of Sir Edward Stafford’s despatches, already read from the contemporary decipherment bound with it.

Summary. The catalogue item paired two Harley 1582 records as “unknown sender to unknown recipient”. They are not one problem. R8501 is Stafford’s, ff. 71–72, and its own f. 72r carries the contemporary English decipherment, transcribed in this repository’s Stafford 1586 work; nothing there is open. R8505 is a different document in a different cipher, and DECODE describes it only as four pages of graphic signs, language “English?”, no title, no date, no author. Its clear passages give it a title: L’Estat du Roy de Navarre & de son party en France, a French survey of Navarre’s party written after 1580 — the peace “de l’an 1577”, Brouage “l’an 1580” — and plainly meant for an outside reader, which is how it came to sit in an English secretary’s file. Every cipher run on all four sides was transcribed here: about 3,630 signs, some fifty shapes, plus ten nomenclator numbers written between dots. The statistics say homophonic substitution, not a syllabary: index of coincidence 0.040, and Sukhotin’s test splits the signs cleanly into about twenty vowel signs carrying half the text. Annealing against French, English, Spanish and Italian language models nevertheless recovers nothing: every language scores the same, about −2.9 per character where real text scores −1.9. Status: not solved. What would finish it is a cleaner single-reader transcription of the sign families that three readers disagreed on, or another copy of this widely-circulated memoir with the ciphered passages written out in clear.

01 Two records, one of them already read

The catalogue item covered DECODE R8501 and R8505, both at BL, Harley MS 1582, both “Non-decrypted”, seven pages between them. Harley 1582 is a made-up volume of state papers, and the two records are a century and a language apart in everything but shelfmark.

R8501 is ff. 71–72: cipher runs in one of Sir Edward Stafford’s 1586 despatches from Paris, with the contemporary decipherment of those runs written out in clear on f. 72r. That page was transcribed while reading the neighbouring letter R8500, and the letter key of Stafford’s Walsingham cipher was rebuilt from it (Stafford, 20 August 1586). There is nothing unread in R8501.

R8505 is ff. 263–264, four images, and is the subject of the rest of this page.

02 What the memoir is

The clear French, which is most of the four sides, is a systematic survey of Henri de Navarre as a political asset. It opens on his two “qualitez” — first prince of the blood, so that his authority can only grow while the king has no children, and chief of the reformed churches of France — and goes on through the places he holds and their garrisons in Guyenne, Languedoc, Dauphiné and Provence, the counts and viscounts of his following (Turenne, Châtillon, Laval, Montgomery, Rohan, Lesdiguières, Ségur, La Rochefoucauld, Montauban, Cahors, Figeac), the harquebusiers each region can raise, the Catholic party and the German and Swiss alliances, and the marriage question. Dates inside it run to 1576, 1577 and 1580; La Rochelle, Brouage and Saint-Jean-d’Angély are current concerns. That is the world of Navarre’s embassies to the Protestant powers in the early and middle 1580s, and this repository holds the cipher of one of them: the numeric key that Navarre used with his envoy Ségur-Pardaillan, recovered in Ségur 1585–86.

The ciphered runs are not a separate passage. They are written into the sentences, in the same hand and ink, wherever the memoir turns to something the writer did not want read in transit — exactly the habit of the Stafford letters elsewhere in the volume.

03 The cipher, as far as the statistics go

  • Graphic signs, about fifty shapes: Greek-looking and geometric forms — L, Γ, Π, &Sha;, β, ε, ψ, φ, χ, η, ∂, an hourglass, an ω with a bar over it, and several verticals distinguished only by where their crossbars and loops sit. The full chart, with shape descriptions and the ASCII names used in the transcription, is signs.md in the folder.
  • Dot groups of one to four dots punctuate the runs and do not behave like letters.
  • Nomenclator numbers written between dots: 6, 21, 26, 36, 51, 57, 72, 81, 99 and 721. Their positions (one of them next to “les plus grands princes de la Chrestienté”, another in a clause about the king of Navarre) say they are names, but none of them is read.
  • Homophonic, not syllabic. Over the 3,630 transcribed tokens the index of coincidence is 0.040, half what a plain substitution on French gives; the commonest sign carries 8.7% of the text, too much for a syllable and about right for one of several signs sharing a common letter; and Sukhotin’s vowel test splits the signs into roughly twenty “vowel” signs carrying 51% of the text against thirty-odd consonantal ones. That is the profile of an alphabet with several homophones per letter.

04 What was done

  1. The four full-resolution images were fetched from DECODE and read in overlapping strips. The written lines rise by nearly a full line-height across the page, so a run has to be followed by its words across the strips rather than by row position; getting this wrong was the main source of error in the first pass.
  2. Every cipher run on all four sides was transcribed against a shared sign chart (ct_p1.txt … ct_p4.txt, ~3,630 tokens), with the clear words before and after each run recorded so any future reading can be placed in its sentence.
  3. Homophonic annealing (solve.py, solve3.py) with the repository’s French language models of 1530–1600 and 1570–1640, order 4 and 5 without spaces, a unigram-frequency penalty, Sukhotin’s vowel/consonant split as a constraint, and up to twenty restarts of 400,000 moves — over the whole text, and page by page in case the three transcribers’ sign names had drifted apart.
  4. A variant in which each sign may stand for a letter, a common syllable, a double or a null (solve2.py), on the model of the design a 1583 letter to Ségur describes for Navarre’s ciphers: “toutes choses tellement dispersées en lettres, syllabes, doubles, nulles…”.
  5. A language control: the same search under English, Spanish, Italian and seventeenth-century French models. All four land within a few per cent of the French despatch model, about −2.9 per character against −1.9 for real text. When no language wins, no key has been found.

05 What stays open, and why

The whole of R8505 is unread: 3,630 signs, ten code numbers, no key material anywhere. Three things could be in the way, in this order.

The transcription. Several sign families are separated by a loop or the side a crossbar sits on: the two hooked verticals, the three ω forms, plain against crossed ε, plain against hooked Π. All three readers flagged them, and a ten to fifteen per cent sign error rate is enough on its own to stop homophonic annealing at this length. One reader re-reading all four sides in a single pass against the fixed chart is the next step, not another solver.

The design. If the key mixes letters with syllables, doubles and nulls, as Navarre’s ciphers of these years did, a letter-only search cannot converge and the syllabic variant is too loose to converge either without a crib.

A crib. This memoir is the sort of paper that circulated in several copies. Another copy — in the Dupuy or français collections, or in one of the printed collections of League-era pieces — with these passages written in clear would decide the key in an afternoon. Searches of the calendars of State Papers Foreign for 1583–86 and of the printed literature under this title found nothing.
