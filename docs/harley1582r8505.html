— f. 264v: the three dots are the letter m

France → an English secretary’s file · homophonic graphic signs, m written as dots · after 1580 · broken here, read

“L’Estat du Roy de Navarre et de son party en France”

BL, Harley MS 1582 ff. 263–264 (DECODE R8505): four sides on Henri de Navarre’s party — his places, his gentlemen, his alliances — with the sensitive clauses enciphered inline in the same hand, in mid-sentence. DECODE has no title, no date, no author, and gives the language as “English?”.

Identified, transcribed and deciphered here. The cipher is a homophonic alphabet of about fifty graphic signs; the letter m is written as a group of two, three or four dots, and taking those for punctuation is what defeated the first attack.

Summary. The catalogue item paired two Harley 1582 records as “unknown sender to unknown recipient”. They are not one problem: R8501 is one of Sir Edward Stafford’s 1586 despatches, already read from the contemporary decipherment on its own f. 72r (Stafford, 20 August 1586), while R8505 is a French political memoir in a cipher nobody had read. Its clear passages give it a title — L’Estat du Roy de Navarre & de son party en France — and a date after 1580. All four sides were transcribed (about 3,600 signs) and the cipher was then broken from the ciphertext alone, with no key, no crib and no decipherment anywhere. The break came out of a control experiment rather than a new idea about the manuscript: real French, enciphered with a homophonic key of the same shape, defeated the solver in exactly the same way, which located the fault in the search and not in the text. With the search corrected the key fell out at once, and the recovered alphabet then diagnosed the transcription — it had no sign for m or b, which is impossible for French. The dot groups, read as punctuation by every earlier pass, are the letter m; a closed oval pierced by a stem is b. Re-read on that basis, 95.1% of the deciphered text falls in French words: the prince of Parma, a party of malcontents, an Italian engineer at Lisbon, Beaucaire on the Rhône, Béziers, Carcassonne, Montmorency, d’Épernon. Status: key recovered, text read (95.1% measured). What is left is the ten nomenclator numbers, which are names, and about 5% of scattered characters where the ink is faint or two shapes of one family cannot be told apart.

01 Two records, one of them already read

The catalogue item covered DECODE R8501 and R8505, both in BL, Harley MS 1582, both “Non-decrypted”. Harley 1582 is a made-up volume of state papers, and the two records have nothing in common but the shelfmark.

R8501 (ff. 71–72) is cipher in a Stafford despatch of 1586 whose contemporary decipherment is written out in clear on f. 72r. That page was transcribed, and Stafford’s letter key rebuilt from it, while reading the neighbouring letter: see Stafford to Walsingham, 20 August 1586. Nothing there is open.

R8505 (ff. 263–264, four images) is the subject of this page.

02 What the memoir is

The clear French, which is most of the four sides, surveys Henri de Navarre as a political asset: his two “qualitez” — first prince of the blood, so that his authority can only grow while the king has no children, and chief of the reformed churches of France — then the places he holds and their garrisons in Guyenne, Languedoc, Dauphiné and Provence, the counts and viscounts of his following, the harquebusiers each region can raise, the Catholic party, the German and Swiss alliances, and the marriage question. Internal dates run to 1576, 1577 and 1580; La Rochelle, Brouage and Saint-Jean-d’Angély are current concerns. That is the world of Navarre’s embassies to the Protestant powers in the early and middle 1580s — this repository holds the numeric cipher of one of them, recovered in Ségur 1585–86 — and it explains how the paper came to rest in an English secretary’s file.

03 The cipher, and the letter written as dots

A homophonic alphabet of about fifty graphic shapes — Greek-looking and geometric forms, written spaced out sign by sign with no word division — plus ten nomenclator numbers written between dots (6, 21, 26, 36, 51, 57, 72, 81, 99, 721), which are names and stay unread. Three to six homophones carry the frequent letters and one sign each the rare ones: an ordinary chancery design of the 1580s.

The dot groups are a letter. Every earlier reading of these pages — including the first pass here — took the clusters of two, three and four dots for punctuation or word division, and dropped them. They are the letter m, which is why the first recovered key had no m in it at all. The controls are unambiguous: 7 Eb :2 psi G o B Gm b Tu o wf = “et maintenant”, Xo psi :3 G B dd 7 = “l’amitié”, :4 PiG b n c Gm q wT ne L = “Monsieur de”. Two further pairs had been collapsed as well: φ (b) against the tall long-s (u) — “de beau|caire”, “d’une bonne armée” — and the two-bowled ε (s) against the lunate ε (a).

04 How it was broken

  1. The first attack failed. All four sides were transcribed (about 3,600 signs) and put through homophonic annealing against the repository’s French models. Nothing came out — and, tested under English, Spanish and Italian models too, every language scored alike, which is what no key at all looks like.
  2. A control located the fault. Real French was enciphered with a fifty-sign homophonic key of the same shape and run through the same solver (control.py). It failed in exactly the same way, so the obstacle was the search, not the manuscript. Two faults: a Sukhotin vowel/consonant constraint that is simply wrong for a key of this shape, and a temperature scale far too small for log-probability differences of this size. With both corrected the control recovers 100% of its key, and still 95.5% of its characters when 15% of its signs are randomly corrupted — so transcription noise at the level this manuscript produces is survivable.
  3. The real text then broke. The corrected search returned French immediately (“et qu’ils attendent”, “à l’avenir”, “duquel”), and basin-hopping with a word-segmentation objective recovered the alphabet.
  4. The key then diagnosed the transcription. A French key with no sign for m and none for b is impossible. That single observation sent the work back to the images, where the dot groups, φ and the two epsilons resolved it; ff. 263v and 264v were re-read sign by sign against the key, changing 29% and 31% of their signs.

05 The reading

The full text, run by run with its clear-text context, is in reading.txt. The cipher carries what the writer would not put in clear: a Spanish drift, a marriage, an army of reiters, and the people who might be bought.

  • “… auec le prince de Parme pour lui rendre les places …”
  • “… contre luy un party de malcontens …”
  • “… ou instrument principal de la ruine de ceus de la religion, come ainsi soit qu’il ne peut desguiser ce faict …”
  • “… italien qui sert d’ingenieur a Lisbone …”
  • “… pour le rejoindre auec ceste maison ennemie de la chrestienté …”
  • “… qu’il la charge d’une armee a sa deuotion …”; “… sortir de la une armee de reistres pour la jeter et espandre sur le pais de celuy qui …”
  • “… tandis que le party de ceus de la religion demeurera ferme en [72] …”
  • f. 264v, on Languedoc: “et maintenant jouissant de l’amitié estroite de Monsieur de Mont[morenci], auquel la leur est necessaire … places de toute la province de Languedoc … estant icelui asseuré de Beaucaire sur le Rosne et de la seneschaussée de Bésiers … Carcassonne … a la devotion du Roy de Navarre.”
  • f. 263r names d’Épernon in cipher, and f. 264r argues about “quelque gentilhomme”, “les causes”, “la guerre”, “nos provinces”, “fortes et asseurées”, “pour la conservation”.

06 What stays open

95.1% of the 3,551 deciphered characters fall inside French words, measured by Viterbi segmentation against a 37,000-word vocabulary built from the repository’s French corpora. The measure is conservative: sixteenth-century spellings and proper names the vocabulary does not hold count as unread.

Two things are left. The ten nomenclator numbers are names; three have contexts that narrow them — 72 is a country and almost certainly France (“la guere contre ceus dela religion en 72”, “demeurera ferme en 72”), 81 a person and probably the King (“le 81 son frere”), 21 a feminine noun, the League or the army (“21 ch[ar]gee dela guere”) — but none is fixed by the text. And about 5% of the characters do not resolve into French words: faint ink, and shapes ambiguous within one family.

A note on method, since it bears on the figure. ff. 263v and 264v were re-read sign by sign from the images against the recovered key. On ff. 263r and 264r the first-pass transcription was corrected only by a constrained pass: a token may be swapped solely between shapes that the second reading proved confusable — the dot groups against the f sign (a stem with a dot cluster at its upper right, so a faint stem reads as a bare cluster), φ against the long-s, the two epsilons, the two stem-loops, χ against the hourglass, the three bar-shapes, the bracket against Π — and only where the word model gains clearly. That is 77 tokens, 2% of the text, and every one is listed in emendations_all.txt so it can be checked against the manuscript.
