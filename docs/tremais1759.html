01 The two letters

Trémais was sent to Quebec in 1759 to work under the intendant François Bigot and to report on the abuses in the colony. His instruction of 3 February 1759, printed by Antoine Roy, ends: “On lui remet ci-joint un chiffre particulier à lui pour sa correspondance dont il ne fera usage que pour les choses secrettes dont il aura à rendre compte.” Roy’s dossier prints neither the table nor this letter.

Two cipher letters of his are in volume 104 of the Canada correspondence. The first, of 22 September 1759 (ff. 342–343v), was deciphered at the ministry: the clear copy is bound after it (ff. 344–345), headed “Copie de la lettre en chiffre de M. Querdisien Tremais, dattée du Canada le 22. 7bre 1759”. The second, of 6 November (ff. 346–347v), is the covering letter for a copy of the first. No decipherment is bound with it, and the transcripts made for the Canadian archives leave it out. It has about 1,350 numbers: 1,277 in the text and 73 in marginal notes (the comparison of the two transcriptions counted 1,279 in the text).

02 The key in the September letter

In September Trémais wrote in haste; his own last lines fear “autant de désordre dans l’arrangement des caractères qu’il s’en trouvera dans mon stile”. He wrote both numbers of most units: one above the other (497 over 119 for p, 219 over 466 for s), or joined by a dash for a word (12–437 de, 15–287 le, 81–178 homme). In November he wrote one number for each unit. The September letter set against its clear copy therefore gives the homophone classes of the table as well as the values.

The alignment covers 564 cipher places and gives a value to 168 numbers, 87 of them confirmed by recurrence. Four conflicts stayed open (437, 89, 176, 274). About 60 values were then added or corrected from the November letter, each with the word that fixed it; those taken from the earlier partial decipherment are marked as such in the key file. None of the three numerical tables of the Montcalm and Vaudreuil file (COL C11E 10) fits: where Trémais has 98 il, 108 r, 115 M. Bigot, two of them have rivière, tion, guerre and comme, quoique, eau; the third had been rejected earlier by Brice Dudout on a score against shuffled keys.

03 What the letter says

The marks follow the reading file: words deciphered and sure, words conjectural (in square brackets with a question mark), and runs not deciphered. The full French text, the English translation and the key are in the Full texts section.

Que [quelque?] répréhensible que soit M. Bigot soit d’avoir donné en faisant un commerce immense un exemple pernicieux d’où sont résultés une infinité d’abus dont il n’a point arrêté le cours, soit par les autres vices que l’on peut reprocher à son administration, on ne sauroit néanmoins sans injustice l’accuser d’avoir seul causé tous les maux qui ont affligé le Canada ni d’avoir seul donné lieu à la prodigieuse augmentation des dépenses de cette colonie: les désordres ont eu bien d’autres sources.

He then describes the posts (two nouns in this paragraph, the stores and the notes, are conjectural), where “le militaire y est absolument despote”: works are paid on its certificates, everything leaves the stores and the provisions go to the detachments on its orders, and rations are delivered on notes signed by the majors of the corps. Officers, he writes, committed “des bassesses dont un simple soldat eût été partout ailleurs sévèrement puni”.

His plain opinion follows, part of it conjectural: Canada lacked a governor and an intendant, and the army a chief. The two heads “n’étoient donc à proprement parler que des fantômes … incapables d’opérer par eux-mêmes ou craignant le travail”; the authority “résidoit réellement” in Péan, helped (a conjectural word) by Mercier, who with the secretaries of the two chiefs governed them both. They were backed by a man “tiré de l’état de garçon boucher”, “d’une activité que rien ne peut déconcerter”, without whom “on eût eu bien de la peine à [faire?] subsister les armées”. By the sense this is the munitionnaire; the number that names him (480) has no confirmed value.

The last portrait is of a man with talents that would have made “un excellent jurisconsulte” (the verb is conjectural) and a prodigious memory, “né d’un [caractère?] violent”, without “la fermeté qu’il faut pour retenir des troupes dans la discipline”, who “perdoit la tête dans les occasions où il étoit le plus important de la conserver”. The sense points to Montcalm; the name rests on a doubtful reading of one number and is marked conjectural. The sentence that closes it, “personne n’a selon moi plus contribué que lui de toutes les manières à la …”, ends in two words not deciphered.

04 Method and control

The November letter was transcribed here before the earlier transcription was opened, then the two were compared: they agree exactly on 737 of 1,279 numbers, and 279 could not be decided on the image. The September key, with six choices among its conflicts, gives a value to 1,131 of the 1,350 groups. Every unread or doubtful place was then tried again on enlarged crops with the extended key: of 268 places, 181 ended sure, 64 conjectural and 23 open. The image decided no digit; the gains came from recurring misreadings of September numbers and from new values that work in two places or more.

Control: the key built from the September pair alone, frozen before any November decode, was applied to November with the first digit alternative everywhere. It scores −2.424 log-probability per character on a French model of the period; the same values dealt at random to the same numbers, 200 times, give −2.617 at best and −3.247 at the median. One reservation: the transcriber of the September letter had been given a list of guide values that came from the earlier partial decipherment.

The share deciphered is an estimate. The reading has 357 cipher words sure and 92 conjectural; the places left open hold 67 numbers, about 23 words at 2.9 numbers a word. That makes about 472 cipher words, 0.756 of them sure and 0.951 with the conjectural words. No person has yet reviewed the reading.

05 What remains uncertain

  • 92 conjectural words: the sense fits, but a digit or a value seen once is not confirmed. Among them: the opening “quelque”, “l’intérêt”, the sentence that Canada lacked a governor and an intendant, “le munitionnaire”, “Beauport” and “M. de Montcalm”.
  • 25 unread runs, 13 on f. 346v and 12 on f. 347r: numbers with no value in the September pair, most of them seen once (39, 474, 411, 405, 288, 388, 487 or 687, 498, 289, 116, 200), or runs where every number has a value and no word comes out.
  • The line ends of 40 of the 53 lines of f. 346v, and some of f. 347v, are lost in the gutter of the microfilm frame.
  • The marginal notes of ff. 346r and 346v (73 numbers) are not deciphered.
  • Library and Archives Canada serves one rendition of the microfilm, 1125 pixels for a two-page spread. Scans from the original at the Archives nationales d’outre-mer, or from microfilm reel F-104, would settle most of the doubtful digits. Trémais’s table may survive with the papers Roy printed from (Marine C 246, now MAR/C/7/246) or in the minutes of series B 109–110; neither was seen.

J'ai eu l'honneur de vous informer huit jours après mon arrivée à Québec, que M. Bigot m'avoit chargé du Bureau des fonds, de celui des troupes et du détail des magazins; mais à peine ai-je été en fonction, que l'arrivée des ennemis dans le fleuve ayant obligé de faire passer aux Trois Rivières tous les papiers concernant le service, je me suis trouvé privé de tout ce qui pouvoit me fournir quelque instruction sur les parties qui m'ont été confiées. Les autres moyens d'avoir des preuves juridiques m'ont manqué également. Je vous avouerai même, Monseigneur, que je ne prévois pas que quelques recherches que je puisse faire, je parvienne à vous en procurer. Les affaires frauduleuses se traitent ordinairement sans écriture; et il est naturel de croire que s'il y en avoit eu icy, les gens qui en sont dépositaires, ne seroient nullement disposés à les montrer. Je me vois donc obligé de m'arrêter aux discours généraux: j'en ai beaucoup recueillis de gens qui m'ont paru dignes de foy; je les ai comparés ensemble, et en les rapprochant de ce que j'ai vû moy même.
Une des premières qualités d'un homme en place étant sans contredit le désintéressement, on ne peut disconvenir que M. Bigot n'ait mérité, en faisant une fortune immense par le commerce, les reproches qu'on lui a faits: il y en a eu à la vérité beaucoup de calomnieux; mais enfin ils étoient tous excités par les maux que l'on ne ressentoit que depuis son administration, où il faut avouer que tout a été dans une confusion d'où sont résultés mille abus différents. Il ne me sieroit certainement point, Monseigneur, d'essayer de développer aux yeux d'un Ministre aussy éclairé que vous les désordres qui me semblent devoir toujours naître du commerce que toute espèce d'officier, et surtout les chefs pourront faire; mais je dois aussy à la confiance dont vous voulez bien
[f.344v]
m'honorer, ne vous point cacher que ce ne sont pas là les seuls dont le Canada ait été affligé. Les troupes qui en France sont insatiables, ont pensé que leurs prétentions ne devoient plus avoir de bornes en Amérique, et surtout vis à vis d'un Intendant qui dans l'inquiétude que lui causoient les discours publics, voulut acheter dans le principe leur suffrage, soit en les traitant splendidement à sa table, soit en leur accordant toutes leurs demandes. Il comptoit que la paix viendroit bientost.
Tant que les choses restèrent sur ce pied, tout retentit de ses éloges; mais dès que l'épuisement des magazins l'obligèrent à tenir une conduite différente, il devint alors tout à coup de l'homme le plus aimable, le plus inique voleur de la terre. Ce qui ne pouvoit plus se délivrer des magazins fut exigé en argent: il n'y eut point à reculer, M. de Montcalm parloit. De ces différentes causes est provenue en partie l'augmentation prodigieuse des dépenses qui vous ont avec tant de raison effrayé. On peut bien accuser M. Bigot d'avoir occasionné par le commerce qu'il a introduit dans la colonie, et encore plus par les abus qu'il y a laissé régner, la cherté des marchandises et des denrées; mais la consommation qui en a été faite, quelque énorme qu'elle paroisse, peut être regardée à bien peu de chose près comme réelle: elle peut être aisément constatée par des pièces presque toutes émanées du militaire même. Il y a, Monseigneur, des manœuvres dans tous les États. J'ai eu en particulier occasion de l'apercevoir dans le détail que j'avois à la suite de l'armée. M. Bigot m'avoit chargé de la partie des vivres et des hôpitaux; j'ai mis tout en usage pour y établir l'ordre. Les rations se délivroient uniquement sur les billets des majors et cependant la quantité en excédoit toujours considérablement le nombre des troupes dont je n'ai jamais pu connoître l'effectif. On n'admet
[f.345r]
point icy l'usage des Rôles: je prendrai même la liberté de vous ajouter, Monseigneur, qu'on y laisse périr toute espèce de discipline militaire: le soldat abandonné à la licence la plus effrénée vole de toutes parts: jamais dégât ne fut plus déplorable: moissons, bétail, volailles, légumes, plantations de tabac, tout a été ravagé impitoyablement dans un pays qui éprouvoit déjà la plus grande disette, et que la famine menaçoit. Toutes représentations faites à ce sujet ont été inutiles. Vous ne serez pas surpris, Monseigneur, après cela de l'évènement funeste qui nous a enfin fait parvenir au comble de l'humiliation.
Je crains bien, Monseigneur, que la précipitation avec laquelle je suis obligé de vous écrire n'ait occasionné autant de désordre dans l'arrangement des caractères qu'il s'en trouvera dans mon stile. Je n'ai le temps de rien vérifier. J'ose espérer que vous voudrez bien tout pardonner à l'embarras où me jette la circonstance.
J'ai l'honneur d'être &c.

# Trémais to the minister, Canada, 22 Sept 1759: English translation of the contemporary decipherment

Translation of `sept_clear_copy.txt` (ANOM COL C11A 104 ff.344-345, "Copie de la lettre en chiffre de M. Querdisien Tremais,
dattée du Canada le 22. 7bre 1759"). Made 23 Nov 2026.

I had the honour to inform you, a week after my arrival at Quebec, that M. Bigot had put me in charge of the office of funds,
of that of the troops and of the detail of the stores; but I had hardly taken up my duties when the arrival of the enemy in the
river made it necessary to send to Trois-Rivières all the papers concerning the service, and I found myself deprived of
everything that could give me some instruction on the branches entrusted to me. The other means of obtaining legal proofs
failed me equally. I shall even admit to you, My Lord, that I do not foresee that, whatever inquiries I may make, I shall manage
to procure you any. Fraudulent business is ordinarily done without writing; and it is natural to believe that if there had been
any here, the people who hold it would not be at all disposed to show it. I therefore see myself obliged to stop at general
talk: I have gathered much of it from people who seemed to me worthy of belief; I have compared them together, and with what I
have seen myself.

One of the first qualities of a man in office being without question disinterestedness, it cannot be denied that M. Bigot, by
making an immense fortune through trade, has deserved the reproaches made to him: many of them, in truth, were slanderous; but
in the end they were all provoked by the ills that were felt only since his administration, in which it must be admitted that
everything has been in a confusion from which a thousand different abuses have resulted. It would certainly not become me, My
Lord, to try to set out before the eyes of a minister as enlightened as you the disorders that seem to me bound always to arise
from the trade that every kind of officer, and above all the chiefs, may carry on; but I owe it also to the confidence with
which you are good enough to honour me not to hide from you that these are not the only ones by which Canada has been
afflicted. The troops, who in France are insatiable, thought that their claims should no longer have any bounds in America, and
above all towards an intendant who, in the uneasiness that public talk caused him, wished at the outset to buy their good word,
either by entertaining them splendidly at his table or by granting them all their requests. He counted on peace coming soon.

As long as things stayed on that footing, everything rang with his praises; but as soon as the exhaustion of the stores obliged
him to behave differently, he became all at once, from the most amiable of men, the most iniquitous thief on earth. What could
no longer be issued from the stores was demanded in money: there was no drawing back, M. de Montcalm spoke. From these different
causes has come in part the prodigious increase of the expenses that have with so much reason alarmed you. M. Bigot may well be
accused of having brought about, by the trade he introduced into the colony, and still more by the abuses he let reign there,
the dearness of goods and provisions; but the consumption that was made of them, however enormous it appears, may be regarded
as real to within very little: it can easily be established by documents almost all issued by the military itself. There are
tricks, My Lord, in every estate. I had particular occasion to notice it in the detail I had with the army. M. Bigot had put me
in charge of provisions and hospitals; I used every means to establish order there. Rations were issued solely on the notes of
the majors, and yet their quantity always considerably exceeded the number of the troops, whose effective strength I was never
able to learn. The use of rolls is not admitted here: I shall even take the liberty of adding, My Lord, that every kind of
military discipline is left to perish here: the soldier, abandoned to the most unbridled licence, steals on all sides: never was
damage more deplorable: harvests, cattle, poultry, vegetables, tobacco plantations, everything was laid waste without pity in a
country that was already suffering the greatest dearth and that famine threatened. All representations made on this subject
were useless. You will not be surprised, My Lord, after that, at the fatal event that has at last brought us to the height of
humiliation.

I much fear, My Lord, that the haste with which I am obliged to write to you may have caused as much disorder in the arrangement
of the characters as there will be in my style. I have no time to check anything. I dare hope that you will kindly forgive
everything to the confusion into which the circumstance throws me.

I have the honour to be, etc.
