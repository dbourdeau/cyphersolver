The letters
Seven court despatches to Marshal Catinat, July–September 1691: two from Louvois (8 and 9 July) and five from Louis XIV (19, 24 and 29 August, 6 and 14 September).
Where
Printed in figures in the 1819 Mémoires of Catinat, vol. 2, pp. 295–342, from the Bavarian State Library’s OCR; the originals at Vincennes were not consulted.
The cipher
A three-figure group code. Le Bouyer de Saint-Gervais printed it in 1819 unread.
How it was read
Étienne Bazeries reconstructed the code from these pages in 1893 and printed only two letters and the opening of a third. His published table decodes all seven, recovering 12,362 groups.
What it says
Louvois orders Bulonde shut up at Pignerol “avec un masque”. The longest letter, 14 September, has the King choose the solid course: bring the army back over the Alps, hold the passes, keep Carmagnole then burn it, and take Coni in winter.

Six groups misprinted above the table’s range in 1819, and an OCR error rate near one group in seventy.

01 The documents

The 1691 campaign in Piedmont opened with the failed siege of Coni (Cuneo), abandoned by Bulonde on 28 June against orders, and closed with the French army still in the plain between Turin and Pignerol, unable to take Coni or to winter. Catinat commanded; Louvois, secretary of state for war, died on 16 July; his son Barbezieux succeeded him and the King wrote to Catinat himself. The court’s ciphered despatches to Catinat survived among the marshal’s papers and were printed in figures in the second volume of the 1819 Mémoires, pp. 295–342, with the clear passages the clerk had left in clear. Bazeries states that except for the 19 August letter, whose minute is at the Dépôt de la guerre, these despatches “n’ont pas été enregistrées”: if so, the 1819 print is the only text of the other six.*

02 The code and the decoding

The Grand Chiffre de 1691 is a two-part code of 587 groups: two or three homophones for each letter, consonant-vowel syllables, word stems (Envoi, Trouv, Expliqu), whole words (Sa Majesté, Pignerol, Carmagnole), a dozen nulls that mark the ends of sentences, and one group that cancels the group before it. Bazeries rebuilt it from these letters over three years and printed his table, with 91 entries left blank, at the end of Le Masque de fer; that table, transcribed from the Gallica scan for the Feuquières letter, is used here unchanged.

The figures were taken from the Bavarian State Library’s hOCR of the volume, word by word with their boxes. Specks the OCR read as a stray “1” (which would decode as Passage) were dropped by box size, show-through glyphs stripped, the editor’s footnote markers and running heads removed, and the letters cut at their printed headers. Each group was looked up, the units concatenated between nulls, and the result word-segmented with a lexicon drawn from the three Catinat volumes, Rousset’s Louvois, Feuquières’ memoirs and Bazeries’ book, so that sa maj este vou loit sca voi r comes out as sa majesté vouloit scavoir. The word division is an aid to the reader; the group-level decoding is what is checked.

Controls. Bazeries printed the 8 July and 19 August letters in clear. Our decoding of the OCR’d figures matches his text at 2,640 of 2,690 characters (98.1 %) and 3,204 of 3,233 (99.1 %) after normalising accents and i/j, u/v. The residue is single groups the OCR misread (26 Vous as 24 Le), a few places where his clear text departs from the printed figures, and his free rendering of two table entries. Every stretch that could not be divided into words, 137 in all, was then cropped from the page image and checked by eye: seven OCR errors were found and corrected, six groups turned out to be misprinted above 587 in the 1819 edition itself, and the rest were seventeenth-century spelling correctly transcribed.

03 What the King and Louvois wrote

Louvois, 9 July. The more the King reflects on Coni, the more he is touched, for the reputation of his arms and for what the place will cost the winter quarters; a siege with the whole army is no longer possible. Two tasks: fortify Carmagnole so the enemy “n’osent le regarder”, and destroy every post the enemy could hold in winter against the roads to Casale and Pignerol: Villeneuve d’Ast and Quiers “vous les devriez brusler absolument”, sparing the churches, and the castles between Turin and Asti likewise, all done “de manière qu’il ne paroisse point qu’elles vous aient esté prescrites”. Seize Bene; burn Carignan if the enemy could winter in it. Seek battle before the Germans arrive and avoid it after, to let them fall sick and desert for want of pay. Oblige the peasants to bring their grain into the King’s garrisons, with commissaries to hold the governors to it. The Duke of Savoy’s talk “mériteroit bien” two thousand bombs into Turin, and the bridge of Turin would be worth breaking, “mais ce sont des vues que Sa Majesté vous met devant les yeux et non des ordres”.

Louis XIV, 24 August. Chamlay carries the King’s thoughts on every course still open in the war. Will the enemy march into Savoy, or mass by Turin to eat the forage and force Catinat back over the mountains first? The negotiation at Rome makes it very desirable to stay beyond the Alps as long as possible. Casale: palisades, money for eighteen months, grain, more cavalry, gunners. Some at court think Villefranche fortified and Saluces, Savillan and Fossano garrisoned would let the army winter in Piedmont; the King does not tire of saying so, but Catinat is not to let the King’s wish override his judgement. Pignerol is “le premier objet” in any plan. And the King would greatly wish Coni taken at the close of the campaign.

Louis XIV, 29 August and 6 September. A treaty offered by the people of Mondovì is good but can only serve to sound them, since the army can no longer take quarters there. The resident at Geneva reports the enemy has asked the city for passage into Savoy and would go by the Valais into the Chablais. Larray has thrown an Irish battalion into Briançon for fear the Barbets would seize it. If Catinat crosses the Po, let it be before the enemy moves. Chamlay has arrived.

Louis XIV, 14 September, Fontainebleau. Chamlay has reported, and the King has seen “avec peine” that the upshot is to withdraw the army of Piedmont over the mountains at the end of the campaign and not to bring it back. He sets out both sides at length. Against withdrawal: the enemy masters of Italy, the allies and neutral princes forced to declare against him, Casale lost from view, the reputation of his arms damaged and his enemies encouraged by an appearance of weakness “quoyque fausse dans le fonds”. For it: the impossibility of subsisting in Piedmont without Turin; a country “fort estroit et extrêmement couvert”, cut by torrents that rise in hours and cannot be bridged; the distance of Casale; the sickness of the troops every campaign, with no hospitals to hold them; the enemy’s freedom to engage or refuse behind the Doire and the Po. Then Catinat’s plan for the defensive: the cols of Provence, Dauphiné and Savoy held, the heights of Saint-Pierre by Pignerol and of Suze occupied, cheaper subsistence, fewer sick, the surplus cavalry on the Saône ready to go to Germany, and the Duke of Savoy left to choose between keeping the allied army idle in his ruined country or sending it away and seeing the French return. “J’ay comparé les inconvénients et les avantages d’un parti avec ceux de l’autre … et je me suis déterminé à préférer le parti solide à l’honorable.”

The orders follow. Prolong the campaign to All Saints or even Saint-Martin, and keep Carmagnole to the last, because both cover the negotiation with the Pope and the Italian princes that might extricate him from Casale “et mesme peut-estre de la guerre de Piedmont”; the enemy must not learn that he means to quit Piedmont for good, so the works at Carmagnole go on, “avec un peu moins de vivacité et de dépense”. When the order comes, carry off what can be carried, the grain on the cavalry’s cruppers to Pignerol, throw the rest in the water, tear up the works, burn palisades, gates, hay and grain. Secure Pignerol’s magazines against a bombardment, which “me donneroit beaucoup de mortification en présence de mon armée”. Hold the valleys of Pragelas and Pérouse for the Briançon road. After recrossing, take Coni in the winter; the artillery and stores are ordered to Lyon and Grenoble. Fortify Briançon and the frontier against the valleys of Luzerne, Saint-Martin and Angrogne. Winter quarters by Catinat’s memoir. And retreat with such precaution that the enemy can attempt nothing.

04 What remains uncertain

  • Six groups stand above 587 in the 1819 print (654, 593, 855, 942, 854, 595) and one glyph on p. 326 is damaged; they are flagged and left without a value.
  • The OCR error rate measured on the two controls is about one group in seventy. Errors that break the French were caught by the image check; a substitution that leaves plausible French (24 for 26, 8 for 587) can survive at that rate, so a passage to be quoted should be checked against the page image; every group’s box is recorded.
  • About a hundred groups fall on entries Bazeries left blank and are decoded from context. Where his clear text of the 8 July letter departs from the figures as printed, either the 1819 compositor or his table is wrong, and only the originals at Vincennes can say which.
  • Whether the minutes of the five unpublished letters survive at the Service historique de la Défense, and whether their substance was already known to Rousset and Catinat’s biographers from other papers, is not checked.*

A Versailles le 8 juillet 1691. Monsieur, l'ordinaire m'a rendu les lettres que vous avez pris la peine de m'écrire le
[1er!] de ce mois, lesquelles le Roy a esté surpris de voir que vous n'ayez point envoié par un courrier exprés, les
mauvaises comme les bonnes nouvelle [?l] devant estre envoiées avec diligence. Sa Majesté me commende de vous repeter ce
que je vous ay desja mandé, qui est que Sa Majesté vouloit sçavoir par vous qui estoit l'autheur de la proposition de
l'attaque generale du dehors de Coni. Je vous suplie de me le mander au plustost; vous [?moins] es en estat de le sçavoir
presentement, puisque l'armée doit estre reunie.

Il n'est pas necessaire que je vous explique avec quel desplaisir Sa Majesté a appris le desordre avec lequel, contre
vostre ordre et sans necessité, Monsieur de Bulonde a pris le parti de lever le siege de Coni, puisque Sa Majesté, en
connoissant mieux que personne les consequences, connoist aussi combien est grand le prejudice que l'on recevra de
n'avoir pas pris cette place, dont il faudra tascher de se rendre maistre pendant l'hiver. Elle desire que vous fassiez
arrester Monsieur de Bulonde et le fassiez conduire à la citadelle de Pignerol, où Sa Majesté veut qu'il [?vo] gardé
enfermé pendant la nuit dans une chambre de ladite citadelle, et le jour, ayant la [?juge]berté de se promener sur les
remparts, avec un masque. Comme le gouverneur de Pignerol est à vos ordres, vous luy envoierez, s'il vous plaist,
ceux-ci pour [?dax]e[x]cution de la volonté de Sa Majesté.

Le Roy est en peine de Monsieur de Vins; et quoyque [?le] ne m'en ayez rien mandé, je suis persuadé que vous n'avez pas
oublié de luy mander par des courriers ce qu'il aura à faire, et que vous ne m'en ayez envoié copie en mesme temps. Je
doute pas que vous ne fassiez avancer les ouvrages de Carmagnole avec toute la diligence [?posrble], afin de pouvoir
vous esloigner de cette place sans en avoir d'inquietude. Sa Majesté demande ce que vous projettez de faire en vous
avançant à Poirin et à Quiers. Si vous ne croiez pas pouvoir garder Quiers et Villeneuve d'Ast pendant l'hiver, ne
conviendroit-il point de les destruire, pour empescher les ennemis de s'y mettre et de s'en servir à incommoder vos
quartiers? Si la mesme chose pouvoit estre pratiquée à Ast, vous en tireriez un grand avantage.

Vous devez estre informé presentement des ordres que le Roy cependant envoie à Monsieur de la Hoguette de vous envoier
quatre bataillons et quinse escadrons; dez que le Roy aura eu nouvelle [19?] du succés qu'aura eu Monsieur de la
[?rraya] Mirebouc, Sa Majesté luy fera passer des ordres de vous en envoier encore deux. Je vous ai marqué par mes
dernières les jours que les troupes de l'empereur et de M de Bavière sont parties d'auprès de Philisbourg. J'envoie
ordre au sieur du Pile de contremander les treise mille septiers de bled qu'il doit envoier à Nice, veu les six mille
qui y sont desja. Vous aurez apris par Monsieur d'Herleville les discours que Monsieur de Savoye a tenus depuis la
nouvelle qu'il a eue de la levée du siege de Coni sur les negociations de l'avocat Perraquin. Si vous pouvez envoier de
l'argent à Cazal, je vous prie que ce soit jusque à un million de livres. Vous pouvez garder les mulets du sieur Olri
autant que vous le jugerez à propos, mais aprés cela je vous suplie de les renvoier en Dauphiné.

Je suis, Monsieur, votre très humble et très affectionné serviteur. M. Louvois.

# L1, Louvois to Catinat, Versailles, 8 July 1691: English translation

At Versailles, 8 July 1691. Sir, the ordinary post has delivered to me the letters you took the trouble to write me on
the [1st!] of this month, about which the King was surprised to see that you did not send by an express courier the
bad as well as the good news [?], which must be sent with diligence. His Majesty orders me to repeat to you what I have
already told you, which is that His Majesty wished to learn from you who was the author of the proposal for the general
assault on the outworks of Cuneo. I beg you to let me know as soon as possible; you [?] are in a position to know it now,
since the army is to be reunited.

There is no need for me to explain to you with what displeasure His Majesty learned of the disorder with which, against
your orders and without necessity, Monsieur de Bulonde took the decision to raise the siege of Cuneo; for His Majesty,
knowing the consequences better than anyone, also knows how great is the harm that will come from not having taken
that place, which we shall have to try to master during the winter. He wishes you to have Monsieur de Bulonde arrested
and taken to the citadel of Pinerolo, where His Majesty wants him [?] kept shut up at night in a room of the said
citadel, and by day having the [?li]berty of walking on the ramparts, with a mask. As the governor of Pinerolo is under
your orders, you will please send him these [orders] for the [?execution] of His Majesty's will.

The King is anxious about Monsieur de Vins; and although [?you] have told me nothing of it, I am persuaded that you have
not forgotten to send him word by couriers of what he is to do, and that you have [not] sent me a copy at the same time.
I [do not] doubt that you are pushing on the works at Carmagnola with all [?possible] diligence, so as to be able to
move away from that place without anxiety about it. His Majesty asks what you plan to do in advancing to Poirino and
Chieri. If you do not think you can hold Chieri and Villanova d'Asti during the winter, would it not be advisable to
destroy them, to prevent the enemy from settling there and using them to harass your quarters? If the same thing could
be done at Asti, you would gain a great advantage from it.

You must by now be informed of the orders the King is meanwhile sending to Monsieur de la Hoguette to send you four
battalions and fifteen squadrons; as soon as the King has had news [19?] of the success Monsieur de la [?] Mirabouc has
had, His Majesty will send him orders to send you two more. I told you in my last the days on which the troops of the
Emperor and of the Elector of Bavaria left the neighbourhood of Philippsburg. I am sending orders to the Sieur du Pile
to countermand the thirteen thousand setiers of wheat he is to send to Nice, in view of the six thousand already there.
You will have learned from Monsieur d'Herleville the talk that Monsieur de Savoie has held, since the news he had of the
raising of the siege of Cuneo, about the negotiations of the advocate Perraquin. If you can send money to Casale, I beg
you that it be up to a million livres. You may keep the Sieur Olri's mules as long as you think fit, but after that I
beg you to send them back to Dauphiné.

I am, Sir, your very humble and very devoted servant. M. Louvois.

# L2, Louvois to Catinat, Versailles, 9 July 1691: English translation

At Versailles, 9 July 1691. Sir, the more the King reflects on what happened at Cuneo, the more His Majesty is affected
by it, not only for the reputation of his arms, but also in consideration of the obstacles that this place may put in
the way of establishing winter quarters in Piedmont; and His Majesty, who would have wished that you could have marched
back to that place to besiege it with the whole army, well knows that this is no longer possible at present. His Majesty
judges that you should apply yourself to two things: the one, to fortify Carmagnola so well that the enemy dare not look
at it; and the other, to destroy all the places the enemy might occupy during the winter that could disturb your
communication with Casale and with Pinerolo. Villanova d'Asti and Chieri seem to be of that kind: His Majesty would
think that, when marching to change the garrison of Casale, you should burn them completely, sparing the churches as far
as can be done. If there were castles that could either hem in your quarters or facilitate communication from Turin to
Asti, His Majesty would further think that, while you are master of the field, you should make them suffer the same
fate; and you must do this kind of thing in such a way that it does not appear that it was prescribed to you, but on the
contrary that you are doing it of yourself. It would also be useful to seize Bene, if it is practicable; and if Carignano
[?] could be occupied by the enemy during the winter, you should not hesitate to burn it. And as much as you should seek
a battle before the arrival of the Germans, so much should you avoid one after they have arrived, so as to give them time
to fall sick and waste away for want of pay [?]. When I say avoid, the King does not mean to prescribe that you retreat
before the enemy, but rather that you try to post yourself well and not hesitate to entrench, so that if the enemy wish
to attack you it is to their disadvantage. On all the above the King prescribes nothing to you; His Majesty only opens
views to you, on which you will do what your good sense and your devotion to his service inspire. On sending Monsieur
the Marquis de Vins back to the Provence frontier, you may dispose of the two [?battalions] of [?Montcas], putting them
[?] in garrison in the places behind, which will give you the means of strengthening yourself by as much. Only take
care that you must not put them into Pinerolo, where there are already two Irish battalions. I beg you to press the
engineers to send me a plan of Carmagnola and to explain to me on it the slopes of the waters. As the [?harvest] must be
about finished when you receive this letter, nothing is more important than to oblige the people to take refuge with
their wheat in the places where the King has a garrison; and this is of such importance that you and Monsieur Bouchu
should send commissaries there to see to it strictly, and to prevent the governors, on their own account, from
excusing the people from carrying out the orders that will be sent them for this purpose. I am not of the opinion that
you should oblige the people to deliver their wheat now, but only to take refuge with it in the places, where they will
be promised that it will not be taken without payment. I beg you to advise Monsieur Bouchu to have sent to me every week
statements of how much wheat comes in and at what price it sells in the markets. Monsieur d'Herleville will have told
you of Monsieur de Savoie's talk about the negotiations you have had with him; he would well deserve that, if you have
time, you should throw two thousand bombs into Turin. If it were possible to break the bridge of Turin, it [?would be] a
very useful thing; but, as I told you above, these are views that His Majesty puts before your eyes, not orders he gives
you. You [?may] count on its being the fifteenth of August before most of the German troops can enter Piedmont, that
they march there with infinite reluctance, and that a great number will desert. I do not [?once more] recommend you to
bring a great deal of forage into Carmagnola, because I [?] am persuaded that you know its importance. If the troops you
are send[ing] to Casale could pass Montafia, it could only be very proper that they should [243?] fire there, as well as
the garrison of Susa at Piossasco when it can push forward. I am, Sir, your very humble and very devoted servant, M.
LOUVOIS.

thinking of sending anyone towards the Val d'Aosta to try to penetrate into Savoy; that would be a diversion which,
though of small consequence, would not fail to be capable of embarrassing us.

I have received your two letters of the 13th of this month from the camp at Pancalieri, on which I should have many
things to tell you [?had I not] resolved to send you the Sieur de Chamlay, instructed in my intentions and in everything
regarding the continuation of the war in Piedmont, that in Savoy, and in a word whatever concerns my whole frontier. I
have taken this resolution, not being able to explain to you well enough in writing my thoughts on all that I believe
you should do, and in order to learn yours in still more detail than you can explain them to me in writing, although you
do so with great clarity and exactness. You may speak with confidence on everything to the said Sieur de Chamlay; you
will find him well instructed; and when you have talked with him for a few days, he can go back through the places where
the war will probably be waged next year, to report to me on the measures to be taken, both to prepare the places the
enemy may come to and to make preparations there so that the troops destined for them can live there. The said Sieur
Chamlay's journey will give me knowledge I do not have, and he will also gain some that may be useful in future.

wheat that is in Saluzzo, Savigliano and Fossano, and all the other grain, mine as well as [?] that of private persons,
which you will have deposited either at Carmagnola or at Pinerolo. I tell you my intentions on this only to gain a few
days in advance, the said Sieur de Chamlay being instructed in my intentions; he leaves tomorrow without fail, and will
probably arrive two or three days after this letter, which I am sending by an express courier.

I am pleased with the frankness with which you speak to me; continue to use it in the same way, and always tell me the
true state of things and what you think. You speak to me in one of your letters of the 13th [?] of the little forage you
find, and of your fear that the enemy may approach Pinerolo to ravage the surroundings of that town and ruin the
establishments made there. You will regulate yourself by the enemy's movements and do what you think best in the
matter. As I am sending you the Sieur de Chamlay, who, as I have already told you above, is fully instructed in my
intentions, which he will explain to you, it would be useless to say more. And this letter being for no other purpose,
I pray God, M. de Catinat, to keep you in His holy care. Written at Versailles, 19 August 1691.

# L4, Louis XIV to Catinat, 24 August 1691: English translation

LOUIS. 24 August 1691. M. Catinat, I have received by the last two couriers the three letters you wrote me from
Pancalieri dated the 20th of this month, for I believe the one dated the [16?] is so only by mistake. I shall not answer
in detail everything they contain, having charged the Sieur de Chamlay with explaining to you my thoughts on all the
courses we can take in the rest of this war. You will have examined well, the two of you [?] together, what is
practicable to do, knowing as you both know the state of my affairs, of my negotiations, that of the country and the
strength of the enemy. You will probably know, when you are together, what course they have taken: whether they have
marched into Savoy, or whether they have all gathered on the side of Turin to come back at you, or to oblige you, after
consuming all the forage, to withdraw first, cross the mountains and leave them entire freedom in all the country between
Turin and Pinerolo. I still contemplate with regret what will force you to leave that country. I am very glad to see,
in the letter in which you speak of the negotiation in Rome, that you will neglect nothing possible to lengthen the stay
of my army beyond the mountains.

I have ordered the Marquis de Barbezieux to write to the Marquis de Crenan to have palisades planted round the town and
citadel of Casale and to do everything he thinks necessary to defend the place well; and I have ordered at the same time
that money be sent there, so that there should be enough for at least a year and a half. The Marquis de Crenan also has
orders to prepare the wheat, grain and other things necessary for the same period. It would be desirable that the
garrison were stronger, above all in cavalry, so that it could harass the enemy and make itself more respected than it
is. I should also much like there to be more artillery officers and gunners; but I do not know how to get them in; and
I have had the Marquis de Crenan told to train soldiers to handle the artillery well.

From what you tell me of Carmagnola, I no longer see you so resolved to abandon it since you saw the Sieur de Chamlay's
memorandum. I wish that you [?would resolve] together to keep it, and that it be possible to do so. What I tell you will
not change [?] what the Sieur de Chamlay is to tell you on my behalf. I do not think I tell you anything new when I say
that there are [654!] here who believe that if one could fortify Villafranca, put a large body of infantry into it, and
fill Saluzzo, Savigliano and Fossano with troops, one could remain in Piedmont; you will see with the Sieur de Chamlay
what can be done on this proposal. I do not tire of speaking to you of what might keep my troops in Piedmont; [but] the
wish I have for it must not make you pass over what you think will be most useful for the good of my service. Memoranda
have been sent to the Sieur Peletier de Souzy from which it appears that work is going on hard and fast on the
fortifications of Carmagnola; this makes me think that it could be kept. Neglect nothing for that, if we are fortunate
enough for it to be possible; have whatever you think necessary put into it.

There is nothing I wish so much as the preservation of Pinerolo, whatever course we take. That must be the first object;
and when you can, I shall be very glad for you to examine carefully everything you think can be done [593!] relieve me of
the anxiety into which the mere thought of its siege puts me. If we are fortunate [507?] enough not to leave Piedmont,
which I much doubt, that [?] [16?] remove entirely; if on the contrary we put ourselves on the defensive next year, then
by dint of precautions and work in the places you think necessary, and of infantry, the enemy must be prevented from even
thinking of it: that side well guarded, that of Susa in the state in which you say [855!] will have to put it, the passes
of Savoy furnished with troops, and the small towns of Dauphiné and Provence well closed. I believe the enemy will be
much embarrassed.

What you propose for the stages of the Maurienne and the Tarentaise seems to me well thought out, and that, since there
is no more cavalry to pass, the forage must be removed. I am ordering the Marquis de Barbezieux to write that people are
to conform on this to what you send word of. I am waiting for the Sieur de Chamlay to return before deciding on
everything. As soon as I have taken my final resolution, I shall give all my orders so that everything may be in the
state I could wish for next campaign. [?I] would much like you to be able to take Cuneo at the end of the campaign.
Nothing would be so good for the good of my service, for all the reasons you give me. If you think you can do it, think
of the preparations to be made, both for the artillery and for the provisions.

# L5, Louis XIV to Catinat, 29 August 1691: English translation

seems to me that we are no longer in a position to [?march] in that direction. It can therefore be good at present only
for letting us know the feelings of those people, so as to profit by them when the occasion arises. If you think that,
as things stand, there is something more to be done, I [?leave it to you] to say and conclude whatever you think will be
most suitable for my service. ⟨By the other letter you tell me it is said that the march the enemy has made towards
Savoy is to try to oblige you to make some movement. I find it hard to believe they can undertake anything without
making some preparations, and you tell me there are none. The rumour on all sides is that they mean to enter Savoy,⟩ and
the resident I have at Geneva writes that he has been told they are resolved [?] [?] [?] the passage for their troops to
those of the said town, and that it will surely not be granted them. ⟨He also writes that they would like to pass
through the Valais to enter the Chablais. He likewise says he has warned the Sieur de la Hoguette to be on his guard. I
do not doubt that you now know⟩ [?]. It would be unfortunate if the enemy entered Savoy and put us, at the end of the

# L6, Louis XIV to Catinat, Versailles, 6 September 1691: English translation

⟨LOUIS. 6 September 1691.⟩ If I see fit to send someone to Casale this winter, there is no other way than to have him
pass through Genoa, and even that is not very safe. I am not sorry about this resolution, for it pained me to see so few
troops as there were to defend those passes. The Marquis de la Rraya writes to the Marquis de Barbezieux that he had sent
an Irish battalion to Briançon, fearing that the barbets might think of making themselves masters of that town, and that
he had told Bachevilliers to redouble his care. ⟨I am very glad of what you tell me, that you are pleased with the
conduct of the Prince d'Elbeuf; what I wrote you and what I had written to him can do no harm for the future, since it
will oblige him to do still better than he has done up to now.⟩ If you must cross the Po, the resolution you have taken
is very good [?], to do it before the enemy [?] have set out to approach you. You know everything that can be foreseen of
the courses the enemy may take, and I [?] that you will conduct yourself so that, if they approached Pinerolo, [you]
would be in a position to disturb whatever plans they might have. You do well, at all events, to have the sick [942!] [?]
withdrawn to the abbey near Pinerolo. ⟨I have since received by the ordinary post your letter of the [28?] of last month,
