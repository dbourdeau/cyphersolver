Barbavara and Pusterla to Sforza, 1447

The letters
Marcolino Barbavara, ducal secretary, from Rimini and Rome (13 February – 4 May 1447), and Pietro Pusterla, the Duke of Milan’s procurator, from Gualdo, Ferrara and Milan (21 January – 10 March 1447), to Francesco Sforza.
Where
Bibliothèque nationale de France, italien 1584 (Archivio Sforzesco papers of 1447), on Gallica. Leads 1313 and 1314 of the catalogue sweep, now closed.
The cipher
One key for both men, copied in the Milan archive: a homophonic alphabet, signs for vowel–consonant syllables, signs for common words, nulls and a dozen code signs for names (DECODE R1857).
How the text was obtained
Every cipher letter has a decipherment: a later clear copy filed beside it (fourteen letters), or fifteenth-century glosses. The copies were transcribed and translated, checked against the key, and their blanks filled.
What it says
The money for Sforza’s condotta, the death of Eugenius IV and the election of Nicholas V, King Alfonso’s 94,000 ducats, and the Venetian army at Cremona. The King says priests are “homini da bastonate, non da pregare”.
Still open
Five cipher runs of the 4 May letter, which have no gloss and fall in blotted cells of the key. Five rare code signs, each used once to three times; a sixth, read as Micheletto Attendolo, rests on context. The cipher original of 6 February is not on the film.

01 The letters

In the winter of 1447 Francesco Sforza, at Pesaro, was being drawn back into the service of his father-in-law Filippo Maria Visconti, Duke of Milan, against Venice. Pietro Pusterla negotiated and sealed the capitoli in January and then chased the money for Sforza’s condotta between Milan and Ferrara. Marcolino Barbavara, a ducal secretary, went to Rome and to King Alfonso of Aragon for the same money. He arrived as Eugenius IV died and stayed through the conclave that elected Nicholas V on 6 March. Both wrote to Sforza in cipher, in the same key.

BnF italien 1584 is one of the volumes of the Sforza papers in Paris. Mazzatinti’s inventory of 1887 marks the letters “in cifre” and notes a translation for only two of them. The project’s sweep of the volume turned them into two leads: Barbavara’s six letters (1314) and Pusterla’s eight (1313).

Opening every leaf showed what the inventory does not say. Beside each cipher letter a later archive hand, in a regular italic that looks eighteenth-century, filed a complete clear copy, usually on the leaf before it. Every letter is covered this way or by a decipherment of the time:

02 The key

The key is in Milan, in an eighteenth-century copy among the Archivio di Stato’s Potenze sovrane cipher sheets. DECODE has it as record R1857, image 11, under the heading “Marcolinus de Barbavara et Symon Arrigonus — Petrus de Pusterla”. Satoshi Tomokiyo noted in 2024 that it is the key of the f. 69 letter. It has these parts:

  • an alphabet of two to four signs for each letter (o-, —o for a; fo, ɱ for s);
  • a table of signs for vowel–consonant syllables, ab to uz (per is or, d÷o is in);
  • signs for common words: che, con, per, non, qua, quando, que, perché, quanto, se, ciò, come;
  • nulls (“insignificanti”);
  • code signs for the Signori Malatesti, the King, money, the Count of Urbino, the Duca di Ferrara, V.S., the Pope, the Camerlengo, the Duke, the Venetians and qui.

Several cells are blotted, and three code signs carry no label.

Spot checks against the cipher agree with the copies. The first line of f. 69 decodes in questa hora (the copy: “In questa hora decima septima”). The first line of f. 40 decodes se lo c… (“Se lo cavalario”). The clear words left in the cipher of f. 75 recur in the same order in its copy. The copied letters were not transcribed sign by sign: the copies are complete texts. The project’s table of the code signs is key_R1857_codes.tsv; the key image itself is the archive’s and is not reproduced.

03 The copyist’s blanks

04 What the letters say

Pusterla. On 21 January he is at Gualdo, waiting for word where to meet Sforza. At Ferrara on 6 February the Duca di Ferrara is suspicious for lack of news. Pusterla gives Barbavara, who is going to the Pope and the King about the money and has no cipher, “la copia de la mia”. In Milan on 14 February the Duke receives him gladly but will not let him go back without money. Back at Ferrara (6–10 March) he presses the Duca di Ferrara to pay, in vain. He has 20,000 ducats waiting for a messenger who never comes, and reports the Venetian army at Cremona crying “viva San Marco”, the move on Soncino and the Count of Urbino’s negotiations.

Barbavara. From Rimini (13 February) he arranges a safe-conduct and his road. In Rome (27 February) he finds Eugenius IV dead. The Camerlengo will wait for the next pope, and Capua and Fermo are tipped. On 3 March he reports that King Alfonso will pay Sforza’s condotta, 94,000 ducats a year for two years, on a schedule that counts 20,000 from the Duca di Ferrara. On 4 March the cardinals go into conclave. On 6 March he writes:

In questa hora decima septima, o circha, è publicato Papa Monsignore da Cologna. Credo firmamente che serà amico…
At this seventeenth hour or about, Monsignor of “Cologna” has been proclaimed Pope. I firmly believe he will be a friend…

On 8 March Barbavara has been at the new pope’s feet. The pope sends the Camerlengo and the Vice-Chancellor to Alfonso to talk of peace, and the King answers that unless they first provide money and troops, he will make them pay: priests are “homini da bastonate, non da pregare”. 05 The two glossed letters

Barbavara’s undated f. 135 and his letter of 4 May with Simone Arrigoni (f. 143), whose names head the key, are written mostly in clear with runs of cipher. A fifteenth-century hand wrote decipherments above the runs, but only in part: words such as “li S. Malatesti”, “la Maiestà”, “del Re”, “con loro”. The runs are deciphered here with the key, the glosses serving as a check.

f. 135 (undated, late April) has four runs. All read in sense: Barbavara, having heard what the Duke writes “circha li facti de Roberto da Montalboddo” and what the King said, thinks his “restare de qua” is no longer needed and asks to be told at once. f. 143 (4 May, Barbavara and Arrigoni) has 51 runs, on the Malatesta coming over to the King, the bulls for their vicariates, the new pope stalling, and 4,000 ducats agreed with the Count of Urbino’s ambassadors:

  • 9 runs come out of the key alone and agree with the gloss.
  • 31 are read from key and gloss together.
  • 6 rest on the gloss alone.
  • 5 are unread: they have no gloss and their signs fall in blotted cells of the key copy.

The glosses give some values the late key copy does not, among them ϵg = s and fto = ap (in papa), and a few signs read as nulls. These are a first pass, to be checked sign by sign before they are used to correct the key.

06 What remains open

  • Five runs of f. 143 (143.17, .31, .35, .46, .51) and words inside two others: no gloss, and blotted cells on the only key copy.
  • Code signs: ∠ (three blanks), A (one), and three signs on f. 41 (one each). They are unlabelled on R1857 or absent from it.
  • The Z-like sign as Micheletto Attendolo rests on context in three places.
  • “Cologna” on f. 68 is not compared with the cipher.
  • The cipher original of 6 February (copy f. 27) is not on the microfilm. The f. 33 copy has no date line; its date comes from a later margin note.

Most illustrious Prince and excellent Lord, humble and devout recommendation. To carry out what Your Lordship charged me with at my departure, I inform you that today I arrived here, and tomorrow I shall set out towards Montefiascone [?], and there I shall wait until Wednesday morning to be informed by Your Lordship of your whereabouts, and where I am to come; not having anything else by Wednesday morning, I shall set out towards Monte Guiduzo, and from there I shall not leave until I am advised by Your Lordship of what I am to do; and I earnestly beg you to be pleased to send me a guide who is to guide me in your way. Further, I inform Your Lordship that Antonio da Trezzo is in company with me, so may it please you now to trust us [?], if you wish, that we come together or separately, because we shall all keep the order that will be given by Your Lordship, to whom I always humbly commend myself.

Given at Gualdo, 21 January 1447.

Of Your Excellent Lordship the humble servant P. de Pusterla

To the illustrious Lord Francesco Sforza, etc.

## f. 14 = clear copy of f. 13 (Gualdo, 21 Jan 1447)
Most illustrious Prince and excellent Lord, humble and devout recommendation. To carry out what Your Lordship charged me with at my departure, I inform you that today I arrived here, and tomorrow I shall set out towards Montefiascone [?], and there I shall wait until Wednesday morning to be informed by Your Lordship of your whereabouts, and where I am to come; not having anything else by Wednesday morning, I shall set out towards Monte Guiduzo, and from there I shall not leave until I am advised by Your Lordship of what I am to do; and I earnestly beg you to be pleased to send me a guide who is to guide me in your way. Further, I inform Your Lordship that Antonio da Trezzo is in company with me, so may it please you now to trust us [?], if you wish, that we come together or separately, because we shall all keep the order that will be given by Your Lordship, to whom I always humbly commend myself.

Given at Gualdo, 21 January 1447.

Of Your Excellent Lordship the humble servant P. de Pusterla

## f. 27 = clear copy (Ferrara, 6 Feb 1447; cipher original not located)
Illustrious and excellent Lord, humble and devout recommendation. This morning I arrived here, and at once I was with [gap], whom I found not without suspicion, for not having heard for days past from anywhere at what stage our affairs stand, etc. I used with him all the good words that were possible for me to remove his suspicion, without however revealing to him anything done, but I assured him of your good disposition, and urged him to be willing to come to the conclusion with [gap] soon; by means of which conclusion, whenever it should take place, I was certain Your Lordship would agree to everything that would please my lord, and what would be useful to the same [gap]; and so he suspected, indeed he was certain, that if His Lordship did not provide for the said [gap], the matter would come to no good end, and to the danger that Your Lordship might become vexed with the negotiation, etc. He answered me plenty of good words, which however are not of such effect that one can build great foundations on them; still he has charged me to carry some messages to the Duke on his behalf, showing himself well disposed. I do not know now what effect will follow, but I shall inform Your Lordship of everything. Marcolino Barbavara is going to His Holiness the Pope, and also to His Majesty the King, for the money business, etc., and because [?] what he is to do there about this will be laid on him by Your Lordship, and he not knowing how to write to you, having no cipher, I have given him the copy of mine, so that he can write, and so that no scandal may arise. Tomorrow I shall set out towards Milan, and I shall strive with all diligence to carry out what Your Lordship has charged me with, to whom I always commend myself.

Given at Ferrara, 6 February 1447.

Humble servant Pietro de Pusterla

## f. 48r-v = f. 49 (Rome, 27 Feb 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Having arrived here I found the Pope had passed from this life, as you
will have heard, and he has indeed wished to do as Your Lordship used to say: to die so as to do harm not only living
but also dying. I have been with the Camerlengo, who, having heard the reason for my coming, says that in effect it is
necessary to wait for the election of another Pope and to treat the matter with His Holiness, and that meanwhile I can
go to His Majesty the King to tighten the matter with him; and so I shall do, and Your Lordship will be told what
follows. The said Camerlengo received very gratefully all I told him on your behalf, and from the words he used with
me he seems to me very willing to have and keep good friendship with Your Lordship. He charged me to write to you to be
of good heart, because whoever is elected to the papacy will gladly do things that please you. He says too that he will
be a friend of the lord your father, or at least not his enemy, but rather believes he will be his friend and
favourable, and God grant it be so. It is thought that the election must fall either to Monsignor of Capua or to
Monsignor of Fermo; yet it could be that it falls to others, because it seems to me it always falls to the one no one
has commonly thought of. If Your Excellency made some show and demonstration of wanting to advance into the Marche or
elsewhere, so that news of it came here, I believe it would be very useful to warm the minds of these priests and make
them hotter to contribute to your expenses; and I say this because they have built an almost certain foundation that
Your Lordship will in any case go to the favour and help of the said lord your father. That opinion, however, I have
striven to get out of their heads, and said that such a foundation is vain, and that, though you are well disposed, this
good disposition must be helped otherwise than with words, that is with money. But to take it from them still [gap?].
Rome, 27 February. Your Excellency's little servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## ff. 55r-v, 57r = f. 56 (Rome, 3 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. I have been to His Majesty the King, and with such reasons as came to
mind I urged and begged him, in the name of my lord, to take all the greater care and burden of contributing to your
expenses as the need is greater through the death of the Pope, from whom no subsidy is to be expected any more. In
effect His Serenity has been content not only to pay what the Pope was to pay, but also to contribute a greater sum
than had first been discussed, namely ninety-four thousand ducats of gold, in gold, every year and for two years, as
the said lord wrote that he was content with two years. He will pay the said money in this way: twenty thousand ducats
this month, and another twenty thousand in the coming month of April at the latest, and the rest half at the end of six
months from the day of your engagement and the other half at the end of the year, and this for the first year; but for
the second year he is content to pay forty thousand ducats by the whole of March of that year, and the other 54
thousand from six months to six months as said above. And so His Serenity has promised, by articles signed and
concluded with him, sealed with his seal and also signed in his own hand; and he is to have the said money paid out at
the due times to whatever messenger of the said lord, or of yours, holds a sufficient mandate for it. On the lord's
part he has been promised that you will make no attacks on his men; rather, if need be, the said lord will send to his
aid either as many of your men, or of the other ducal troops, as amounts to the expense he bears for your condotta; and
he has also been promised that what the Pope pays, once he is elected, shall come to His Majesty. Of all this I inform
Your Excellency so that you know what has been done, and in good faith I recall two things. The first: that either His
Excellency the lord or you provide someone with a sufficient mandate to receive the money and to acknowledge receiving

use too much diligence. The second: it seems to me necessary to make clear to His Majesty as soon as possible that the

Majesty the King remains bound to pay the said sum of ninety-four thousand ducats every year. Had I been able to do
better, which I strove for as far as was possible, I would gladly have done it; but not being able, I accepted what I
could obtain. As for the election of the Pope, I wrote to you in other letters what I had heard; now I have nothing
else to add, except that everything will be done to have one who is a friend, and if he will not be, they will have

## f. 74r-v = f. 75 (Rome, 8 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Since I wrote to you of the election of the new Pope, I was, on the very
day of the election, so as to lose no time, at the feet of His Holiness, and after some general words rejoicing in the
name of the Duke at his election I touched also on the matter of money for Your Lordship. In effect it seems to me that
His Holiness together with the cardinals has resolved to procure peace in Italy, and for this reason to send the
Camerlengo and the Vice-Chancellor together to His Majesty the King; they left today, but their plans will not succeed,
because the King, whom I have seen again for this reason, means to make them understand very well, and especially the
Camerlengo (because the other is a Venetian), that these words of peace do not please him unless they first make
provision of money for Your Lordship, and of troops to send together with His Majesty, so that by this way the

terms of peace, or, not wanting it out of love, can be brought to it by force. His Majesty resolves also, if this
speech does not move the Pope and the cardinals to some better and more agreeable resolution, to declare to them that
His Majesty means to take on himself the burden of paying your expenses, and to tell them to be sure he will make them
pay it all, and if even this does not help, he shows he will use other than words, that is, come to deeds, saying that

If the courier has not kept the ways Your Lordship would have wished, it came about because, those of the [gap] being

## f. 48r-v = f. 49 (Rome, 27 Feb 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Having arrived here I found the Pope had passed from this life, as you
will have heard, and he has indeed wished to do as Your Lordship used to say: to die so as to do harm not only living
but also dying. I have been with the Camerlengo, who, having heard the reason for my coming, says that in effect it is
necessary to wait for the election of another Pope and to treat the matter with His Holiness, and that meanwhile I can
go to His Majesty the King to tighten the matter with him; and so I shall do, and Your Lordship will be told what
follows. The said Camerlengo received very gratefully all I told him on your behalf, and from the words he used with
me he seems to me very willing to have and keep good friendship with Your Lordship. He charged me to write to you to be
of good heart, because whoever is elected to the papacy will gladly do things that please you. He says too that he will
be a friend of the lord your father, or at least not his enemy, but rather believes he will be his friend and
favourable, and God grant it be so. It is thought that the election must fall either to Monsignor of Capua or to
Monsignor of Fermo; yet it could be that it falls to others, because it seems to me it always falls to the one no one
has commonly thought of. If Your Excellency made some show and demonstration of wanting to advance into the Marche or
elsewhere, so that news of it came here, I believe it would be very useful to warm the minds of these priests and make
them hotter to contribute to your expenses; and I say this because they have built an almost certain foundation that
Your Lordship will in any case go to the favour and help of the said lord your father. That opinion, however, I have
striven to get out of their heads, and said that such a foundation is vain, and that, though you are well disposed, this
good disposition must be helped otherwise than with words, that is with money. But to take it from them still [gap?].
Rome, 27 February. Your Excellency's little servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## ff. 55r-v, 57r = f. 56 (Rome, 3 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. I have been to His Majesty the King, and with such reasons as came to
mind I urged and begged him, in the name of my lord, to take all the greater care and burden of contributing to your
expenses as the need is greater through the death of the Pope, from whom no subsidy is to be expected any more. In
effect His Serenity has been content not only to pay what the Pope was to pay, but also to contribute a greater sum
than had first been discussed, namely ninety-four thousand ducats of gold, in gold, every year and for two years, as
the said lord wrote that he was content with two years. He will pay the said money in this way: twenty thousand ducats
this month, and another twenty thousand in the coming month of April at the latest, and the rest half at the end of six
months from the day of your engagement and the other half at the end of the year, and this for the first year; but for
the second year he is content to pay forty thousand ducats by the whole of March of that year, and the other 54
thousand from six months to six months as said above. And so His Serenity has promised, by articles signed and
concluded with him, sealed with his seal and also signed in his own hand; and he is to have the said money paid out at
the due times to whatever messenger of the said lord, or of yours, holds a sufficient mandate for it. On the lord's
part he has been promised that you will make no attacks on his men; rather, if need be, the said lord will send to his
aid either as many of your men, or of the other ducal troops, as amounts to the expense he bears for your condotta; and
he has also been promised that what the Pope pays, once he is elected, shall come to His Majesty. Of all this I inform
Your Excellency so that you know what has been done, and in good faith I recall two things. The first: that either His
Excellency the lord or you provide someone with a sufficient mandate to receive the money and to acknowledge receiving
it, and also to press for it with His Majesty the King, because you know well that where money is concerned one cannot
use too much diligence. The second: it seems to me necessary to make clear to His Majesty as soon as possible that the
conclusion has been made with him, so that he cannot excuse himself that, not having been made certain, he has not
provided the money. I have indeed given him good hope of the conclusion according to what Your Lordship told me, but I
do not think that is enough, and from my side I would not go on to give him any other assurance, having nothing else
either from the lord or from Your Lordship, who may write to me your intention about this, and I shall carry it out
without waiting for anything else. I inform Your Excellency also that His Majesty the King understands that in the said
sum of ninety-four thousand ducats which he is to pay are included the 20 thousand of the [gap: the lord of Ferrara?];
but he takes on himself the burden of making him pay them, and soon. For this he will at once send one of his men, and
when they are paid they will not be counted in the said 40 thousand ducats to be paid in March and April, but in the
others to be paid from six months to six months; and even if the [gap: lord of Ferrara?] paid no part of them, His
Majesty the King remains bound to pay the said sum of ninety-four thousand ducats every year. Had I been able to do
better, which I strove for as far as was possible, I would gladly have done it; but not being able, I accepted what I
could obtain. As for the election of the Pope, I wrote to you in other letters what I had heard; now I have nothing
else to add, except that everything will be done to have one who is a friend, and if he will not be, they will have
ways to make him repent. I commend myself to Your Lordship and also to the illustrious Madonna Bianca. Rome, 3 March.
Devoted servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## f. 61r = f. 62 (Rome, 4 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Replying briefly to what Your Excellency writes to me in letters of the
twenty-seventh of last month, I say that the ducal courier whom you had escorted brought me no letter but yours; I hear
indeed that he brought others to the Bishop of Novara and to Messer Pietro of Monferrato, and also to Simone Arrigoni,
servant of the lord your father, who has stayed in this place many months for His Lordship. I say also that by three
letters of mine I have informed Your Lordship of the death of the Pope and of what happened to me about it; nor has
anything new happened since, except that the cardinals entered the conclave this evening, and it is hoped they will
very soon elect another Pope, and opinions are very divided as to whom the election should fall to: God grant it be a
good one. Likewise I informed you by other letters of the conclusion made with His Majesty the King. Both the first and
the second letters I sent into the hands of the Magnificent Madonna Aura, as Your Lordship told me, and by sure
messengers, and the said letters should have arrived safely. From now on I shall direct such letters to Messer Angelo
in Urbino, as you write. And I shall continually inform Your Lordship of things as they happen. Now I send you, enclosed
with these, certain letters of His Majesty the King, by which you will understand his good disposition; informing Your
Lordship that he heard very gladly what I told him on your behalf, and above all he relished what you said of having
turned your mind from the affairs of the Kingdom, and showed a good will to have you wholly his. I commend myself to
Your Lordship. Rome, 4 March 1447, by Your Lordship's devoted servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the illustrious lord
Francesco Sforza.

## f. 68 = f. 69 (Rome, 6 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. At this seventeenth hour or about, Monsignor of "Cologna" [so the copy;
Tommaso Parentucelli, cardinal of Bologna] has been proclaimed Pope. I firmly believe he will be a friend, and I shall
soon strive to see proof of it in the matters entrusted to me. Of what follows Your Lordship will be informed; and you
may tell me whether it seems to you that one way rather than another should be kept by me. I commend myself to Your
Excellency. Rome, 6 March 1447, by Your Lordship's servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the illustrious lord Francesco
Sforza.

## f. 74r-v = f. 75 (Rome, 8 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Since I wrote to you of the election of the new Pope, I was, on the very
day of the election, so as to lose no time, at the feet of His Holiness, and after some general words rejoicing in the
name of the Duke at his election I touched also on the matter of money for Your Lordship. In effect it seems to me that
His Holiness together with the cardinals has resolved to procure peace in Italy, and for this reason to send the
Camerlengo and the Vice-Chancellor together to His Majesty the King; they left today, but their plans will not succeed,
because the King, whom I have seen again for this reason, means to make them understand very well, and especially the
Camerlengo (because the other is a Venetian), that these words of peace do not please him unless they first make
provision of money for Your Lordship, and of troops to send together with His Majesty, so that by this way the
security of the state of the said Duke is provided for, and the enemies, fearing the provisions, are brought to better
terms of peace, or, not wanting it out of love, can be brought to it by force. His Majesty resolves also, if this
speech does not move the Pope and the cardinals to some better and more agreeable resolution, to declare to them that
His Majesty means to take on himself the burden of paying your expenses, and to tell them to be sure he will make them
pay it all, and if even this does not help, he shows he will use other than words, that is, come to deeds, saying that
priests are men to be beaten, not begged. I do not know what will follow; still I am not yet without hope that the Pope
and the cardinals, understanding the King's discontent, may be moved to take a better resolution; of what follows Your
Lordship will be informed. And perhaps, as I have written elsewhere, nothing else will do but that Your Lordship make
some demonstrations, to frighten the company still more and bring it to what we desire. I reminded His Majesty the King
again of the matter of the money for Your Lordship, and he answered that without fail he will give such orders that it
will not fail you at the terms, and likewise he said that this week he will send his man, whom he is sending to the lord
[gap: of Ferrara?] for those twenty thousand ducats. I commend myself to Your Excellency, and I marvel that I have
never had from you either letter or answer. Rome, 8 March 1447, by your devoted servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the
illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

Marcolino Barbavara, Rome, 3 Mar 1447 (cipher f. 56; copy ff. 55r-v, 57r)

If the courier has not kept the ways Your Lordship would have wished, it came about because, those of the [gap] being

## f. 48r-v = f. 49 (Rome, 27 Feb 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Having arrived here I found the Pope had passed from this life, as you
will have heard, and he has indeed wished to do as Your Lordship used to say: to die so as to do harm not only living
but also dying. I have been with the Camerlengo, who, having heard the reason for my coming, says that in effect it is
necessary to wait for the election of another Pope and to treat the matter with His Holiness, and that meanwhile I can
go to His Majesty the King to tighten the matter with him; and so I shall do, and Your Lordship will be told what
follows. The said Camerlengo received very gratefully all I told him on your behalf, and from the words he used with
me he seems to me very willing to have and keep good friendship with Your Lordship. He charged me to write to you to be
of good heart, because whoever is elected to the papacy will gladly do things that please you. He says too that he will
be a friend of the lord your father, or at least not his enemy, but rather believes he will be his friend and
favourable, and God grant it be so. It is thought that the election must fall either to Monsignor of Capua or to
Monsignor of Fermo; yet it could be that it falls to others, because it seems to me it always falls to the one no one
has commonly thought of. If Your Excellency made some show and demonstration of wanting to advance into the Marche or
elsewhere, so that news of it came here, I believe it would be very useful to warm the minds of these priests and make
them hotter to contribute to your expenses; and I say this because they have built an almost certain foundation that
Your Lordship will in any case go to the favour and help of the said lord your father. That opinion, however, I have
striven to get out of their heads, and said that such a foundation is vain, and that, though you are well disposed, this
good disposition must be helped otherwise than with words, that is with money. But to take it from them still [gap?].
Rome, 27 February. Your Excellency's little servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## ff. 55r-v, 57r = f. 56 (Rome, 3 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. I have been to His Majesty the King, and with such reasons as came to
mind I urged and begged him, in the name of my lord, to take all the greater care and burden of contributing to your
expenses as the need is greater through the death of the Pope, from whom no subsidy is to be expected any more. In
effect His Serenity has been content not only to pay what the Pope was to pay, but also to contribute a greater sum
than had first been discussed, namely ninety-four thousand ducats of gold, in gold, every year and for two years, as
the said lord wrote that he was content with two years. He will pay the said money in this way: twenty thousand ducats
this month, and another twenty thousand in the coming month of April at the latest, and the rest half at the end of six
months from the day of your engagement and the other half at the end of the year, and this for the first year; but for
the second year he is content to pay forty thousand ducats by the whole of March of that year, and the other 54
thousand from six months to six months as said above. And so His Serenity has promised, by articles signed and
concluded with him, sealed with his seal and also signed in his own hand; and he is to have the said money paid out at
the due times to whatever messenger of the said lord, or of yours, holds a sufficient mandate for it. On the lord's
part he has been promised that you will make no attacks on his men; rather, if need be, the said lord will send to his
aid either as many of your men, or of the other ducal troops, as amounts to the expense he bears for your condotta; and
he has also been promised that what the Pope pays, once he is elected, shall come to His Majesty. Of all this I inform
Your Excellency so that you know what has been done, and in good faith I recall two things. The first: that either His
Excellency the lord or you provide someone with a sufficient mandate to receive the money and to acknowledge receiving
it, and also to press for it with His Majesty the King, because you know well that where money is concerned one cannot
use too much diligence. The second: it seems to me necessary to make clear to His Majesty as soon as possible that the
conclusion has been made with him, so that he cannot excuse himself that, not having been made certain, he has not
provided the money. I have indeed given him good hope of the conclusion according to what Your Lordship told me, but I
do not think that is enough, and from my side I would not go on to give him any other assurance, having nothing else
either from the lord or from Your Lordship, who may write to me your intention about this, and I shall carry it out
without waiting for anything else. I inform Your Excellency also that His Majesty the King understands that in the said
sum of ninety-four thousand ducats which he is to pay are included the 20 thousand of the [gap: the lord of Ferrara?];
but he takes on himself the burden of making him pay them, and soon. For this he will at once send one of his men, and
when they are paid they will not be counted in the said 40 thousand ducats to be paid in March and April, but in the
others to be paid from six months to six months; and even if the [gap: lord of Ferrara?] paid no part of them, His
Majesty the King remains bound to pay the said sum of ninety-four thousand ducats every year. Had I been able to do
better, which I strove for as far as was possible, I would gladly have done it; but not being able, I accepted what I
could obtain. As for the election of the Pope, I wrote to you in other letters what I had heard; now I have nothing
else to add, except that everything will be done to have one who is a friend, and if he will not be, they will have
ways to make him repent. I commend myself to Your Lordship and also to the illustrious Madonna Bianca. Rome, 3 March.
Devoted servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## f. 61r = f. 62 (Rome, 4 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Replying briefly to what Your Excellency writes to me in letters of the
twenty-seventh of last month, I say that the ducal courier whom you had escorted brought me no letter but yours; I hear
indeed that he brought others to the Bishop of Novara and to Messer Pietro of Monferrato, and also to Simone Arrigoni,
servant of the lord your father, who has stayed in this place many months for His Lordship. I say also that by three

very soon elect another Pope, and opinions are very divided as to whom the election should fall to: God grant it be a
good one. Likewise I informed you by other letters of the conclusion made with His Majesty the King. Both the first and
the second letters I sent into the hands of the Magnificent Madonna Aura, as Your Lordship told me, and by sure
messengers, and the said letters should have arrived safely. From now on I shall direct such letters to Messer Angelo
in Urbino, as you write. And I shall continually inform Your Lordship of things as they happen. Now I send you, enclosed
with these, certain letters of His Majesty the King, by which you will understand his good disposition; informing Your
Lordship that he heard very gladly what I told him on your behalf, and above all he relished what you said of having
turned your mind from the affairs of the Kingdom, and showed a good will to have you wholly his. I commend myself to
Your Lordship. Rome, 4 March 1447, by Your Lordship's devoted servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the illustrious lord
Francesco Sforza.

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. At this seventeenth hour or about, Monsignor of "Cologna" [so the copy;

soon strive to see proof of it in the matters entrusted to me. Of what follows Your Lordship will be informed; and you

## f. 74r-v = f. 75 (Rome, 8 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Since I wrote to you of the election of the new Pope, I was, on the very
day of the election, so as to lose no time, at the feet of His Holiness, and after some general words rejoicing in the
name of the Duke at his election I touched also on the matter of money for Your Lordship. In effect it seems to me that
His Holiness together with the cardinals has resolved to procure peace in Italy, and for this reason to send the
Camerlengo and the Vice-Chancellor together to His Majesty the King; they left today, but their plans will not succeed,
because the King, whom I have seen again for this reason, means to make them understand very well, and especially the
Camerlengo (because the other is a Venetian), that these words of peace do not please him unless they first make
provision of money for Your Lordship, and of troops to send together with His Majesty, so that by this way the
security of the state of the said Duke is provided for, and the enemies, fearing the provisions, are brought to better
terms of peace, or, not wanting it out of love, can be brought to it by force. His Majesty resolves also, if this
speech does not move the Pope and the cardinals to some better and more agreeable resolution, to declare to them that
His Majesty means to take on himself the burden of paying your expenses, and to tell them to be sure he will make them
pay it all, and if even this does not help, he shows he will use other than words, that is, come to deeds, saying that
priests are men to be beaten, not begged. I do not know what will follow; still I am not yet without hope that the Pope
and the cardinals, understanding the King's discontent, may be moved to take a better resolution; of what follows Your
Lordship will be informed. And perhaps, as I have written elsewhere, nothing else will do but that Your Lordship make
some demonstrations, to frighten the company still more and bring it to what we desire. I reminded His Majesty the King
again of the matter of the money for Your Lordship, and he answered that without fail he will give such orders that it
will not fail you at the terms, and likewise he said that this week he will send his man, whom he is sending to the lord
[gap: of Ferrara?] for those twenty thousand ducats. I commend myself to Your Excellency, and I marvel that I have
never had from you either letter or answer. Rome, 8 March 1447, by your devoted servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the
illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

Marcolino Barbavara, Rome, 4 Mar 1447 (cipher f. 62; copy f. 61r)

The cardinals have entered the conclave; letters go by Madonna Aura and Messer Angelo in Urbino; Alfonso's letters enclosed, the King pleased that Sforza has turned from the Kingdom.

## f. 48r-v = f. 49 (Rome, 27 Feb 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Having arrived here I found the Pope had passed from this life, as you
will have heard, and he has indeed wished to do as Your Lordship used to say: to die so as to do harm not only living
but also dying. I have been with the Camerlengo, who, having heard the reason for my coming, says that in effect it is

me he seems to me very willing to have and keep good friendship with Your Lordship. He charged me to write to you to be

be a friend of the lord your father, or at least not his enemy, but rather believes he will be his friend and
favourable, and God grant it be so. It is thought that the election must fall either to Monsignor of Capua or to

has commonly thought of. If Your Excellency made some show and demonstration of wanting to advance into the Marche or

striven to get out of their heads, and said that such a foundation is vain, and that, though you are well disposed, this
good disposition must be helped otherwise than with words, that is with money. But to take it from them still [gap?].
Rome, 27 February. Your Excellency's little servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## f. 74r-v = f. 75 (Rome, 8 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Since I wrote to you of the election of the new Pope, I was, on the very
day of the election, so as to lose no time, at the feet of His Holiness, and after some general words rejoicing in the
name of the Duke at his election I touched also on the matter of money for Your Lordship. In effect it seems to me that
His Holiness together with the cardinals has resolved to procure peace in Italy, and for this reason to send the
Camerlengo and the Vice-Chancellor together to His Majesty the King; they left today, but their plans will not succeed,
because the King, whom I have seen again for this reason, means to make them understand very well, and especially the
Camerlengo (because the other is a Venetian), that these words of peace do not please him unless they first make
provision of money for Your Lordship, and of troops to send together with His Majesty, so that by this way the
security of the state of the said Duke is provided for, and the enemies, fearing the provisions, are brought to better
terms of peace, or, not wanting it out of love, can be brought to it by force. His Majesty resolves also, if this
speech does not move the Pope and the cardinals to some better and more agreeable resolution, to declare to them that
His Majesty means to take on himself the burden of paying your expenses, and to tell them to be sure he will make them
pay it all, and if even this does not help, he shows he will use other than words, that is, come to deeds, saying that
priests are men to be beaten, not begged. I do not know what will follow; still I am not yet without hope that the Pope
and the cardinals, understanding the King's discontent, may be moved to take a better resolution; of what follows Your
Lordship will be informed. And perhaps, as I have written elsewhere, nothing else will do but that Your Lordship make
some demonstrations, to frighten the company still more and bring it to what we desire. I reminded His Majesty the King
again of the matter of the money for Your Lordship, and he answered that without fail he will give such orders that it
will not fail you at the terms, and likewise he said that this week he will send his man, whom he is sending to the lord
[gap: of Ferrara?] for those twenty thousand ducats. I commend myself to Your Excellency, and I marvel that I have
never had from you either letter or answer. Rome, 8 March 1447, by your devoted servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the
illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

Marcolino Barbavara, Rome, 6 Mar 1447 (cipher f. 69; copy f. 68)

## f. 39 = f. 40 (Rimini, 13 Feb 1447)

If the courier has not kept the ways Your Lordship would have wished, it came about because, those of the [gap] being

## f. 48r-v = f. 49 (Rome, 27 Feb 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Having arrived here I found the Pope had passed from this life, as you
will have heard, and he has indeed wished to do as Your Lordship used to say: to die so as to do harm not only living
but also dying. I have been with the Camerlengo, who, having heard the reason for my coming, says that in effect it is
necessary to wait for the election of another Pope and to treat the matter with His Holiness, and that meanwhile I can
go to His Majesty the King to tighten the matter with him; and so I shall do, and Your Lordship will be told what
follows. The said Camerlengo received very gratefully all I told him on your behalf, and from the words he used with
me he seems to me very willing to have and keep good friendship with Your Lordship. He charged me to write to you to be
of good heart, because whoever is elected to the papacy will gladly do things that please you. He says too that he will
be a friend of the lord your father, or at least not his enemy, but rather believes he will be his friend and
favourable, and God grant it be so. It is thought that the election must fall either to Monsignor of Capua or to
Monsignor of Fermo; yet it could be that it falls to others, because it seems to me it always falls to the one no one
has commonly thought of. If Your Excellency made some show and demonstration of wanting to advance into the Marche or
elsewhere, so that news of it came here, I believe it would be very useful to warm the minds of these priests and make
them hotter to contribute to your expenses; and I say this because they have built an almost certain foundation that
Your Lordship will in any case go to the favour and help of the said lord your father. That opinion, however, I have
striven to get out of their heads, and said that such a foundation is vain, and that, though you are well disposed, this
good disposition must be helped otherwise than with words, that is with money. But to take it from them still [gap?].
Rome, 27 February. Your Excellency's little servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## ff. 55r-v, 57r = f. 56 (Rome, 3 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. I have been to His Majesty the King, and with such reasons as came to
mind I urged and begged him, in the name of my lord, to take all the greater care and burden of contributing to your
expenses as the need is greater through the death of the Pope, from whom no subsidy is to be expected any more. In
effect His Serenity has been content not only to pay what the Pope was to pay, but also to contribute a greater sum
than had first been discussed, namely ninety-four thousand ducats of gold, in gold, every year and for two years, as
the said lord wrote that he was content with two years. He will pay the said money in this way: twenty thousand ducats
this month, and another twenty thousand in the coming month of April at the latest, and the rest half at the end of six
months from the day of your engagement and the other half at the end of the year, and this for the first year; but for
the second year he is content to pay forty thousand ducats by the whole of March of that year, and the other 54
thousand from six months to six months as said above. And so His Serenity has promised, by articles signed and
concluded with him, sealed with his seal and also signed in his own hand; and he is to have the said money paid out at
the due times to whatever messenger of the said lord, or of yours, holds a sufficient mandate for it. On the lord's
part he has been promised that you will make no attacks on his men; rather, if need be, the said lord will send to his
aid either as many of your men, or of the other ducal troops, as amounts to the expense he bears for your condotta; and
he has also been promised that what the Pope pays, once he is elected, shall come to His Majesty. Of all this I inform
Your Excellency so that you know what has been done, and in good faith I recall two things. The first: that either His
Excellency the lord or you provide someone with a sufficient mandate to receive the money and to acknowledge receiving
it, and also to press for it with His Majesty the King, because you know well that where money is concerned one cannot
use too much diligence. The second: it seems to me necessary to make clear to His Majesty as soon as possible that the
conclusion has been made with him, so that he cannot excuse himself that, not having been made certain, he has not
provided the money. I have indeed given him good hope of the conclusion according to what Your Lordship told me, but I
do not think that is enough, and from my side I would not go on to give him any other assurance, having nothing else
either from the lord or from Your Lordship, who may write to me your intention about this, and I shall carry it out
without waiting for anything else. I inform Your Excellency also that His Majesty the King understands that in the said
sum of ninety-four thousand ducats which he is to pay are included the 20 thousand of the [gap: the lord of Ferrara?];
but he takes on himself the burden of making him pay them, and soon. For this he will at once send one of his men, and
when they are paid they will not be counted in the said 40 thousand ducats to be paid in March and April, but in the
others to be paid from six months to six months; and even if the [gap: lord of Ferrara?] paid no part of them, His
Majesty the King remains bound to pay the said sum of ninety-four thousand ducats every year. Had I been able to do
better, which I strove for as far as was possible, I would gladly have done it; but not being able, I accepted what I
could obtain. As for the election of the Pope, I wrote to you in other letters what I had heard; now I have nothing
else to add, except that everything will be done to have one who is a friend, and if he will not be, they will have
ways to make him repent. I commend myself to Your Lordship and also to the illustrious Madonna Bianca. Rome, 3 March.
Devoted servant, Marcolino. To the illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

## f. 61r = f. 62 (Rome, 4 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Replying briefly to what Your Excellency writes to me in letters of the
twenty-seventh of last month, I say that the ducal courier whom you had escorted brought me no letter but yours; I hear
indeed that he brought others to the Bishop of Novara and to Messer Pietro of Monferrato, and also to Simone Arrigoni,
servant of the lord your father, who has stayed in this place many months for His Lordship. I say also that by three
letters of mine I have informed Your Lordship of the death of the Pope and of what happened to me about it; nor has
anything new happened since, except that the cardinals entered the conclave this evening, and it is hoped they will
very soon elect another Pope, and opinions are very divided as to whom the election should fall to: God grant it be a
good one. Likewise I informed you by other letters of the conclusion made with His Majesty the King. Both the first and
the second letters I sent into the hands of the Magnificent Madonna Aura, as Your Lordship told me, and by sure
messengers, and the said letters should have arrived safely. From now on I shall direct such letters to Messer Angelo
in Urbino, as you write. And I shall continually inform Your Lordship of things as they happen. Now I send you, enclosed
with these, certain letters of His Majesty the King, by which you will understand his good disposition; informing Your
Lordship that he heard very gladly what I told him on your behalf, and above all he relished what you said of having
turned your mind from the affairs of the Kingdom, and showed a good will to have you wholly his. I commend myself to
Your Lordship. Rome, 4 March 1447, by Your Lordship's devoted servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the illustrious lord
Francesco Sforza.

## f. 68 = f. 69 (Rome, 6 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. At this seventeenth hour or about, Monsignor of "Cologna" [so the copy;
Tommaso Parentucelli, cardinal of Bologna] has been proclaimed Pope. I firmly believe he will be a friend, and I shall
soon strive to see proof of it in the matters entrusted to me. Of what follows Your Lordship will be informed; and you
may tell me whether it seems to you that one way rather than another should be kept by me. I commend myself to Your

## f. 74r-v = f. 75 (Rome, 8 Mar 1447)

Illustrious and excellent, most honoured lord. Since I wrote to you of the election of the new Pope, I was, on the very
day of the election, so as to lose no time, at the feet of His Holiness, and after some general words rejoicing in the
name of the Duke at his election I touched also on the matter of money for Your Lordship. In effect it seems to me that
His Holiness together with the cardinals has resolved to procure peace in Italy, and for this reason to send the
Camerlengo and the Vice-Chancellor together to His Majesty the King; they left today, but their plans will not succeed,
because the King, whom I have seen again for this reason, means to make them understand very well, and especially the
Camerlengo (because the other is a Venetian), that these words of peace do not please him unless they first make
provision of money for Your Lordship, and of troops to send together with His Majesty, so that by this way the
security of the state of the said Duke is provided for, and the enemies, fearing the provisions, are brought to better
terms of peace, or, not wanting it out of love, can be brought to it by force. His Majesty resolves also, if this
speech does not move the Pope and the cardinals to some better and more agreeable resolution, to declare to them that
His Majesty means to take on himself the burden of paying your expenses, and to tell them to be sure he will make them
pay it all, and if even this does not help, he shows he will use other than words, that is, come to deeds, saying that
priests are men to be beaten, not begged. I do not know what will follow; still I am not yet without hope that the Pope
and the cardinals, understanding the King's discontent, may be moved to take a better resolution; of what follows Your
Lordship will be informed. And perhaps, as I have written elsewhere, nothing else will do but that Your Lordship make
some demonstrations, to frighten the company still more and bring it to what we desire. I reminded His Majesty the King
again of the matter of the money for Your Lordship, and he answered that without fail he will give such orders that it
will not fail you at the terms, and likewise he said that this week he will send his man, whom he is sending to the lord
[gap: of Ferrara?] for those twenty thousand ducats. I commend myself to Your Excellency, and I marvel that I have
never had from you either letter or answer. Rome, 8 March 1447, by your devoted servant Marcolino de Barbavara. To the
illustrious lord Francesco Sforza.

Pietro Pusterla, Ferrara, 8 Mar 1447 (cipher f. 77; copy f. 76r-v)

And seeing {the Pope with the [?] [?] and perhaps} because {fearing worse} {would become} {[dangerous?]}, but
from the words we can say to him, we are sure he will hold firm in his opinion. And so, for the enclosed
letters, we are answering the lord your father, who wrote to us, that we pressed again with {the Pope} for the
matter {of [?]} {and for the other agreements}, praying your Excellency to be pleased to send them to his
Lordship by the first courier you happen to have. And because you write that you have from the lord Count that
he has sent and written on these matters {to the Pope and to His Majesty the King}, we tell you that we have
nothing else from his Lordship, as said. It may well be that Messer Pietro has had something on account of His
Majesty the King, and if so, we doubt he will tell your Excellency {to the bishop of Novara [for?] goodness}.
{In this respect, your letters have not been shown}; so we pray your Excellency to make no show of it at all
{?}. To whom we commend ourselves. Given at Rome, 4 May 1447.

Your Excellency's devoted servants, Marcolinus de Barbavara and Symon Arrigonus.
