The Hague → Dresden · two-figure letter-and-syllable cipher, unseparated, with nulls · December 1754 – May 1756

Johann Heinrich Kauderbach’s intercepted despatches from The Hague, 1754–56

Go to the full text ↓

Koninklijk Huisarchief, The Hague, Prins Willem V inv. 201: the Dutch copies of ten letters in which the Saxon-Polish resident Johann Heinrich Kauderbach put his secret passages in figures (DECODE R1043, R1044, R1954, R1958, R1959, R2078–R2082). DECODE records that the Dutch cabinet could not decipher them; no later decipherment was found in print or on DECODE.

The nulls were identified and the figures divided into two-figure codes. The key was then recovered from the ciphertext alone, by a solver that assigned the codes to the letters and syllables of Kauderbach’s 1761 key in Dresden. All ten letters are decrypted, 65–97% of the cipher per letter; nine read as sentences (seven at 95% or more of their words; R2078 at 93.6% and R1043 at 90.4% build coherent sentences under the 95% bar), and R1954 (about 65%, on a weak transcription) is decoded, not read.

At a glanceCiphertext-only break · partial (81% of the cipher)

The letters
Ten copies, made by the Dutch, of letters of the Saxon resident Kauderbach at The Hague, 1754–56, with passages in figures.
Where
DECODE notes that the Stadtholder’s cabinet deciphered none of them. Kauderbach wrote the figures without separation, so the office could not tell where a group ended.
The cipher
The digits 5 and 8 never occur inside a group and are nulls; the rest falls into about sixty two-figure groups, one key for all four years.
How it was read
A homophonic solver found nothing. Kauderbach’s own 1761 cipher (DECODE R936) uses two-figure numbers for letters and syllables; its numbers do not fit, but its vocabulary does. An annealing search for a one-to-one assignment converged twice to the same key, and hand correction fixed the rest.
What it says
The Dutch argument over the thirteen-thousand-man augmentation in 1755, Colonel Yorke and the Saxon subsidy treaty, and the Treaty of Versailles of May 1756 (“une consternation extraordinaire ici”).
Still open
The names stay in clear capitals (S./., Q./., P./.); their 1754 meanings are known only from context.

01 The documents

Kauderbach (1707–1785) served the Saxon legation at The Hague from 1739 for almost thirty years; his despatches went to Count Brühl in Dresden (“Votre Excellence”). The Dutch post office copied them for the Stadtholder’s cabinet, and the copies are in the Koninklijk Huisarchief, Prins Willem V inv. 201, beside the d’Affry intercepts of inv. 192. Most are wholly in clear. DECODE catalogued the ten with figures, from photographs, and transcribed three.

02 Splitting the figures

The Hague cabinet’s note on DECODE gives the reason for their failure: “the codegroups are not separated, by blanks or dots as is usually the case.” A first count showed no preferred period of two or three figures from the start of a passage, and the copyists’ spacing is not systematic. Two figures behave differently from the rest. 5 and 8 are rare (135 and 78 in the first 6,096 digits), never follow 5 or 8, and typically stand next to the clear sigla (…254 M./., J./. C./. 8095…). Deleting them as single figures restores a clean two-figure phase (index of coincidence 0.027 against 0.021 for the shifted reading; for four-figure windows 0.155 and 0.161 at offsets 0 and 2, 0.103 at 1 and 3). A short Viterbi pass over the pair frequencies then repairs the places where a copyist dropped or doubled a figure. What is left is a table of about sixty two-figure groups: first figure 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 or 9, second 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 or 9, never 0, 5 or 8. Every letter shows the same frequency profile, so one key served from 1754 to 1756 at least.

A homophonic solver failed on that table. A hill-climbing solver that decrypts synthetic French in the same form (sixty codes, two per cent noise, nulls, realignment) at 98% produced nothing on the real stream, in French, German, Dutch or Italian, with nulls or merged look-alike figures. The statistics of the stream still looked like language (entropy, vowel alternation 0.63, long exact repeats such as 19 29 14 44 nineteen times), so the units were not single letters.

03 The key

DECODE also holds the Dresden chancery’s cipher register, and one of its leaves is the key made for Kauderbach in 1761. It explains the 1754 system at once: a null figure, capitals in clear for the names, and a single number for each of about seventy letters and syllables. It is not the same key (its numbers use 5 and 8), but a successor of the same design. So the problem became an assignment: each 1754 code to a different item of the 1761 vocabulary. A simulated-annealing search over such assignments, scored by a four-letter French model built from contemporary diplomatic letters, reached the same key from two independent starts (seeds 22 and 24, −3.85 per letter, against −3.95 for true French on the same model), and the result was French. Reading the decryption then settled what the 1761 vocabulary lacked or the search had misplaced: 27 in (principales, provinces, interet), 91 ou, 33 qu, 79 st, 17 par, 22 pas, 46 qui, 66 pour, 23 fai, 76 ke (Yorke, Kniphausen), 77 z (treize, declarez), and the exchange of 01 oi and 12 ai, found independently by both readers (avoit, etoit, croire; raisons, mais, Pais Bas).

R1954, 16 March 1756 (decoded, not read)

On the demand for a secours of six thousand men. The September reading supplied words the figures do not carry. On 23 October 2026 the 314 digits marked uncertain in the transcription (the copyist’s curly 5/7, open 8/9 and u-shaped 11/4) were resolved by a French language-model search over the look-alikes and checked by eye, seven places were corrected on the images, and a partial reading was written with its holes marked (read/R1954_partial_reading.md, with English). The paragraph of arguments now holds: que [S./.] ne peut […] refuser ce secours sans rompre […] a [B./.], qui prendra […] refus pour une infraction des [U./.]z, et s'en [?vengera] sur son commerce; if the neutrality party loses, le peuple s'animera pour [C./.] et l'etat sera entraine malgre lui dans la querelle des [C./.]. The clause on the comte d’A[ffry] and most of f. 4 do not: the digits are legible on the images but do not decode, slips of the copyist of this intercept copy; the original in Dresden is not online. The letter stays decoded, not read.

05 What remains open

  • The sigla. The capitals with ./. (S, Q, P, U, J, C, M, E, Y, Z, W, B, G) are names written in clear, as in the 1761 key, but their 1754 meanings differ from it. S is almost certainly Holland (the Estates, the provinces), Y a subsidy treaty, Z troops; the rest are identified from context only and marked as sigla in the decryptions.
  • Rare codes. 10, 20, 40 and 90 occur twelve times in all and are guesses; 13 stands for y and apparently also re (membres, reflexion); 66 may stand for er inside some words.
  • R1954 (four pages in a letter-like hand): decoded, not read. After the look-alike digits were resolved on 23 October 2026, garbled runs remain on f. 2 lines 6, 8 and 13 and f. 4 lines 6–10 and 17–25; the digits there are legible but do not decode with the key, slips of the Dutch copyist. Only the original despatch (Hauptstaatsarchiv Dresden, Geheimes Kabinett, not online) could settle them.
  • Transcription slips. The DECODE transcriptions and the new ones both drop or double figures; the decryption files list those found. The clear passages were transcribed quickly and are summarised, not edited.
  • The percentages are the readers’ estimates of the share of the ciphertext that gives sense; 99.9% of the groups carry a key value.
