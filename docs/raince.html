Rome → the Court · homophonic letter cipher with nulls and word signs · 1526

Nicolas Raince to Montmorency, Rome, 13 May and 20 November 1526

BnF Français 2984, pp. 29–31 and 105 · about 106 ciphered lines, 5,629 glyphs · in no edition · the nine days before the League of Cognac, and two months after the Colonna raid on the Vatican

Tomokiyo published the key in 2020, without plaintext. For these two passages no decipherment was found on the leaves or in Mignet (1886) or Bourrilly (1901). The key was applied here: first taken off his table one column out, then re-measured and checked symbol by symbol on the leaf. 86 of the 106 lines were decoded by hand against numbered-token images; 20 have only a machine decryption. The decode has gaps in nearly every line, so the passages are decoded, not read.

02 The key as first transcribed was one column off

Tomokiyo set out the 1526 key in French Ciphers during the Reign of Francis I (2020) as an image: a header row of plaintext letters over up to four rows of cipher glyphs, the columns about 78 px apart. It is a monoalphabetic substitution with homophones, three nulls and three word signs. Assigning each glyph to its letter by eye is error-prone, and the first pass here slipped a column.

Measuring the positions settles it. Locating the white header letters in the magenta band, then labelling the ink blobs below and assigning each to the nearest header column, every blob falls within 15 px of a column centre, a fifth of the column spacing. The measured table is:

  • The correction. l is the 9, not the round s; m is the round s, not the long f; n is the long f. The first pass had these one place to the left. H measures under s, not under r where it had been put.
  • Why it matters. That single shift is what produced dongUeaent for longuement, egsembde for ensemble and SonseigEur for monseigneur in the first machine draft. Every file derived from the shifted table was discarded.
  • Confusable pairs. p has a T and a circle with a dash to its right; r has a 4 and a dash then a circle. The two are near mirror images and are the easiest pair on the leaf to confuse. The most frequent single glyph, a circle at the foot of a barred stem, is an e.
  • Word signs. A looped L with a tail = con; two small circles joined by a curve underneath = l’empereur; a cursive y with a descender = le pape. The letter y is E, so a y-shaped glyph is the word sign, not the letter.

The control. With the measured table, p. 29 line 20 decrypts glyph for glyph and leaves nothing over:

∧ λ n v ✕✕ 7 ∧ ℓ ꝛ I ℓ 7 f 9 o ⊡ ꝛ v 4 ℓ ϝ 7 ∧ o R ꝛ E ψ ny 7 ∧ ℓ T o 4 ℓ E T ꝛ v 4 R 7 f ✕✕ 4 I ⊡ E
→ s [null] qui estoit en la court de Savoye [null] est party pour venir icy

The line uses a, c, d, three of the four e homophones, two of the three i, l, n, o, p, q, r, s, t, u/v, y, and two of the three nulls. That is the evidence the table is right.

03 pp. 29–31, not ff. 29r, 30v, 31r

The numbers written on successive Gallica views run 29, 31, 33, 35, 37, two apart. Taken as folio numbers that would mean half the volume’s openings were never filmed, and every letter in it half missing. They are page numbers, written on odd pages only: view n shows pages 2n−4 and 2n−3, 85 views cover about 167 pages, and the digitisation is complete. So the 13 May letter is three consecutive pages, 29, 30 and 31, and the 20 November one is p. 105.

The text settles it independently. Page 29 ends … et n’est possible … monstrer plus a[ffe]- and page 30 opens -ction. Both halves of that join were deciphered glyph by glyph before the join was noticed.

04 Reading a microfilm

The film is legible at about 2× but the ink is grey and the hand is small, so the difficulty is transcription. Three steps made it possible.

  1. One image per manuscript line, cut into four overlapping quarters and stacked, so a whole line fits in one view without losing magnification.
  2. Numbered tokens. Connected-component segmentation gives 5,629 glyph tokens; drawing each token’s box on the line and numbering it means a hand transcription is written as one character per token and lands aligned to the segmentation with no guesswork. Every line was checked against its token count before it was kept.
  3. Machine suggestions. A classifier over the token images, combined with a 6-gram language model of period French without spaces, proposes a letter for every token. It is right on about 85–90 % of them: enough to speed up hand transcription, too few to replace it.

How accurate the classifier is. The hand decipherments double as training data, so the classifier can be scored by leaving one hand-deciphered line out at a time:

At 0.90 per token a six-letter word survives at 0.53, which is why only about a third of the machine text segments into real French words however the decoder is tuned. The residual confusions are between glyphs that really are similar at this resolution: ψ (e) against 4 (r), the cursive ℓ (t) against 9 (l) against the lollipop (d), the caret (s) against the vee (u) against × (a). The key itself leaves none of these ambiguous. More labels will not fix that; better images would.

05 The letter of 13 May 1526

Nine days before the League of Cognac was signed. The clear close on p. 31 (transcribed here; no printed text of it was found) dates and signs it (De Rome, ce xiij jour de may, 15c xxvj … Nicolas Rainse) and reports that Rome expects Andrea Doria at Civitavecchia that day or the next, with six galleys and two brigantines. The three ciphered pages that precede it give, in part:

  • … ne cessera jamais qu’il n’ayt destruict ledict seigneur …; and, a line later, d’autres propo[s] … ains de vert[u] et mensonges.
  • … ont receu … de Castille, qu’ilz disent …; ensemble … les bourguignons; and, of a great lord, les castilliens li[sent] au (con)traire que lesdessusdict[z] sont …
  • … delib-eré prendre le chemin de Valence … à ceste fin, et qu’il ne voul-oyent qu’il feust … si grant seigneur.
  • … et qu’il[z] seront cause de la destruction, de la part d’avoir faic-t capituler avec …; the likeliest referent is the Treaty of Madrid of January 1526, though the letter does not name it.
  • Ledict He[rr]er[a](?) doit partir de dans deux ou troys jours pour s’en retourner (the name is not settled); and …s qui estoit en la court de Savoye est party pour venir icy.
  • p. 30: (L’empereur) est esbay de ce que … n’a escript autrement, aucune part deça, depuis son retour … en son royaume; et m’en a parlé plusieurs fois.
  • … que mondict seigneur l’Admiral luy av-oit dict que seroit celuy qui y tiendroit. J’ay tousiours respondu à ce que ce qu’on a envoyé d’icy et de Venise par devers ledict seigneur …

  • p. 31: … la peste qui est au logis …; …s foys escript des advis qui viennent aux imperiaulx par leu[rs] …; … es grant dommaige en toutes les sortes qu’on pourroit …; [il] y ayt grant obligation pour les dommaiges qu’ilz disent ledict avoir…
  • and, near the end, a named man: …t un jour, en plaine place pres du palais, un nommé Pietro Anthonio … secretaire ou serviteur dudict, qui estoit icy pour luy … followed by propos … quel estoit cause de tout le mal … deux ou trois fois.

06 The letter of 20 November 1526

Two months after the Colonna raid on the Vatican, and five months before the Sack. The ciphered part turns on the Pope:

… qui a … peust … faire s[e]cre[te]ment · et … pontif-icat seroit la cause de la totale(?) ruine de sa maison, de-…

With, in the same passage, …me, et s’en fuire; et que encores … luy feist … la grace; … et que … l’on penseroit … y respondre de part de nature; …t jamais pensé que les choses … prinst, et le for[t]-; sincerement … qu’il estoit … en besoing; … et que l’i[n]convenient … peril à qu[oy] …; and et aussi qu’il estoit … bien cler que n’estoit … dicte de. “Sa maison” is the Medici, and the pontificate is Clement VII’s.

Read in full by Brice Dudout, 27 October 2026. The cipher does not stop at the foot of p. 105: p. 106 is a whole page of it, 35 lines, which this page had missed, ending in the clear postscript on the bull of monitory against Cardinal Colonna. Dudout read the lower 21 lines of p. 105 and all of p. 106 sign by sign on the full-size Gallica images (IIIF, about 7,900 pixels wide), with Tomokiyo’s table as re-measured here and frozen before reading: about 98% of some 610 words, the rest mostly word signs missing from the table. On p. 105 his text agrees with the decode made here on 95.6% of the letters, and fills most of its holes. It is his reading, not re-read here on the image. The Pope, says Raince, wishes for death: his pontificate would be “la cause de la totale ruine de sa maison”, the King would lose “le meilleur amy qu’il eut oncque au monde”, and His Holiness charged him to write it all to the King. The full text and translation are under Full texts.

07 What remains open

  • Re-measured 5 October 2026. Every line was re-read from the image (trans/reading_v3.txt) and the reading scored for sense: p. 29 0.923, p. 30 0.877, p. 31 0.872, p. 105 0.781, all 0.870 (on the old reading the same measure gives 0.476). The circled cross is now identified as i. Open: about 13% of letters whose glyphs do not resolve on the black-and-white Gallica microfilm (worst p. 105 ll. 15–17, 20–21, p. 30 ll. 30–34, p. 31 ll. 1–2, 17, 23–24), and two word signs absent from Tomokiyo’s key (a bold two-crossbar stem, a person after son maistre, and ℓ°), about four occurrences each.
  • Twenty lines of the 106 carry only the machine decode. They are kept, marked, in trans/reading.txt and left out of the edited text.
  • Gaps inside the 86. The edited text leaves … wherever the glyphs did not resolve. Nothing is supplied by conjecture; a wrong confident decipherment would be worse than a gap.
  • One glyph is in no published table: a bold vertical stem crossed by two horizontal bars, the lower one longer and projecting right. It occurs ten times across all four pages and every occurrence falls at a clause boundary: between plaisirs and qu’il, between lectres and qui est, or opening a sentence. It is not the barred-b g, not the barred-stem e, and not any of the three nulls. Most likely a point or a fourth null; it is written · in the edited text.
  • The p. 29 / p. 30 join gives …monstrer plus a[ffe]- + -ctio?e, where the glyph after ctio measures as a plain triangle (f) rather than the n the sense wants. Left with a query.
  • Two personal names are not settled: messire Ca?in, who appears twice on p. 30, and the agent transcribed as He[rr]er[a] on p. 29.
  • What would finish it was thought to be better images. Brice Dudout’s reading of pp. 105–106 (27 October 2026) shows the black-and-white microfilm resolves at Gallica’s full size on crops of half a line at about three times magnification; the unresolved glyphs here came from the half-size crops. The 13 May letter, pp. 29–31, can be re-read the same way.

08 Method

  1. Key measured off the image. Header letters located in the key image, ink blobs labelled and assigned to the nearest column, every assignment within 15 px of a column centre. Contact sheet of every labelled glyph kept.
  2. Verified on the leaf against a control line that decrypts with nothing left over, using sixteen of the alphabet’s letters and two of its three nulls.
  3. Segmentation by connected components on a background-normalised binarisation, tokens assigned to lines by a y-histogram, x-overlapping components merged; 5,629 tokens over the four pages.
  4. Cluster-to-letter map fitted by hill-climbing against a space-free 6-gram model of period French, with each cluster restricted to the letters its glyph shape allows. Restricting the moves is what stops the collapse into a degenerate map that defeats an unconstrained solver on a stream with this much segmentation noise.
  5. Hand decipherment line by line against numbered-token images, one character per token, length-checked against the segmentation.
  6. Scoring by leave-one-line-out over the hand-deciphered lines, since a model trained on its own output otherwise scores itself.

Everything is in the repository: the measured key and reading method in targets/raince/CALIBRATION.md, the account in targets/raince/NOTES.md, the hand labels in targets/raince/gold/labels.txt, the edited reading in targets/raince/trans/reading_edited.txt, the line-by-line text with each line marked hand or machine in targets/raince/trans/reading.txt, and the clear close of 13 May in targets/raince/trans/p31_clear_close.txt.

Raince to Montmorency, Rome, 13 May 1526 (BnF fr. 2984 no. 7, pp. 29-31): ciphered part, reading by Brice Dudout

A letter written nine days before the League of Cognac was signed (22 May 1526): the ciphered part fills the lower half of p. 29 (after ten lines glossed at the time), the whole of p. 30 and the top of p. 31; the plain close announces Andrea Doria’s arrival at Civitavecchia with six galleys and gives the date “De Rome, ce xiij jour de may 15c xxvj”. Raince reports the quarrels at Charles V’s court after the release of Francis I, the complaints of his Roman contact that the King has sent nobody to the Pope, and the leaks running through the Duke of Ferrara’s men to the Imperialists.

Raince to Montmorency, Rome, 20 Nov 1526 (BnF fr. 2984 no. 24): p. 105 lower part and p. 106, reading by Brice Dudout

Two months after the Colonna raid on the Vatican, Clement VII summoned the French king’s agent and spoke “sighing so deeply that there is no heart so hard that it would not have felt the greatest compassion in the world”. He thought of abandoning Rome and fleeing, blamed France for not breaking with the enemy “over there”, and predicted that people in France would soon say: “the Pope has run away”. Nicolas Raince put it all into cipher for Anne de Montmorency, Grand Master of France. The end of page 105 and the whole of page 106 had never been deciphered.

What the ciphered passages are about
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The 13 May 1526 letter, written nine days before the League of Cognac was signed, reports
on the Emperor's side of the negotiation and on the movement of agents: someone "qui estoit
en la court de Savoye est party pour venir icy"; the Castilians and "les bourguignons";
a plan to "prendre le chemin de Valence"; a capitulation already made, which "il[z] seront
cause de la destruction … de la part d'avoir faict capituler"; an agent Herrera(?) who
"doit partir … dans deux ou troys jours pour s'en retourner". Rome's own despatches are
described as going "d'icy et de Venise par devers ledict seigneur", and the Emperor "est
esbay de ce que … n'a escript autrement". The Admiral of France is quoted at second hand
("mondict seigneur l'Admiral luy avoit dict que seroit celuy qui y tiendroit"). The plague
is in Rome ("la peste qui est au logis"), advices reach the Imperials through their own
people ("des advis qui viennent aux imperiaulx par leurs …"), a named man — Pietro Anthonio,
secretary or servant of the same — was taken "un jour, en plaine place pres du palais", and
there is talk of "grant obligation pour les dommaiges qu'ilz disent ledict avoir …".
The clear close of the same letter, on p. 31, expects Andrea Doria at Civitavecchia within
a day with six galleys and two brigantines.

The 20 November letter, two months after the Colonna raid on the Vatican, turns on the
Pope: a pontificate that "seroit la cause de la totale ruine de sa maison" — Clement VII
and the Medici — with talk of doing something "secretement", of flight ("s'en fuire"), of
"le peril à quoy", and of what "l'on penseroit".
