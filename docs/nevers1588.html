Paris → the duc de Nevers · homophonic cipher of letters and figures, with code numbers · 29 April 1588

A court informant to the Duke of Nevers, 29 April 1588

BnF Français 3976 f. 62 (DECODE R3705, catalogued as “1 Jan 1588”, not deciphered). One page of Paris news in clear French with nineteen cipher runs and ten code numbers, and no decipherment. The key was recovered from the interlinear decipherment of a later letter by the same informant in the same volume, then extended run by run from the clear context. All nineteen runs were deciphered; one name, one word and four code numbers stay uncertain or open. No earlier decipherment of this letter is known.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 22 September 2026 · updated 26 October 2026

At a glanceKey from adjacent plaintext · complete

The letter
A court informant to the Duke of Nevers, 29 April 1588: news including the lifting of the sieges of Sedan and Jametz. DECODE gives the date as 1 January 1588, a placeholder.
Where
BnF fr. 3976 f. 62 (Nevers papers, 1588); catalogued on DECODE as a non-deciphered letter from an unknown sender.
The cipher
Names and words spelled in a cipher of letters and figures, plus bare code numbers. A sign is one character, or the figure 1 plus a character.
How it was read
None of Tomokiyo’s Nevers keys fitted and a ciphertext-only annealing solver found nothing. The key came from f. 131, the same informant’s letter of 4 June 1588, where a secretary wrote plaintext over each run.
What it says
Henri III was nearly resolved in council to have five or six leading Paris ligueurs thrown in the river, a fortnight before the Day of the Barricades; the letter gives Guise’s answer.
Still open
One name, one word and three code numbers.
What was added
The June letter that gave the key, f. 131, was itself deciphered in full on 2 October 2026: all 130 cipher signs and all eight code numbers.

01 The document

Fr. 3976 is a volume of Nevers’s papers for 1588, on Gallica as btv1b9060548h; f. 62 is view 111. The BnF’s piece-by-piece notice lists it as no. 30: “Lettre, avec chiffre, adressée au duc de Nevers, contenant entre autres nouvelles, celle de la levée du siège de Sedan et de Jametz. Ce 29 avril 1588.” The neighbouring pieces at ff. 56 and 60 are symbol ciphers from the cardinals of Bourbon and Guise, each with its decipherment. They use a different system.

The last lines explain the method: “Il y a quelques motz au chiffre cy dessus que j’eusse plus aisement et briefvement mis par nombres, mais craignant que ne feussiez memoratif d’iceulx n’ayant ce chiffre, j’ay mieulx aymé estre plus long.”

02 The key, from the informant’s June letter

The same hand, cipher and monogram appear on f. 131 (4 June 1588) and f. 133 (6 June 1588). On f. 131 someone wrote the plaintext above the runs: “quelques mutins d’entr’eux”, “conte de Brissac”, “que s’il perissoit” (first read here as “pouvoit”; corrected on 2 October 2026 from the cipher, which has r and ss), “Roy”, and over the numbers 24 “nevers” and 11 “le Roy”. (The gloss over 24 was first taken here as “Navarre”; the letter of 6 June, deciphered later the same day, showed that 24 is Nevers.)

The Brissac gloss fixed the unit. 7 · i7 · id · i2 · t · 6 · s · 8 · g · p · i3 · f · 2 · X is fourteen signs for the fourteen letters of contedebrissac, where the i-like character is the figure 1 and pairs with the sign after it. With some fifteen values from f. 131, the runs of f. 62 began to give sense. Each run then added values to the key: 11 p n o t 14 17 ϙ after “les grosses larmes de 12 et” gave Villeroy; the next person named gave Bellièvre; …n ce royaume gave 9 = a; “chancellier et d’O” gave v = d. The alphabet is homophonic, and some signs are polyphonic (one sign, two or three letters): the curly 8 is b, u or v, 9 is a, h or r, and 3 is g or j. The hand’s 2 looks like a z, its 8 like a curly d and its 9 like a g, and the first transcription confused all three. The key is in key.md.

03 The decipherment

The deciphered runs are in bold. Numbers are the writer’s code numbers.

J’ay communiqué a 28 la lettre de [monogram]. Suivant laquelle je diz qu’il ne faudra pas, si Fougères(?) est encore icy, de luy representer les pertinentes raisons contenues en icelles, par lesquelles se congnoist assez que les advertissements de 24 ne peuvent avoir esté cause de leur ruyne comme ilz disent, ny de quoy leur entreprise a failly. Mais 28 et 70 estiment bien que 24 a assez de jugement pour n’embrasser telle entreprise que bien a propos.

J’ay sceu que led. 28 a prié 70 d’advertir icelluy 24 […]. Il cuyda estre resolu au conseil de prendre et jetter en l’eau cinq ou six des plus grans ligueurs de 102, a quoy poussoyent fort a la Royne, chancellier et d’O. Et 11 n’en fut desmeu sinon par les remonstrances et grosses larmes de 12 et Villeroy, qui saigement remonstrerent que tout aussi tost que cela seroit executé, non seulement l’on perdroit entierement 102, mais encore 20 des plus belles et fortes villes du royaume.

De cela 9 fut incontinent adverty, qui appela Bellièvre pour le luy dire, jurant et reniant que si le moindre de la ville l’avoit mal, il feroit aud. 49 pareil traitement, et qu’il le mandast hardiment a 11, et que si telle affaire s’entreprenoit et executoit, luy avoit a son commandement 40 mil hommes, avec lesquels il remueroit tant de messages(?) en ce royaume que led. 11 en auroit la plus petite part. Voila tout ce que j’ay peu scavoir de nouveau.

Translation of the core. The King was nearly resolved in council to seize and throw into the river five or six of the chief ligueurs of Paris, pressed to it by the Queen, the chancellor and d’O. Only the remonstrances and tears of 12 and of Villeroy stopped him: they argued that once it was done he would lose Paris and twenty of the finest towns of the kingdom as well. Guise (9) heard of it at once and sent for Bellièvre to tell him, swearing that if the least man of the town came to harm he would deal with 49 the same way. Bellièvre could tell the King so plainly. If the thing were attempted, Guise had forty thousand men at his command, and with them he would stir up so much in the kingdom that the King would be left with the smallest share.

Code numbers: 9 = the duc de Guise, 11 = the King and 24 = the duc de Nevers are glossed on f. 131, and 10 = the duc d’Épernon on ff. 131 and 139 (see the June letters). 102 = Paris follows from the context. 12, who wept with Villeroy, is probably Catherine de Médicis.

04 The letter that gave the key: f. 131, 4 June 1588

F. 131 (Gallica view 221; view 222 is f. 132, blank) is one page in the same hand, closed with the same monogram, written three weeks after the Day of the Barricades. It has eight cipher runs, 130 signs, and eight code numbers, and a decipherer of the time wrote the plaintext over every run and every number. On 2 October 2026 the page was transcribed whole and each run was checked sign by sign against the key of ff. 62, 133 and 139 (f131/decode.py). All 130 signs are deciphered by the key, none from context alone, and all eight runs agree with their glosses. Three values are new: a bare 1 is i (so the old two-character signs “1n” and “1e” are 1 + l and 1 + t, which leaves the readings of f. 62 unchanged), a b-like sign is y (once, in Roy, glossed), and h carries qu. The runs read as French throughout (33 of 33 words; the fr-1600-letters model gives −1.56 per character).

Paris three weeks after the Barricades: the militia captains at the Hôtel de Ville moving to an apology to Henri III, then at Vernon on his way to Rouen; rumours about Brissac and the duc d’Elbeuf in the fort Sainte-Catherine at Rouen; the fort of Meulan invested by Biron and Aumont; Guise’s troops round Corbeil; Épernon gone to Loches, saying that if he perished the whole kingdom should perish with him. The postscript warns Nevers that the duke of Savoy is arming, while offering the King his help, to take the marquisate of Saluzzo; Charles-Emmanuel did so in October 1588. Two clear-text words are uncertain readings of the hand (contenante, praticquer); no cipher sign is open.

05 What failed first

  • Tomokiyo’s Nevers keys in fr. 3995, nos. 10, 11, 16 and 18, and an alphabet on view 45 of that volume. None decrypts run 1.
  • A ciphertext-only homophonic solver (simulated annealing) on about 440 characters, with letter, null and word-divider variants for the i-like sign. It collapsed to strings of e, n and t, because the unit was not yet known and the transcription was too noisy.
  • The symbol ciphers of ff. 56 and 60, which come with decipherments. They belong to another system.

06 What remains uncertain

  • Run 1: decrypted letter by letter as Fougères (grade C). This is a person at court, not identified.
  • Run 18: messages (grade C) holds only if the sign 1r = g. That sign occurs once.
  • Run 15, l’avoit mal: grade C.
  • Code numbers 28, 49 and 70 are not identified; the informant’s number list is not in fr. 3995 as far as checked, and the glosses of ff. 131 and 139 (9, 10, 11, 24, 38, 72, 73, 98, 107) do not include them. 12 is identified by context only.
  • F. 131: no cipher sign open. The b-like sign = y rests on one glossed occurrence.
  • The June letters on ff. 133–134 (DECODE R3708) and f. 139 use the same key. They were deciphered later the same day in the write-up on the June letters.

# BnF fr. 3976 f. 62 (DECODE R3705): the Paris informant, 29 April 1588: English translation

Translation (Claude Opus 5.5, 23 Oct 2026) of the reading in `texts/f62_reading.md`: the cipher runs (**bold**) in their
clear context. Code numbers are kept as numbers; by the glosses of f. 131 and the context, 9 = the duc de Guise,
11 = the King, 24 = the duc de Nevers, 102 = Paris, 12 = probably the Queen Mother; 10, 20 (a count), 28, 49, 70 not
identified. Same marks: `(?)` doubtful, `[...]` a gap in the transcription, `[monogram]` the writer's sign.

## Translation

I have shown 28 the letter of [monogram]. In accordance with which, I say that, if **Fougères(?)** is still here, one
must not fail to set before him the pertinent reasons contained in it, by which it is clear enough that 24's warnings
cannot have been the cause of **their ruin**, as they say, nor of why **their enterprise failed**. Also most of those
who spoke of it did not mean that 24's [enterprise] was like the other, nor that 24's coming had been at the instance
of 9, but only **for the ruin of 10, assisted** by many **honest men of authority** who were well disposed to it **and
are disposing themselves to it** still every day. But 28 and 70 consider well that 24 has judgement enough not to
embrace **such an enterprise** except very much to the purpose. I have learned that the said 28 has begged 70 to warn
the said 24. [...] It was nearly **resolved in council to take and throw into the water five or six of the greatest
Leaguers of** 102, to which the Queen, **the Chancellor and d'O** were strongly pushing. And 11 was dissuaded from it
only by the remonstrances and great tears of 12 and **Villeroy**, who wisely pointed out that as soon as that was
carried out, not only would 102 be lost entirely, but also 20 **of the finest and strongest towns of the kingdom**. Of
this 9 was at once informed, who called **Bellièvre** to tell him so, swearing and blaspheming that if **the least man
of the town were harmed**, he would give the said 49 like treatment, and that he should boldly send word of it to 11;
and that if such a business were undertaken and carried out, he **had 40 thousand men at his command**, with which he
**would stir up so many messages(?) in this kingdom** that the said 11 would have **the smallest part** of it. That is
all the news I have been able to learn.

Paris, 4 June 1588, BnF fr. 3976 f. 131r

The same informant reports the Hôtel de Ville assembly of 3 June moving to an apology to the King, rumours from Rouen about Brissac and Elbeuf, Meulan invested by Biron and Aumont, Corbeil surrounded by Guise, and Épernon at Loches saying that if he perished he wanted the whole kingdom to perish with him; a postscript says Savoy is arming to take the marquisate of Saluzzo. Every run carries a gloss of the time, and all agree with the key.

# BnF fr. 3976 f. 131r: the Paris informant, 4 June 1588

Gallica `btv1b9060548h` canvas 221 (f. 131r; canvas 222 is f. 132, blank, so the letter is one page). Not a DECODE
record. Same hand, cipher and monogram as f. 62 (R3705, this folder), ff. 133-134 (R3708) and f. 139
(`targets/r3708/`). Transcribed and read 2 Oct 2026.

Files: `transcription.md` (whole clear text with run markers), `ciphertext.txt` (8 runs G01-G08), `reading.tsv`
(reading, context, the contemporary gloss), `decode.py` (sign-by-sign check against the key, blind decode, LM score).
Images in `../img/` (git-ignored): `c221.jpg` full page, crops `z*`, `y*`, `p*`, `L*`.

Paris three weeks after the Barricades: the Hôtel de Ville assembly of the militia captains moving to an apology to
Henri III (then at Vernon on his way to Rouen); rumours about Brissac and the duc d'Elbeuf at the fort Sainte-Catherine
of Rouen; the Meulan fort, invested by Biron and Aumont; Guise's troops round Corbeil; Épernon gone to Loches. The
postscript warns Nevers that the duke of Savoy is arming, under cover of offering help to the King, to take the
marquisate of Saluzzo, which Charles-Emmanuel did in October 1588.

## Translation

4 June 1588. Yesterday an assembly of the captains of the town was held at the Hôtel de Ville, where **some mutineers
among them** proposed several things rather ill to the purpose, as people say. But afterwards others of the said
captains, of the body of the court of Parlement, took the floor and made very fine remonstrances, containing in sum
the obedience and acknowledgement due to the King [...], and that, since there were no more Huguenots in Paris, all
must remain united in religion and in duty towards their prince [...]; one can do no less than make excuses to him for
it and beg him to pardon the offence [...]. It was resolved that another, more notable assembly would be held, both of
the bodies and courts and of the burgesses, to decide on it. [...] And it would seem that in the meantime 24 [Nevers]
would be very much to the purpose near 11 [the King].

The secretary of the post has assured me that the King slept last night at Vernon, to go from there to Rouen. Some
tried to spread the rumour this morning that **the Count of Brissac has been killed and that d'Elbeuf is in the fort
of Sainte-Catherine**. You will have learned that the man who commanded in the fort of Meulan [...] had sent word to 9
[Guise] to send him others [...]; His Majesty [...] at once had the place invested by Messieurs the Marshals de Biron
and d'Aumont. [...] Corbeil is all surrounded by the troops of Monsieur de Guise [...] It is held for certain that
Monsieur d'Épernon has gone to Loches only to receive the forces of Lavardin and others of Poitou, to return and join
the King. And I have heard trustworthy people say that they knew that 10 [Épernon] had said to some of his friends
**that if he perished** he wanted the whole kingdom **to perish with him**. [...] This 4th of June. [monogram]

I believe that, if 24 [Nevers] goes to see 11 [the King], it will be well that he be warned that 72 [Savoy] **is
arming**, and that it has been drawn by conjecture from his 73 [ambassador] that he is advised to do so with the
intention of keeping the **King** in alarm and nevertheless offering him his help and service, but on condition of
doing his own business and negotiating, if he can, the **marquisate of Saluzzo**. Therefore it would be well to keep
the places well provided. [monogram]
