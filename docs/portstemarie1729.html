Port Sainte-Marie → Duke of Newcastle · French one-part code · 1729 · key rebuilt from siblings

A British agent at Port Sainte-Marie writes to Newcastle in code, 1729

Go to the full text ↓

A coded report that nobody deciphered on the leaf now decodes in part, using a code table rebuilt from the same agent's letters that were glossed at the time: 116 of its 141 groups carry a value written down in 1729, 18 more were filled from the sense, and 7 are open.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 28 October 2026 · updated 12 November 2026

Method: key recovered based on adjacent plaintext · Extent: partial (82% of the cipher)

The letter
An unnamed British agent at El Puerto de Santa María (Port Sainte-Marie, near Cadiz) to "Milord", the Duke of Newcastle, about July 1729. It was sent on with a covering letter of 8 August 1729.
Where
Huntington Library, William Blathwayt papers addenda, mssBLA 191, p. 5 (Huntington Digital Library p15150coll7 id 61005). Not on DECODE.
The cipher
A one-part French code of numbers from 1 to about 1370. Letters, syllables, words and names are listed in alphabetical order, so 580 is je, 581 ie and 585 ig.
How it was deciphered
1,055 code/gloss pairs were copied from five sibling letters in the same file, which were deciphered between the lines in 1729. That gives 364 values. Fifteen more groups (18 tokens) were filled from the sense, bracketed by their alphabetical neighbours; the 95% bar counts those as holes, so they are marked [?] and not counted as deciphered.
What it says
The agent complains that Mr Keene, the British envoy at Madrid, has left him without orders for weeks during the accommodation with Spain: "Mandez-moi donc quelque ordre pour ma conduite."
How much is deciphered
116 of 141 groups (0.823) carry a glossed value: partial. This page first said 134 of 141 (95%, complete); that counted the 18 groups filled from the sense, and was corrected on 12 November 2026.
Still open
Seven groups with no value (6, 274, 254, 937, 1052, 726 and 836), and the 18 filled from the sense. Group 1118 has two values on record, “trouver” (from the sense) and “tain” (one retouched gloss in mssBLA 194); neither is adopted.

01 The document and its siblings

The covering letter to Newcastle of 8 August 1729 encloses two letters from Port Sainte-Marie. One is in clear French and dated 21 July. The other (p. 5) is a full page of numbers with no gloss. It is shown below.

Other letters from the same agent in the file were deciphered in London. Each one carries its reading written under the groups:

  • The cipher part of the letter of 23 June 1729, deciphered on 19 July (mssBLA 187 p. 3, HDL 60963).
  • The letter of 29 June, which asks for a new cipher, an address and money (HDL 60917–60920).
  • The cipher passages of the letter of 14 July (HDL 60887).
  • Two later letters, one of them dated 3 November 1729 (HDL 60907–60909).

The folder targets/newcastle1729/ covers the same document and the same key (a duplicate folder, kept in progress, with no page of its own); its checks of the table against a clear copy of the 4 August letter and against the glossed letter mssBLA 194 are recorded there.

02 The reading

Groups with no value are shown in brackets with their number, supplied words in square brackets, and words filled from the sense alone as [?word]; those count as holes.

Quand vous me [?fe]rez sçavoir, comme je [?di]s, [il s']agit [?après] le départ du [274]. Je [?lu]i ai fait [?trouver / ?tain] bon [1052] ce, sans qu'il m'ait jamais dit ce [726] je pouvois faire pour le mieux à l'accommodement. English: The [?obligation] I owe you, my Lord, is very great for your letter of the [?]. I answered it this [?sixth] of [?M]arch at the first address. When you let me know, as I say, it concerns [what follows] [?after] the departure of [?]. For Mr Keene had [told] me nothing, and there has been no order for me for [?weeks] [?] in the past affair. I [?got him] to [?find / ?“tain”] good [?] this, without his ever telling me what I could do for the best towards the accommodation. [?] I do not know your intentions, and I shall be useless in [?] ignorance. Send me then some [?order] for my [?conduct] …

The "accommodement" is the settlement between Britain and Spain that was being negotiated in the summer of 1729 and ended in the Treaty of Seville (9 November 1729). Benjamin Keene was the British envoy at Madrid. The page breaks off after the last group with a row of dots.

03 Method

  1. The page was transcribed from the HDL image: 141 groups, 102 of them distinct.
  2. The sibling letters were transcribed group by group with their glosses, giving 1,055 glossed pairs (first reported as 942; counted again from the two files on 12 November 2026) and 364 distinct values (pairs_A.tsv, pairs_B.tsv).
  3. Applying the table directly gave a glossed value to 116 of the 141 groups, and the result already formed French sentences. Counted against the 95% bar, that is 0.823.
  4. Because the code is alphabetical, an unknown group can be bracketed by its neighbours. 585 lies between 581 ie and 588 il, and gives ig-no-re and ig-no-ra-n-ce. 1210 lies between 1209 cinq and 1211 sept, so it is six. 222 lies between 218 conclure and 224 confidence, so it is conduite. On the same reasoning 275 was taken as dans, 816 ordre, 666 lu, 1118 trouver (1118 is glossed “tain” in mssBLA 194; see §04), 689 m (de m-ar-s), 460 fe (fe-re-z), 84 apres, 1152 vos and 805 obligation. None of these has a gloss behind it in the letters transcribed here, so they are marked [?] and are holes in the count: 18 tokens of 15 groups in all, counting 1250.
  5. Groups 1240–1370 appear under dashes in the glosses. 1250 is taken here as a separator, by position alone, and is also a hole.

04 What remains open

Of the 141 groups, 116 have the value glossed in the siblings (0.823). 18 tokens (15 groups) were filled from the sense and the alphabetical order of the code and count as holes. 7 groups have no value. The extent is partial (0.823): the remaining pieces have no gloss in any sibling letter transcribed here.

  • Filled from the sense (holes): 805 obligation, 585 ig (twice), 275 dans (twice), 222 conduite, 816 ordre (twice), 666 lu, 689 m, 460 fe, 84 apres, 1152 vos, 1210 six, 285 di, 711 maine, 1250 as a separator, and 1118.
  • 1118 has two values on record. Here it was filled as “trouver” from the sense, with no gloss behind it. In mssBLA 194 (HDL 60893, line 14) the clerk glossed “974 163 1118” as “reponse cer-tain”, with 1118 = “tain” in darker ink written over an earlier gloss and the first figure of 1118 retouched in the same ink (recorded in targets/newcastle1729/; the Huntington image of that line shows an overwritten gloss at that place). “Tain” is the only glossed occurrence of the group, but it does not make “je lui ai fait [1118] bon” read, and the retouching leaves open that the gloss was corrected. Neither value is adopted; the group is marked [?trouver / ?tain].
  • 6: a number, the date of Newcastle's letter. The code's numbers run 8 vingt and 11 cinquante.
  • 274: a d- word or a name, after “le départ du”.
  • 254, 937 (qu-), 1052 (s-), 726 (m-), 836 (p-): none of these is glossed in any sibling transcribed here. The duplicate folder reads the digits of 60919 line 11 as 937 = “monsieur” where this folder has 737, and 60917 line 4 as 275 = “dans” where this folder has 255; the Huntington images do not settle either digit at the size seen. It also finds glosses for 84 and 816 in other glossed letters (mssBLA 185, 179, from a transcription by another contributor, not re-checked on the images). None of these is counted here; if the two digit readings hold, 119 of 141 groups (0.844) would carry a glossed value, still under the bar.

The agent's other coded pieces in the file (mssBLA 184 and 186) carry no glosses. More of the series may hold the missing values but has not been digitised.

mssBLA 191 p. 5, agent at Port Sainte-Marie to Newcastle, c. July 1729 read in part

The agent thanks 'Milord' for his letter, says he answered on the sixth at the first address, complains that Mr Keene (British envoy at Madrid) has given him no orders for weeks about the accommodation, and asks for orders for his conduct. Written during the negotiations that led to the Treaty of Seville (Nov 1729). Read in part: 116 of 141 groups carry a glossed value; the rest is filled from the sense or open.

Builds coherent sentences, but under the read bar of 95% of words read as sense (82% here); the holes are marked in the reading.
