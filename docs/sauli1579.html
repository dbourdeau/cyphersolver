At a glanceExisting decipherment · complete

The letters
Letters of the nuncio Antonio Sauli (1579) and of Cardinal Alessandro Riario, legate to Philip II during the Portuguese succession (1580–81), with sensitive passages in figures.
Where
Archivio Apostolico Vaticano, Segreteria di Stato, Portogallo 8; DECODE R190–R194, catalogued as partially decrypted.
The cipher
Two-digit homophones for the letters, a few word groups (78 che, 84 per), and the digit 9 as a word separator.
How it was read
Almost every cipher passage has the secretariat’s decipherment written above it. The digits of R192–R194 were transcribed and decrypted with Lasry’s key; every legible passage agrees with its gloss, and five code words were added.
What it says
The Pope’s warning about a plot touching Piedmont, Italian troops raised for Ireland, and Philip II’s answer that the English enterprise must wait until Flanders is subdued.
Credit
The key and the decryption of ff. 92–101 are George Lasry’s (2020).
Still open
Some code groups (20/28, 72/82, 10) are unsettled, four-digit groups are never glossed, and one short tail on R192 p. 1 has no gloss.

The key is Lasry’s reconstruction: fixed two-digit homophones (a 00 81, e 07 83, i 05 85, o 03 87, l 25 75, u/v 01 27, one number for each other letter), a few words (78 che, 84 per, 24 quell, 14 quest, 44 ancora, 28 M.tà) and the single digit 9 as a word separator. No group begins with 9, so a digit string splits in only one way. Numbers are left as plain numerals between separators (“4 mesi”, “1300”).

02 What the ciphers say

R192, Riario. Riario spoke with the King “sopra il negotio di Piamonte”. Philip II was very pleased with the avvertimento and much obliged to His Holiness. Idiáquez then came to report that the King had taken the warning in good part. Cipher passages name the Marquis of Ayamonte, formerly governor of Milan, the Anselmi, and Ugonotti of the Dauphiné who were to help them in Italy. The King promised that nothing would disturb Italy. Further on: the Italian soldiers were to be sent with artillery, munitions and four months’ pay under the name of S.S.tà. Going on in plain danger, they would all be killed, because the Queen of England’s ships and men were waiting. The appended copy of 29 November 1580 answers the King’s ministers. The English enterprise must wait until Flanders is pacified. The Italians are embarked on the river at Lisbon with victuals for three months and are to be sent back toward Italy.

R193, Riario to the Cardinal of Como, Badajoz. The impresa d’Inghilterra: the King lacks the means and wants Flanders subdued first. It also covers galleys, Italian infantry, and 1,300 badly armed soldiers in need of “aiuti, armi et munitioni”. The third page, a clean decipherment from Elvas, says of the President that he “è pietra scandali et mal sodisfatto della Corte romana”.

R194. The Irish captains; the King needs a “gran provisione di denari” and must settle Flanders before “questa impresa”; the truce; what he has spent “per mantenimento la religione cattolica in Fiandra”.

R190 and R191. Riario writes to the Earl of Desmond about the papal soldiers in Ireland, and to the Secretary in mid-January 1581 about the massacre at Smerwick (the “distruttione di quelli poveri Italiani”), the Duke of Alba’s report on the 700 Italians, two ships to be sent to Ireland, and the Duke of Sora.

Per-passage digit strings, decrypts and glosses are in the repository (transcription_R192.md, transcription_R193_R194.md); the readings of 22 October 2026, with the clear text and the decipherments in full, are texts/R190_reading.md to R194_reading.md, with English in texts/R19*_en.md.

03 Method

The five records, their 22 page images and DECODE’s attachments were fetched. The attachments are Midas’s 2017 transcription of R190–R191, Lasry’s key and his decryption. That decryption covers only ff. 92–101, so R192–R194 had key but no text. Two image-reading passes transcribed the digits of each cipher passage on those eleven pages and copied the interlinear gloss. A table decoder applied Lasry’s key. Where a digit was ambiguous in this hand (3/5/8, 0/6, 1/7), the value that gives Italian was kept. That makes the decrypt a check on the gloss rather than an independent decryption. Groups that decrypted to nothing in the key were lined up with their glosses to recover code words.

04 What remains uncertain

  • 20 and 28 are hard to tell apart in this hand. One is glossed S.M.tà and the other Italiani, probably 28 and 20 respectively; not settled.
  • 82 is navi, settled on the re-reading (R191 f. 99r “dui navi” twice; R192 “la Regina d’Inghilterra navi”): Lasry’s “Hune” came from a misreading of the gloss. 72 has two glossed senses, Regina (R190, R192) and denari (R191, the R192 copy, R194); 60 is Duca and 77 9 00 il Marchese. The four-digit prefixes 6371, 7771, 7361 and 7761 and the code for religione cattolica (R194) have no gloss word.
  • 10 is glossed Italia three times, but once apparently V.S. Ill.ma, which may be a misread 18.
  • Four-digit groups 6371, 7771, 7361 and 7761 stand before several phrases in R193–R194 and are never glossed. They may be nulls or a code.
  • Religione cattolica in R194 p. 2 is enciphered by a short group that could not be made out.
  • On R192 p. 1 the tail after “dubbio che ha S.S.tà” (“0?9 32 0 9 75 71 63”) has no gloss and was not decrypted.
  • Letter dates: R193 p. 1’s month and R194’s date are not established; R190–R191 were not re-examined here (Lasry’s decryption stands).

R190, Portogallo 8 ff. 92r-95r, Riario to the Earl of Desmond and to Philip II, Elvas, Jan 1581

Riario to the Earl of Desmond, Elvas 12 January 1581: the papal fort in Ireland is reported lost; aid is being sought. A news note: the viceroy put the garrison to the sword without resistance. A memorial to Philip II on the Italian infantry (3,037 paid, about 1,000 fit), the offer of 2,000 under the Pope's name for 4 months, and a commissary to hand them to Desmond.

# R193, Portogallo 8 ff. 236-238: two letters of Cardinal Riario to the Cardinal of Como

## 1. Badajoz, 2 [October?] 1580

Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord. Eight days ago the Secretary, Signor Idiáquez, came in His Majesty's name to tell me
that, as I had pressed His Majesty {for the English enterprise} in His Holiness's name, His Majesty greatly wished to
please His Holiness in what was his satisfaction and the service of Christendom, but was in no position for {this
enterprise}, which needed very great {preparations and men}; and that the aids and graces offered to him in His
Holiness's name, meaning by Monsignor Nuncio (for I have never gone into particulars on this), were weak {for such an
enterprise}, which he had to put off until he was {master of Flanders}, which would be safer and easier; and meanwhile
{for Flanders} His Holiness should give him generous aid, complaining that now, on the pretext that the galleys were in
Portugal, or that the term had not ended, His Holiness was delaying the confirmation of the galley subsidy; and that I
ought to do good offices in this, showing I believed I had done the contrary; and with all that, in His Majesty's name,
he offered me {the Italians for Ireland} as Your Lordship will see in the note marked A. I answered with the hope His
Holiness had in His Majesty {for this enterprise}, with many reasons, and the hopes His Majesty had given, to me in
particular; I showed him that the {aids} they spoke of, although I had no commission for them, were of greater weight
than they made out, and that when His Holiness had seen {His Majesty's happy progress} in this business, one could
believe His Holiness would have done what he could to His Majesty's satisfaction. As for the graces, that was not my
business, and I had done neither good nor harm in it; His Holiness, most prudent and inspired by God, knew what he was
doing. As for the offer {for this Irish matter}, it had not been my negotiation, especially if there was no intention
of going on {into England}, and I would write of it to Monsignor Nuncio if they gave me time; so they sent a courier,
having had it put in writing as seen in the note marked A, and answered in writing as in mine marked B; and I wrote to
Monsignor Nuncio as in the letter marked C, who answered me with that marked D, which arrived last night, where, as
Your Lordship will see, he reasons prudently on this business, but will not take any charge {of these soldiers}, who
they tell me do not reach {1,300, and badly armed}. And I thought it right to accept that offer, but to beg His Majesty
through Idiáquez to wait for this post in eight days, so that His Holiness's mind can be better seen, pointing out that
if His Majesty means to put me in charge {of these foot}, he ought to help {those poor wretches}, his vassals {sent
there}, especially as they have made some gain and give good hopes, by sending them {aid, arms and munitions}, as
Monsignor Nuncio will have told Your Lordship, whose hands I humbly kiss, and God keep you well. From Badajoz, 2
[?October] 1580. Your Lordship's most humble and devoted servant, A. Cardinal Riario.

# R194, Portogallo 8 f. 281r-v: Cardinal Riario to the Cardinal Secretary, [late 1580 - 1581]

On the occasion of being written to and having to send {the Irish captains} to His Majesty, as Monsignor Nuncio had
written to me, I invited His Majesty to give me some good and sure intimation of his will {regarding the enterprise}
desired by His Holiness for the service of God, of the Christian religion, and all to the honour and greatness of His
Majesty; who answered that he had the best will and inclination, but that it needed {a great provision of money}, and
that His Holiness not only did not help him with new graces but denied him the usual ones, and that his real aim was to
attend {to Flanders} and then to {this enterprise}. I answered that if His Majesty wished, he could very well attend to
both with {his great forces}, and that, as I understand, it might be more convenient for the affairs {of Flanders} to
begin {with those of England}; and that as to the graces I could not speak, but I hoped His Holiness governed with the
greatest prudence and loved His Majesty cordially, and I believed that when His Majesty resolved to say and act in
earnest he could hope for every reasonable aid from His Holiness. I also tried to get from His Majesty some decision on
the business {of the truce}; he told me there was nothing yet, and that he was trying to free some man of his over there,
but that necessity pressed him, that he did not know what he would do, and that he found the whole weight {of
Christendom} on his back, and that {to maintain the Catholic religion in Flanders} he had spent an infinite amount of
money, of which, by my reckoning {of State}, he could have kept a great part had he been willing to give way {to them}
[...] thus {in religion}. I answered what seemed fitting in this case for the service of God, of His Majesty and my
duty, but could get no other answer.
