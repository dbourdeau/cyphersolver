Dirk van Hogendorp to Maarten van der Goes: the Van Suchtelen channel

→

Nationaal Archief, 2.01.08, Ministerie van Buitenlandse Zaken 1796–1810, inv. 318 · DECODE R1942 · ciphered dispatch no. 12, two pages, 286 groups

Listed as non-decrypted, with its hidden subject unknown. An 1890 biography identifies the dispatch precisely: after Vorontsov rebuffed him, Hogendorp tried to reach Emperor Alexander I through the Dutch-born Russian general Jan Pieter van Suchtelen. This resolves the content, not the exact plaintext.

Summary. The opening leaf is headed No. 12 and dated 5 July 1803. J. A. Sillem’s biography of Dirk van Hogendorp describes how the envoy, dissatisfied with Chancellor Alexander Vorontsov’s response, sought another channel to place Batavian interests before Emperor Alexander I. He approached General Jan Pieter van Suchtelen, a Dutch-born officer who commanded the Russian engineers, enjoyed the emperor’s confidence and saw him daily. Sillem’s footnote identifies the source of that paragraph as the ciphered dispatch of the same day, no. 12. The preceding footnote separately assigns dispatch no. 10 to the commercial-treaty discussion, removing the only plausible ambiguity. Authenticated DECODE access also found the matching 1803 codebook, R1035, whose marked three-digit groups have the same form as the letter. Status: the subject and argument are identified with high confidence from print, but no exact code-group alignment or diplomatic plaintext is claimed.

01 The document

Dirk van Hogendorp, the Batavian Republic’s envoy in Russia, sent the dispatch from St Petersburg to Maarten van der Goes, the Batavian agent for foreign relations. The clear address names both men and the first leaf carries the dispatch number. The rest is written as numerical groups, mostly one to three digits, distinguished by bars, ticks, dots and wave-like marks above the figures.

The numeric-only transcription has 286 groups and 218 distinct bare values. That count deliberately collapses the marks: 73 with a bar and 73 with a wave are different codebook entries. A marked transcription exists in draft, but the pale superposed signs still need a second pass against the scans.

02 The answer in Sillem, 1890

J. A. Sillem narrates Hogendorp’s first approaches in St Petersburg on pp. 151–153 of his biography. Dispatch no. 10 of 5 July supplied the argument for a Russo-Batavian commercial treaty: Russia should have an immediate interest in the Batavian Republic’s continued existence as an independent state, and concluding the treaty would demonstrate that interest.

Vorontsov was not persuaded. Sillem continues:

Hogendorp vond zijne houding althans “zo wonderlijk” dat hij naar andere kanalen zocht om den Keizer onze belangen voor te dragen. Hij meende een gelukkigen greep gedaan te hebben door de tusschenkomst in te roepen van den Generaal van Suchtelen, Nederlander van geboorte, die in russischen dienst tot Opperbevelhebber der genie was opgeklommen, en het volste vertrouwen genoot van Czaar Alexander, met wien hij dagelijks omging.

The footnote attached to that passage reads: “Rijksarchief. Dep. van denzelfden dag No. 12 gecijferd.” In English: “National Archives. Dispatch of the same day, no. 12, ciphered.” The manuscript supplies No. 12; Sillem supplies the content; and his preceding note fixes “the same day” as 5 July 1803.

03 What the hidden passage says

The secure reading is at the level of subject and argument. Vorontsov’s attitude and answer struck Hogendorp as so strange that he sought another route to the emperor. He chose Van Suchtelen because the general’s Dutch origin, senior position and daily access to Alexander made him a plausible intermediary. The object was to place the Batavian Republic’s interests—and in context its independent survival and the desired commercial treaty—before the emperor by a channel outside the chancellor.

A concise English reconstruction is:

Chancellor Vorontsov’s attitude and answer appeared so strange to me that I sought another way to bring our interests to the emperor’s attention. I believe I have found it in General Van Suchtelen, Dutch by birth, commander of the engineers in Russian service, who enjoys Emperor Alexander’s full confidence and sees him daily.

This is an editorial paraphrase of Sillem’s account, not a quotation from a deciphered text. Connectives, spelling, the exact request made of Van Suchtelen and any material Sillem omitted remain unknown.

04 The matching codebook

DECODE R1035 is catalogued as Legatie Rusland, inv. nr. 228, codebook, 1803. Its twenty images show the same system used in R1942: numerical entries extended by superposed marks, with values for letters, syllables, word parts, whole words, names and phrases. DECODE’s downloadable partial transcription contains 1,849 distinct entries.

The find confirms that the letter belongs to the documented 1803 Russian-legation system and gives the route to an exact reading. It does not by itself decode the dispatch: the CSV is partial, and the letter’s marks must be transcribed accurately before the corresponding rows can be selected.

05 Why the identification is secure

  1. Number. The leaf itself says No. 12.
  2. Date. The docket and DECODE record give 5 July 1803.
  3. Source citation. Sillem assigns the Van Suchtelen paragraph to the ciphered no. 12 of that same day.
  4. Separation from no. 10. His preceding footnote names no. 10 as the source for the commercial-treaty exchange with Vorontsov. The two dispatches cannot be conflated.
  5. System. The 1803 Russian-legation codebook R1035 matches the manuscript’s marked numerical format.

No cryptanalytic claim is needed for the identification. Its strength is bibliographical: a contemporary archive number visible on the cipher sheet is repeated in a printed account that names what the dispatch reported.

06 What remains

  • Exact marks. Every bar, tick, dot and wave over the 286 groups needs independent visual verification. The current marked transcription is preliminary.
  • Exact plaintext. R1035 must be applied group by group, with its images consulted wherever the downloadable CSV has no entry. Until then, no original wording receives an H, C, M or I reading grade.
  • Sillem’s omissions. His paragraph may compress, modernise or omit part of the dispatch. Only a complete alignment can show how much.
  • Related traffic. Dispatch no. 10 of the same day and no. 21 of 19 August frame the approach: proposal, attempted alternative channel, then Vorontsov’s definitive refusal. Comparing their code use may settle doubtful signs.
