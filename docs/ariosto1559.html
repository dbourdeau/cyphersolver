Ercole II d’Este to Claudio Ariosto, Ferrara, 23 April 1559

At a glanceCiphertext-only break · partial (89% of the cipher)

The letter
Ercole II d’Este, duke of Ferrara, to his secretary Claudio Ariosto, Ferrara, 23 April 1559, with a postscript. The letter is in clear Italian, and the passages about the countess, her husband and their affairs are set in cipher.
Where
Biblioteca Estense Universitaria, Modena, Autografoteca Campori, Lettera E, Este (d’), Ercole II d’Este, duca di Ferrara, cc. 39–41 (IIIF manifest 8d50f9af-95d8-405e-a3a4-f66933e69af1). Catalogue 2159 here, from WAINUT’s sweep of the Estense collection. No DECODE record, no entry at Cryptiana, no edition found.
The cipher
Consonant signs of Greek and Latin shape, each followed by a small raised digit that gives the vowel: 2 o, 3 i, 4 e, 5 a, 6 a, 7 e, 8 i, 9 o, 1 u. A raised ring doubles the consonant, a dot is a null, & is qu, and a few code numbers under a bar stand for words. About 1,830 signs and raised marks in 77 cipher lines.
How it was deciphered
Brice Dudout froze a protocol and the transcription with SHA-256 hashes, held out 22.1% of the signs, learned the key by annealing on the rest and measured it once: 0.605 of the held-out letters fall in Italian words of five letters or more, against 0.155 for the best of 200 shuffled keys. The keys of four neighbouring Este ciphers do not fit.
What it says
A countess asks the duke to pay her debts out of arrears of censi (rents). He refuses those that fell due during her husband’s rebellion, “a causa della sua ribellione non s’ha da haver nulla”, and offers to pay the future ones to her rather than to him. The peace of 1559 will lessen the husband’s credit.
Still open
39 of 402 cipher words are unresolved, mostly in five stretches where both blind transcriptions read the same unreadable shapes; the code numbers 22, 24 and 25–27; and who the countess, her husband and the count are.
Credit
Brice Dudout (Europe chiffrée), working with Claude Opus 5.5 agents: the blind transcription, the key, its validation and the reading. WAINUT found the letter in the Estense collection (catalogue lead 2159, PR #242). Merged here in pull request #277.

01 The letter

The duke is Ercole II d’Este of Ferrara (1508–1559), and the letter is headed Hercules Dux Ferrarie. It is written to Claudio Ariosto, his secretary, on 23 April 1559, with a postscript on cc. 40–41. The duke’s clear sentences give the frame; the cipher is set into them where the matter is delicate. A countess has claimed arrears of rents from the duke and wants them used for her debts. The duke tells Ariosto what to say to her, and asks him about two other matters.

Runs of cipher stand on cc. 39r, 39v, 40r, 40v and 41r. No decipherment is kept with them. The leaf belongs to the Autografoteca Campori, a collection of autograph letters, and the Estense records it only as “Lettera cifrata” with its postscript. Ercole II’s letter of 28 May 1559 (Lettera E, cc. 50–51) is in a different cipher with its own target (targets/ercole1559, not yet written up), and a letter of 15 July 1559 in Ariosto’s own hand has a sign that is not in this one; this key does not read it.

02 The cipher

Every group is a sign and, mostly, a small digit written above it. The sign is a consonant and the digit is the vowel that follows it, so one sign with its digit makes a syllable. The signs look Greek or Latin (alpha, omega, lambda, theta, kappa, upsilon, sigma, pi, tau, chi, phi), a few figures are used as signs (8 and 1 for l, 3 for a), and most vowels have two digits.

The key is not that of Ercole II’s letter of 28 May 1559 (targets/ercole1559), nor Francesco d’Este 1546, Bresegno 1548 or Alfonso II 1576. Each was applied once to the training lines before any learning and none reads this letter.

03 The key and its test

Protocol. Brice Dudout froze the protocol, the measuring script and a blind transcription (shapes only, no values) with SHA-256 hashes before any key work. A held-out set H was fixed by line references and excluded in code from learning: lines 40v:08–14, 40vB and 41r, 405 of the 1,832 signs (22.1%).

Learning. The key was learned by annealing with a character five-gram Italian model on the other lines only, with the raised digits constrained to vowels. Before the measurement two hand rules were added and recorded: a dot is a null and & is qu; then the raised ring doubles the preceding consonant. The key was frozen.

One measurement. The score M5 is the share of decoded letters covered by Italian words of five letters or more. On H it was 0.605. The best of 200 keys made by permuting the key’s values scored 0.155, the median 0.038, a gap of 0.451. The thresholds fixed in advance (0.60, and 0.30 above the best control) were met. There is no contemporary decipherment, so there is no character score.

A change after the test. The measured key read the double loop as n. Reading by sense showed it is f (satisfare, far, prefata, fosse, favore, figliolo, Farnesi, and some ten more places). That change is outside the test, which held with the wrong value, and it is the only difference between the measured key and the reading key. Two blind transcriptions by AI agents agree on 87.7% of tokens; on the nine places where the word depends on the difference Brice Dudout answered a pre-registered shape sheet of thumbnails with no values shown: all six double-loop places are that sign, all three of the qoppa-like sign likewise.

A test that failed. The frozen key, unchanged, was applied once to WAINUT’s transcription of Ariosto’s letter of 15 July 1559 (M5 0.189, best control 0.294) and once to a copy made from the image (0.271 against 0.204). It does not read it; that letter is another table, and nothing is claimed for it. Catalogue 2160, Ercole II to Claudio of 8 January 1547 (cc. 16–17), is also in another table, and no key was frozen for it.

04 The reading

Words written in cipher only, counted by script: of 402, 262 are certain, 95 probable, 6 restored from the sense and 39 unresolved. Certain and probable together give 357 words, 88.8%; with the restored words, 90.3%. The 95% bar is not reached. One of the certain words, satisfare (39r:05), begins with a sign read as t in both passes; it is kept certain as pre-registered and flagged. The cipher words construe as Italian sentences inside the clear letter, with gaps; one AI reader graded it B (coherent but raw), and no human palaeographer has checked the transcription or the reading.

Et perché ci scrivete che detta […] la contessa, doppo d’essersi doluta de’ infiniti mali portamenti usateli dal suo consorte, né solo verso lei ma anco del figliolo […]
And because you write to us that the said […] the countess, after complaining of her husband’s countless ill-treatments of her, and not of her alone but of their son […]
[…] a causa della sua ribellione non s’ha da haver nulla; si ché no accade che facia fondamento su li censi passati in pagar […] i debiti, ma si trovi maniera su li futuri
[…] because of her husband’s rebellion nothing is to be had; so she need not build on the past rents to pay the debts, but let a way be found on the future ones
  • The countess’s debts. She wants the duke to have her debts paid out of her husband’s goods, and asks that they be paid from the arrears of the censi, not the future rents. The duke says he does not know which she means; he owes her no censo, only to restore what is hers. He had asked that the rents be held back so they could be disposed of as she wished, but if they were to pay her debts, after her husband had so many false complaints made against him at the court of Philip II, there is no appearance of honesty in it.
  • The rebellion. She may think he owes the rents that fell due during her husband’s rebellion; Ariosto is to undeceive her, because of the rebellion nothing is owed. A way may be found on the future rents, which he would pay far more willingly for her than for her husband.
  • The husband. She fears she must soon go to law against him, as she would have done before but for her honour and the harm to their son; the duke did not dissuade her. With this peace, in which he will be less needed, her husband will also have less favour if the suit begins. A favourite of his, Iacomo, is to have 4,000 ducats; the duke asks whether it is true. The count, her son-in-law, is to intercede, but the countess hopes little from it.
  • Other news. The duke wants word of the rivalry between […] and Don Cesare, the marquis, and what will be done in their academies; and asks Ariosto to see whether a friend would act as of himself, since he has no commission from his masters, with a closing remark about the Farnese.

The peace is presumably Cateau-Cambrésis (April 1559). The countess, her husband and the count are not identified. “Don Cesare” may be Cesare Gonzaga, count of Guastalla, and the unresolved dastalla of 41r:01 may be Guastalla; “il marchese” may be the marquis of Pescara. These are hypotheses, not counted and not checked.

05 What remains uncertain

  • 39 unresolved words in 39r:07–08, 39r:19–21, 40r:12–16, 40v:02–04 and 40v:12–13. They read the same in both blind passes, so a third reading at full resolution by a human reader, or a clear minute, is needed.
  • Code numbers. 22, 24 and the pair 25–27 occur once each, with no context that fixes them; 23 is restored as Sua Maestà Catolica from the sense at both places.
  • Six restored words, not counted in the 88.8%: ella (39r:05), apparenza (39v:06), lei (40r:03), causa (40v:02), non (40v:03), certo (40v:05).
  • The transcription is by AI agents only. No human palaeographer has checked it. Where the two passes differ and the reading depends on it, nine places were settled by the shape sheet.
  • The people. The Archivio di Stato di Modena (Cancelleria, Ambasciatori; Ariosto’s 1559 correspondence), where a clear minute could exist, was not searched. No edition or study of this letter was found in five web searches, DECODE or Cryptiana.

cc. 39-41 (39r, 39v, postscript 40r-41r): cipher runs in a clear letter read in part

A countess claims arrears of censi owed by the duke; he refuses those due at the time of her husband's rebellion and offers to pay the future ones to her rather than to the husband, who had false complaints made against him at Philip II's court; she thinks of suing her husband; a favourite, Iacomo, is to have 4,000 ducats; the peace lessens the husband's credit.

Builds coherent sentences, but under the read bar of 95% of words read as sense (89% here); the holes are marked in the reading.

Conventions of the word list below (one line per cipher line; only the words written in cipher):

- a bare word is **certain**: given as it stands by the reading key on the first or the second blind transcription, clear sense;
- `word~` is **probable**: a choice between the two passes, a systematic shape correction (upsilon t/v, omega c/v), an abbreviation, or a raised-letter value;
- `word!` is **restored**: a letter changed against both passes, justified by the sense (not counted in the 88.8%);
- `[?xxx]` is **unresolved** (the decoded letters are shown);
- a word cut at a line end (`capa-` / `ce`) is counted once;
- a `1` stuck to a code number ({25}{27}1, {23}1) is taken as part of the code and not counted.

# ariosto1559 - English translation (working translation of reading.md)

Read in part: 88.8% of the cipher words without restorations. `[…]` = unresolved; `{nn}` = code number not resolved. The censi are annual rents (here owed by the duke to the countess's family).

As to what we ordered about her debts, which she would like us to have paid out of her husband's goods: the answer His Holiness gave
you {22} […], knowing she would not be satisfied unless she spoke with her husband. She asks us to have those debts paid, not the
future rents (censi) but the arrears. Tell her that, wishing to please her, we had her told what we thought: that she could act to
get what she wanted without her husband being able, with justice, to complain of us to His Catholic Majesty; since she will not, let
her show us another way to satisfy her. As for past censi, we do not know which she means: she is owed no census by us, only that we
restore what is hers. We wrote the other day that the censi should be held back so that they could be disposed of as she wished; if
they were to pay Her Ladyship's debts, she can well see that, after he had so many false complaints made against us at the court of
{23}, there is no appearance of honesty in it. (Postscript) She complained of her husband's countless ill-treatments of her and of their
son, and fears she must soon go to law against him, as she would have done before but for her honour {24} and the harm to her son: we
did not dissuade her. Show her that with this peace, in which he will be less needed, he will also have less favour if the suit begins.
She perhaps thinks we owe her the censi that fell due during her husband's rebellion: undeceive her; because of the rebellion nothing is
owed. Let her not build on the past censi to pay the debts; let a way be found on the future ones, which we will pay far more willingly
for her than for her husband. Tell us whether it is true that Iacomo, her husband's favourite, is to have the 4,000 ducats he wrote of.
As to the good office of the count her son-in-law with her husband, the countess hopes little from it: do not speak of it to the count
otherwise. Tell us about the rivalry between […] and Don Cesare […] the marquis, and what will be done in their academies. As to […],
see whether the friend would do anything as of himself, since he has no commission from his masters […] of the Farnese.
