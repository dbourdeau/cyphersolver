Baudouin and Jean de Neufville, Paris–Amsterdam, 1779–1780

Go to the full text ↓

An Amsterdam merchant and the secretary of the French Navy minister wrote to each other in a private number code; rebuilt from the merchant’s own drafts, it now opens most of three passages he never glossed.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 24 November 2026 · updated 25 November 2026

The letters
De Neufville’s drafts of April–May 1779, with code numbers written over his French, and letters from Paris of February 1779 to November 1781 signed “Baudouin” and later “Dunoyer”, addressed to “Monsieur Van Temmel” at de Neufville’s house, with code groups inside clear French.
Where
US National Archives, RG 360, Papers of the Continental Congress, microfilm M332 roll 4, file unit 464816476 (“Letters from William Lee to John de Neufville and Son”, 808 images), pp. 487–609. Not on DECODE.
The cipher
Numbers from 1 to about 550, one number for a letter, syllable, common word or name, with several numbers for common units (re = 30, 229, 310; que = 22, 23, 278). Neither of the two tables mentioned in the letters survives as a table.
How it was deciphered
The code was rebuilt from de Neufville’s drafts, where he wrote the numbers over his own words, and from his glosses on Baudouin’s letters. Keys built from some drafts predicted the others: 139 of 146 covered groups, 64 of 65 and 64 of 66, against shuffled keys that reach at most 15.
What it says
Paris news for the Pensionary of Amsterdam, and in January 1780 a warning that a loan nobody will put money into “ne peut que faire beaucoup de tort au crédit du [369/349]”.
Still open
Numbers 3, 58, 227, 369/349, 507, 501 and 162, 30 where the word wants g, four likely nulls, and two unglossed lines of p. 527. None is glossed anywhere in the file.
Credit
Andrew Beling, with AI agents (13–15 November 2026): the images found, the code rebuilt and tested, the leaf evidence for “Dunoyer” = Baudouin, and the bare passages decoded. Readings reviewed by Brice Dudout (assisted by Claude). The identification of the correspondent is the 19th-century index-maker’s.

01 The file

The papers of Jean de Neufville & fils, bound in the Department of State and now among the Papers of the Continental Congress, hold a private correspondence in a numeric code between de Neufville in Amsterdam and a correspondent in Paris:

  • De Neufville’s drafts, April–May 1779 (pp. 591–609), headed “Mad. d’Arras” or “Chaumont”: plain French with code numbers written over the syllables, and one enciphered fair draft (pp. 604–606) of the letter of 19 April 1779 whose plain text is on p. 599. About 1,100 numbers.
  • Letters from Paris (pp. 487–590), signed “Baudouin” and later “Dunoyer”. De Neufville wrote the French over many of the groups (about 380 numbers), but not over all: about 90 numbers on pp. 491–492, 527 and 573 stand bare.

Who the correspondent is. The volume’s own index (p. 3, a 19th-century hand) lists him as “Du Noyer, Bowdoin or Mad. D. Arras”. The letters of 23 February and 7 March 1779 are signed “Baudouin”; the second ends “à l’avenir je signeray un des noms que je prends dans le petit chiffre cy joint”, and from 30 March the letters in the same hand are signed “Dunoyer”. The Papers of Benjamin Franklin call Baudouin Sartine’s secretary. That “Dunoyer” is Baudouin rests on the file alone: the index, the signatures, the announced change of name and a hand judged the same by eye (by an AI model, not a person).

Two tables. On 7 March 1779 Baudouin wrote that he was making a “chiffre” and meanwhile sent a “petit chiffre” of cover names; on 30 March he acknowledged “le chiffre” that had come with de Neufville’s letter. The numeric code of the drafts is probably the one de Neufville sent; who composed it is not shown.

02 Rebuilding the code

De Neufville enciphered his letters from his plain drafts and wrote the numbers over the words, so each draft is a set of number–syllable pairs. His glosses on Baudouin’s letters give more pairs. The key was built in rounds by majority vote over these pairs and tested each time on drafts it had not seen:

TestKeyResult200 shuffled keys
T1: the fair draft pp. 604–605 from drafts pp. 600–603 and 609K1, 139 numbers139 of 146 covered groups as in the plain draft p. 599mean 1.8, max 15
T2: ten p. 599 pairs held outK1 + other p. 599 pairs8 of 10mean 0.21, max 3
T3: the draft of 6 May 1779 (p. 608) held outK2, 170 numbers64 of 65 covered groupsmean 0.6, max 7
T4: the glossed letter of 10 April 1779 (pp. 529–531) held outK4, 205 numbers64 of 66 covered groupsmean 0.6, max 6

The misses are de Neufville’s own variants or word forms. All three first tests were rerun at the integration of 14 November 2026 with the same results, and the key files rebuilt byte for byte. Two limits: each test script builds its key from a pair file and scores it in the same run, so the files cannot show that a key was frozen before its test; and T2 is weak, because its held-out numbers all occur among the training pairs. The statistical validation of the key passes (z 9.1 against random keys).

Names are among the code’s entries: 5 and 106 Le Ray de Chaumont, 529 Vergennes, 328 the Minister of Marine, 444, 511 and 213 the Pensionary of Amsterdam, 508 the Stadholder, 500 Mr York, 417 La Vauguyon, 464 the States General, 103 the Amsterdammers.

03 The bare passages

Three passages carry numbers that nobody glossed. In the readings, [n] is a hole and {x} a unit set by the word, not the key. The count is strict: an open number or a unit set against the key is a hole.

24 January 1780 (p. 573). 13 of 18 coded words. “Il est certain que le patriarche et la bande joyeuse feront tous leurs efforts pour faire avaler la dernière pilule, mais il est tout aussi certain que cela couvrira les États Généraux d’opprobre et de ridicule. Il y a longtemps que [3] a retiré aux [58]rands l’affaire de l’emprunt … d’ouvrir un emprunt où personne ne se soucie de [227]er son ar{g}ent, cela ne peut que faire beaucoup de tort au crédit du [369/349].” He advises de Neufville to speak to “M. du Fresne”, whom de Neufville glosses elsewhere as Chaumont. That the loan is the American loan is inference; “Grands” for [58]rands and “Congrès” for [369/349] are guesses from context and are not counted.

18 January 1780 (p. 491). 10 of 16 coded words: on the Stadholder, and on V. Vort, who boasts at court “de son savoir-faire et de ses succès, qu’il ne doit qu’à Monsieur le Pensionnaire des Amsterdammois”. 507 and 501 are open; 405, 387, 390 and 447 are probably nulls (each occurs in de Neufville’s fair draft only where his own plain draft has nothing), which is not proved.

Its second page (p. 492). 2 of 4 coded words: “On a déjà proposé plusieurs fois à ma tante d’acheter son vaisseau” (143 = vaisseau, from a faint gloss on p. 534), and “du tripotage au sujet de ce [162] entre V. Vort et {G}rand”.

Two lines of 9 April 1779 (p. 527) decode to syllables (one names Vergennes) but do not read.

Together: 25 of 38 coded words in the three read passages. “Ma tante”, “le saint homme”, “V. Vort”, “le patriarche” and “l’ami du cœur” are covers written in clear and are not identified.

04 What remains uncertain

  • Open numbers: 3 (a person), 58 (an initial), 227 (“plac-”, “hasard-” or “risqu-”), 369 or 349 (the middle digit is this hand’s 6 or its looped 4), 507, 501, 162, and 30 where “argent” and “Grand” want g but the drafts give “re”. Every glossed letter in the file was transcribed and every open number retried; none is glossed with a value that fits.
  • Likely nulls: 405, 387, 390, 447.
  • No deciphered copy of the bare passages is in the file (all 808 images paged). Baudouin’s office copies or de Neufville’s sent letters may survive in the Archives nationales, Marine series B4 and B7, which were not seen.
  • “Dunoyer” = Baudouin needs a person to compare the hand with Baudouin’s signed notes to Franklin (American Philosophical Society) or his office papers.

All image readings were made by AI agents; no person has checked them against the manuscripts.

I have the honour of yours of the 17th, Sir. It is certain that the patriarch and the merry band will do all they can to make [them] swallow the last pill, but it is just as certain that this will cover the States General with shame and ridicule.
It is a long time since [3] took the loan business away from the [58]rands, and I believe that since he saw you had met obstacles there he has given it up, all the more because to open a loan in which nobody cares to [227] his money can only do great harm to the credit of the [369/349].

Leerding sur le compte du Stadhouder [507] [501] [405 387 390 447] Stadhouder, et je suis persuadé que l'ami du cœur est toujours l'âme de toute cette bande joyeuse.
Ce qui me fâche dans tout cela, c'est l'indiscrétion que V. Vort a d'afficher son intimité avec le respectable M. Leerding, et il se pare à la cour de son savoir-faire et de ses succès, qu'il ne doit qu'à Monsieur le Pensionnaire des Amsterdammois.
D'ailleurs ces liaisons fournissent des prétextes à l'ami du cœur, et je suis convaincu que les affaires de notre associé en eussent bien mieux été, si cette intimité fût toujours restée secrète. Translation

B491_en.txt

I received only the day before yesterday, my dear Sir, the letter you did me the honour to write on the 31st of last month. I think exactly as Mr Leerding does about the Stadholder [507] [501] [...] Stadholder, and I am persuaded that the "friend of the heart" is still the soul of this whole merry band.
What vexes me in all this is the indiscretion of V. Vort in parading his intimacy with the respectable Mr Leerding; and he prides himself at court on his skill and his successes, which he owes only to Monsieur the Pensionary of the Amsterdammers.
Besides, these connections give the "friend of the heart" pretexts, and I am convinced that our partner's business would have gone much better if this intimacy had always stayed secret. But with a fellow, a vain little fool like V. Vort, one had to expect that he would sacrifice the good of the thing to his vanity.
