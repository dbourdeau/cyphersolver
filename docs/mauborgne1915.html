J. O. Mauborgne’s 1915 challenge cipher, Manila, 21 October 1915

A lieutenant’s 499-letter challenge to the Army Signal School, with no solution on record until now, reads as Article 29 of the Hague Convention on spies under a running key from the Engineer Field Manual, with ten slips unexplained.

Daniel Bourdeau

At a glanceCiphertext-only break · partial (89 of 97 words)

The cipher
A challenge cipher of 499 letters in 100 groups, sent from Manila on 21 October 1915 by First Lieutenant J. O. Mauborgne, 8th Infantry, to the Director of the Army Signal School at Fort Leavenworth, who had it posted there on 13 December 1915. Two sealed envelopes, the plaintext and the method, were to be opened months later; neither is known.
Where
The original sheet is lost. Louis Kruh found it among Army Signal School papers in the New York Public Library and printed it in 1993; the library could not find it again. No image is known.
The system
A running key with odd shifts: each plaintext letter is moved back by an odd number of places set by the key letter under it, so every cipher letter has the opposite alphabet parity to its plaintext letter. The key text is the first two paragraphs of Part V, p. 357, of the Engineer Field Manual, with “Field fortification” replaced by “It”.
How it was deciphered
By the contributor’s record, an AI model working at his direction found the key text and the plaintext from the ciphertext alone on 11 November 2026. Checked here with a second decoder: the rule reproduces 489 of 499 letters, and 495 positions have an odd shift.
What it says
The first 499 letters of Article 29 of the Regulations annexed to Hague Convention IV (1907) on who is to be considered a spy, ending mid-sentence at “for” where the key text ends.
Still open
89 of 97 words come out of the key uncorrected (91.8%), under the 95% bar. Ten letters disagree with the rule and are not explained. The order of events rests on the contributor’s word, and nobody here has compared the transcription with the printed Kruh or Bauer letters.

01 The document

The cipher is dated Manila, 21 October 1915, with a covering letter of 23 October, from First Lieutenant J. O. Mauborgne of the 8th Infantry to the Director of the Army Signal School at Fort Leavenworth. It has 499 letters, 99 groups of five and a last group of four, with a dash printed after it. Mauborgne meant two sealed envelopes: the plaintext was to be opened after three months, the method a month later. The school circulated the challenge on 13 December 1915; no record of a submitted solution has been found, and the school’s report for 1915–16 does not mention the envelopes (Altifina).

The sheet itself is lost. Louis Kruh found it, with the covering letter, among Army Signal School papers in the New York Public Library around 1991 and printed it in The Cryptogram (1991) and Cryptologia 17(2), 1993, pp. 172–174; when Craig Bauer later asked for a scan for Unsolved! (2017, pp. 351–352), the library could not find it. No image of the original is known, so none is shown here. The ciphertext used is the text of Futility Closet (14 March 2022), which follows Kruh’s printing and is the same 499 letters as on Altifina’s research page (checked letter for letter). The contributor says two AI sessions found it identical to Bauer’s printing; neither he nor anyone here compared the printed letters of Kruh or Bauer by eye.

Altifina’s page (25 August 2026) found the letter frequencies flat for English text (index of coincidence 4.30%, measured as 0.043 here; R occurs 47 times and Z 33 times) and called the cipher unsolved; its hypotheses did not include a running key.

02 The cipher

With A = 0, a plaintext letter p is enciphered as c = p − (2 (k mod 13) + 1) mod 26, where k is the key letter. The shift is always odd: the key letters A and N give 1, B and O give 3, and so on up to M and Z, which give 25. To decipher, add the shift. Because every shift is odd, a cipher letter always lies on the opposite side of the alphabet’s even/odd split from the plaintext letter; in the ciphertext, 333 of the 498 neighbouring pairs of letters alternate in that way (about 249 are expected by chance).

The key is a running text. It is, by the contributor, paragraphs 1 and 2 of Part V (Field Fortification), p. 357, of the Engineer Field Manual (Professional Papers of the Corps of Engineers No. 29), “Fortification is the art of increasing by engineering devices the fighting power of troops” and so on to “at or before the close of the campaign”, with “Field fortification” at the head of paragraph 2 replaced by “It”. That edit is the contributor’s choice. It makes the two paragraphs run exactly 499 letters, the length of the message, which would explain why the message stops mid-sentence. Mauborgne’s own statement of his method (the second envelope) is lost, so it is not known that this was his rule or his key.

03 How the key was found

This account is the contributor’s. Its order of events rests on his record only (one issue, his own timestamps and screenshots, nothing from outside seen here).

Jacob Houtrouw had an AI model work at his direction on 11 November 2026. A test plan the model drafted and he approved was sealed before any decryption code touched the ciphertext (a SHA-256 hash of the plan, sealed at 15:28:38 UTC by the session’s own clock). The model’s own forecast of success was 2%. Exploratory statistics showed adjacent cipher letters alternating in alphabet parity, which points to shifts that are always odd. A search for an English plaintext and an English key text through 338 candidate tables ranked the real text first of 41, about 20 standard deviations above the average of the decoys, and planted controls were recovered. The key text was traced afterwards to the manual and the plaintext to Hague Convention IV. He sent the result to Craig Bauer that afternoon and published it at 5:01 PM US Mountain time the same day (the platform dates the post 12 November); his screenshot of the live post is his record of the time. The hash list of his files is said to be anchored by OpenTimestamps in a Bitcoin block (not checked here), and the language-model data files of the search (about 60 MB) are available on request and were not seen, so the search was not re-run here.

The ciphertext supplied the only link: no plaintext, crib or key for this item was known beforehand and neither text had been tied to this cipher before, so the method is recorded as key recovered from ciphertext-only. The search drew on a set of candidate texts, which is why the page says the key text and the plaintext were found by search, not derived.

04 What was checked here

The project wrote its own decoder from the rule (decode.py in the target folder, standard library only) and ran it on the strings in the issue. Before the strings were sent, the project had already tried the public Altifina text against Article 29 and found an odd shift at 495 of 499 positions, with the implied key beginning “FORTIFICATION IS THE ART”.

The ten letters that disagree are at positions 69, 88, 122, 194, 238, 255, 397, 443, 457 and 462. At four of them (122, 238, 443, 462) no key letter at all can give the printed cipher letter: those are slips in the cipher letters, Mauborgne’s, a copyist’s or a printer’s. At the others a different key letter could, so they may be slips in the cipher, in the key text of the edition he used, or in the printing. None of the ten is explained.

The key text against the manual. The contributor’s screenshot is of the fourth (1912) edition, p. 357, in a copy bound with the 1916 addenda (Google Books); it was not seen here. The third (revised) edition of 1909, which he had not seen, is on the Internet Archive; its page 357 opens Part V, and its two paragraphs, with “Field fortification” replaced by “It”, give exactly the 499 letters of the key text, with no difference. A 1917 printing carries the same two paragraphs. The text of Article 29 was compared with the Yale Avalon Project’s copy of the 1907 convention. It reads “the hostile party” and “who have penetrated” where the cipher reads “a hostile party” and “who having penetrated”, and it spells endeavours and despatches; which English printing Mauborgne had was not looked at.

05 The reading

The reading, with the spellings the cipher letters give. A word in braces carries one wrong letter, read from the sense.

A person can only be considered a spy when acting clandestinely or on false pretenses, he obtains or {endeavors} to obtain information in the zone of {operations} of a belligerent, with the intention of communicating it to a hostile party. {Thus}, soldiers not wearing a disguise who having {penetrated} into the zone of operations of the hostile army, for the purpose of obtaining information, are not considered spies. Similarly, the following are not considered spies: soldiers and {civilians}, carrying out their mission openly, entrusted with {the} delivery of {dispatches} intended either for their own army or for …

This is Article 29 of the Regulations annexed to Hague Convention IV of 1907, in a printing whose wording is close to the treaty text. The cipher stops at “for” because the key text ends; the article goes on “the enemy’s army”. The plaintext is English, so there is no translation, and its content says nothing about Mauborgne’s own business.

The count. The plaintext has 97 words. 89 come out of the key with no correction, 91.8%. Eight words each carry one wrong letter read from the sense, so by this site’s rule they do not count as read: 8.2% are holes. The two spelling differences (positions 69 and 457) are not holes, because the cipher letters give pretenses and dispatches and the words read as printed. 91.8% is under the 95% needed for a complete extent, so the extent is partial and the class is read in part. The contributor’s own figure is by letters: 489 of 499 (98.0%), or 491 of 499 with the two American spellings; by letters with those spellings the same count gives 98.4%. This page uses the word count only.

06 What remains uncertain

  • The ten slips. Eight words each need one letter read from the sense, and two more letters (69, 457) are spelling differences. Whether each slip is in Mauborgne’s sheet, in Kruh’s printing or in the key text of his edition cannot be told without the lost sheet or the printed letters.
  • The printed letters of Kruh (1993) and Bauer (2017) were not compared by eye with the transcription by anyone in this record: the contributor’s comparison with Bauer was made by two AI sessions, and both books are in copyright and were not scanned. The Chaocipher Clearing House reports that Bauer lists inaccuracies in Kruh’s articles and thinks Kruh may have introduced errors into the transcription of this cipher; that is one possible source of the slips, and it was not tested.
  • The key edit. “Field fortification” replaced by “It” is a contributor choice. The second envelope is lost, so Mauborgne’s own rule and key text are not confirmed; the 1912 page number rests on the contributor’s screenshot, and the 1909 edition (checked here) carries the same wording on its p. 357.
  • The search that found the texts was not re-run here: the language-model data files were not seen. The order of events (sealed plan, forecast, hash anchor, release at 5:01 PM MT on 11 November 2026) rests on the contributor’s record. What the project checked is the result, not the discovery.
  • The printing of Article 29. “a hostile party” and “who having penetrated” are not the wording of the Avalon copy; which English printing Mauborgne used was not looked at.
  • Prior art. Kruh, Bauer, the Chaocipher Clearing House and Altifina treat the cipher as unsolved, and a short web search here found no earlier solution; Kruh’s and Bauer’s books were not read.
