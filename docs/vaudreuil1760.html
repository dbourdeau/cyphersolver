01 The letter

Pierre de Rigaud de Vaudreuil de Cavagnial was the last governor of New France. By the middle of August 1760 the colony had one town left: Montréal, with the British closing on it from the lake, the river and below Québec. On 13 August he sent the minister two kinds of letter. The clear ones, in the same volume, report the movements of the enemy on lake Champlain. This one is in number groups, because, he says, it holds “des particuliarités dont il seroit dangereux que les ennemis fussent instruits dans ce moment critique”.

The catalogue describers had left it as a title only: “Lettre chiffrée … au ministre”. The ciphertext runs from f.147r to f.152r and ends with the clear formula “Je suis avec un très profond respect, Monsieur, votre très humble et très obéissant serviteur, Vaudreuil”. The scans are microfilm images 1125 pixels wide, and the last group of every line on a left-hand page is cut off by the binding.

02 The cipher and the key

Two thousand four hundred and twenty-four groups, of 395 distinct numbers, with 35 words in clear (names, titles, “Monseigneur”, the date and the closing formula). The numbers run from 1 to 700, so the table is a nomenclator: whole words (pour, les, poudre), names of places and ships, syllables and single letters for what is not in the table. The commonest items have several numbers (de 309 and 642, t 51, i 661, s 235). Spelling is phonetic: “vi-t-ma-it-res” for maîtres.

The key was in the volume that the catalogue entry pointed to. COL C11E 10 ff.285–290v, “Chiffre ou code de Montcalm et de Vaudreuil” (New France Archives 15213, ten images), holds three tables, each as an alphabetical sheet and a numerical sheet: two for Vaudreuil and the Marine in 1757, one for Montcalm in 1758. The numerical sheet that reads this letter is the second of Vaudreuil’s, e001295274, labelled Déchiffrant. The first, e001295272, gives nonsense. All 700 entries of e001295274 were transcribed from enlarged crops of the cells.

The same table reads Vaudreuil’s three cipher letters of April 1759 in COL C11A 104 (catalogue items 1388–1390: whole letters of 3,637, 1,225 and 354 groups), which were transcribed and decoded here as French with it, so it was his working table from 1757 to August 1760. Those letters are not part of this write-up; they are decoded, and no one has yet built their text into sentences. A fourth cipher letter of that volume, by Trémais (6 November 1759), does not use the table and is a different code.

03 What Vaudreuil says

The whole French reading is under Full texts below, with an English translation. In outline:

The ships at Restigouche. Three French ships with effects and troops are bound for Restigouche under M. Danjac (François-Gabriel d’Angeac), and the British have overwhelming force at that post. Vaudreuil hopes Danjac will send the frigate Machault, under the Sieur de La Giraudais, past the blockade by night with his despatches; the schooner l’Extravagante, wintering in the baie des Chaleurs, carries the duplicates. M. de Villemont, who had leave to carry the first news, has been sent back by Danjac to report.

The war on the river. “Nous sommes sans marine ni même rien d’approchant.” The British fleet has passed lake Saint-François; twenty-two British ships have passed Jacques-Cartier, and it is announced that ships laden with the rest of the troops of Louisbourg are already at Cap Rouge. In the north the British pay the Canadians for what they take, make speeches, and warn that the houses of absent men will be burned. Vaudreuil has no food to gather the militia, and the French vessels on lakes Champlain and Ontario cannot match the enemy’s.

No powder, no food, no money. The allies tire of the colony’s want. The English will have some forty thousand men against battalions none of which is half what it should be; the colony has only “dix[…] milliers” of powder (between ten and nineteen thousand pounds; the end of the figure is lost in the binding), which would be used up fast. Murray’s circular to the militia captains, a copy of which is enclosed, was written to discredit the bills of exchange and the paper money that Bigot and Vaudreuil had tried to protect; the munitionnaire and Vaudreuil himself offer bills on their own pay to reassure the habitants, and the Intendant and Lévis follow his example.

The request. “Je vous … Monseigneur, de vouloir favorablement assurer Sa Majesté que si nous succombons ce ne sera qu’à la dernière extrémité.” With the stores waiting at Restigouche he could take the troops to a retreat and hold it as long as possible; without them he cannot. The clear letters beside this one in COL C11A 105 confirm the cast: the Machault, the Bienfaisant and the Marquis de Malauze with the troops, d’Angeac, La Giraudais, Villemont, the Extravagante, Bigot, Amherst.

04 Method

The letter was transcribed from enlarged crops, one token per group, with a mark for digits lost in the binding, then decoded with tools/decode.py from the 700-cell table plus a file of context corrections. The first decode read as French at once. Where a sentence would not form, the group was looked at again on the image. The table was then corrected from the letter, 33 cells in all, each with its reason in key/overrides.json: long s read as f, l as t, c as e and b as h were the usual errors in reading the table, and two cells were settled on the alphabetical sheet e001295269 (rotated and enlarged: moyen 473, ton 152, bateau 671; cell 2 is campagne, not compagnie, which is 102).

Three passages gave way to the sense, not to the table. A small line written between two lines of f.150v has a caret and is to be read inside the line below; read in place it made nonsense, and a second 51 that the transcription carried there is not on the image. “qui~ | z e diver s” is quinze diverses with the last group of the line lost, and “je~ | i s bou r g on” is [Lou]isbourg, on. Twenty-five groups cut by the binding were restored from their visible leading digits and the sense; they are counted separately (97.1% with them, 96.0% without).

No control was run, because the key was found and not recovered from the ciphertext. The check is that a 700-entry table made for another year reads six folios of French, with the right ships, officers and enemy commanders in the right places, and reads two other letters of 1759 as well.

05 What remains uncertain

Of 2,424 groups, 2,328 (96.0%) read from the image as written, 25 (1.0%) were restored from visible digits and the sense, 24 (1.0%) are legible and unread, and 47 (1.9%) are lost in the binding. The reading was built from the table and the context by a model, and no person has checked a passage against the images. Cells read doubtfully from the table and settled by the letter are grade M.

  • Forty-seven groups cut by the binding at the right edge of ff.147v–151v (20 partly visible and not restored, 27 wholly lost). The microfilm cannot show them; a clear copy would.
  • f.147v line 4, “et i” after “tiennent ce poste”, and f.147v line 9, “mais son se le pour-ra-t” (eight groups): the cells are transcribed, the words are not identified.
  • f.147v lines 24–25, “au pre[…] i si un instant favorable”, after the order for the Machault (three groups).
  • f.148r lines 13–14, “fin i ra bateau campagne” after “rempli de zèle” (five groups): the cells are confirmed, the phrase is not.
  • f.148v line 17, “de b l[…]” after “quinze diverses charges du” (two groups); f.149v lines 17–18, “ceux un | ton” (two); f.150v line 24, “de moyen” (two); f.151v line 6, “nt s” (two); f.151v lines 19–20, “je vous ent[…] e” before “Monseigneur” (two).
  • Cell 656 of the table is illegible and doubtful readings remain for 323 and a few others; they are marked ? in key/key274.json.
  • Not looked at: the ANOM copy of the letter (F3 series) and the Library and Archives Canada transcript reels of C11A, which might hold a deciphered copy; the 1758 Montcalm table and the third numerical sheet of the volume; Casgrain’s collection of the Lévis papers. Tomokiyo’s and Lasry’s pages were searched, not read through.

# English rendering: Vaudreuil to the minister, Montréal, 13 Aug 1760 (ANOM COL C11A 105 ff.147-152)

Working translation of `reading.md`. `[...]` = lost in the gutter; `[?]` = legible in the cipher but not read.

I have dealt by ordinary letters with what has happened in the various parts of the Colony since the last accounts I had the honour
of giving you. I have kept for this enciphered letter the particulars which it would be dangerous for the enemy to learn at this
critical moment. You will easily judge, Monseigneur, the danger run by the three ships, the stores and the troops that are coming to
Restigouche under M. d'Angeac's orders. [Given the] preparations, we must indeed fear everything from the extreme superiority of the
naval forces [which] hold this post and [?] ships, [...]. When M. d'Angeac does his best, I think, to follow to the letter the orders
I have given him according to what the cases require, but [?] to the test of the enemy's perseverance and obstinacy, who will seize
those ships, provisions and munitions at whatever price, time will show what he will conquer. The only hope left to me, and which I
desire still more [...] is that M. d'Angeac, having received my despatches for you, Monseigneur, [...] precise orders, without my having
[given] on this occasion, will have decided to send off the frigate Machault, which Sr de La Giraudais [commands], at the first [...] if a
favourable moment offers, to pass the enemy's vessels by night, so that she escapes the frigates sent in pursuit.

The duplicates of these same despatches will reach you, Monseigneur, by the schooner l'Extravagante, which has been in the Baie des
Chaleurs since last autumn. M. de Villemont, to whom I had granted the favour of carrying you the first [news], has been detached by
M. d'Angeac to give me an account of the situation. This officer, who is full of zeal [?], here, and I am well persuaded that he will
seize every occasion to distinguish himself. We are without a navy, or anything like one. The English are masters of our waters; they
can go wherever they please on the river; their fleet has now passed Lake Saint-François; the passage of the allies at the outlet of
that lake cannot be obstructed. We have absolutely nothing in the Colony for that: the King's flûte la Marie and our feluccas have more
to fear than to undertake.

So the English, going by water, will meet at the place General Amherst will have appointed for their junction. The movement I ordered
M. D[...] has contained that fleet; it has always [...] facing it the detachment [...] of that officer, and now that it is decided that
it will not [go up] to Trois-Rivières, I have left M. de Longueuil in this town with a poor garrison. Meanwhile twenty-two English ships have
passed Jacques-Cartier; it is announced that fifteen [vessels?] variously laden [?] and the rest of the troops of [Lou]isbourg, it is claimed they are already at
Cap Rouge. Everything proves to me that the English mean to make a diversion from our [forces], however weak, but the main object being to
keep the heart of the Colony, I am drawing in M. [...], I keep my forces re[united] and ready to seize the favourable moment to give battle.

The English have begun landing on the north shore; they behave there as on the south: they make seductive speeches to the Canadians; they
make a marked point of paying them for the refreshments they need, in salt, brandy and various essential articles of which the Colony is
quite bare. They give them everything cheaper than they have ever had it in time of peace. They seize the arms the people have, gently;
they make much of being alone able to relieve their misery, but warn them at the same time that the houses and farms of men who are absent will
be burnt. It is an evil, Monseigneur, which I cannot remedy. The English, masters of the waters, are always in a position to [...] our
detachments if they [...] drown our small forces piecemeal; all the rest would cost them nothing. If I had had provisions it would have been
easy to gather all the Canadians of the government of Québec; their zeal for the King's service would have made them despise the risks and
the certainty of losing their farms. Our frontier on Lake Champlain [...] by far [?] although it is our best post, the little navy we have there
cannot even be compared with the enemy's, and we lack the indispensable necessities [to] repair it.

I am in no better a situation on Lake Ontario: the English navy dominates there; our two corvettes cannot face or resist its two frigates;
one of our corvettes has even run aground and is damaged; we have a third on the stocks which could have put us in a state to dispute the
mastery of the lake, but we have not one of the articles needed to finish it and make it fit to sail. That, Monseigneur, is a very exact
summary of our sad position. Hunger presses us; the Canadians have their harvest to bring in, but they cannot be drawn away from [...] the
defence of the Colony. It is going to be attacked briskly on every side; the English have about forty [thousand] men; what can I set against
them? Battalions of which the fullest is not half [...] what it should be, militia and Indian nations, the latter [grow] weary of our extreme destitution; it needs
nothing less than my care and their attachment to set in motion the smallest of armies. The enemy will be infinitely superior to all our
forces united, far from having a single man over the number of our battalions; most of the militia and Indians have no part in the design.
We have only ten [...] thousand pounds of powder in the interior [?] Colony; it will soon be [used up] if we have to use our artillery.

We are no better off for provisions, although the Canadians hasten to supply all they have. General Amherst, who knows our extreme distress,
seizes the urgent moment of our harvests to make his greatest efforts. The circular letter that M. Bigot wrote jointly with me to the militia
captains about the delay in paying the bills of exchange would have had all the effect we wished, but you will see, Monseigneur, by the
enclosed copy of General Murray's circular letter to most of those captains that he has left nothing unsaid to discredit these bills of
exchange and our paper money entirely. To overcome every obstacle the commissary paid in his bills of exchange on [...] of the inhabitants who
had the least doubt about the coin of the [...]; to put them more at their ease I offered them bills of exchange on my own salary; the
Intendant and the Chevalier de Lévis have gladly followed my example. Such are the present circumstances, more than critical; the Colony is
reduced [...]; I shall make every effort that my zeal for the King's service can suggest to preserve it. I [...] you, Monseigneur, to be so good
as to assure His Majesty that if we succumb it will only be at the last extremity. The Chevalier de L[évis] is animated by the same zeal [...]
with me. I should have the greatest hopes from our close union and our perfect understanding if we were less overwhelmed.

In short, if the English complete the conquest of the Colony they will owe it only to the exhaustion of our forces and our last resources, for if
I had only the provisions and munitions that are in danger of being taken at Restigouche, that together with the precautions I should otherwise
take would put me in a position to secure a retreat where I could hold out with the troops when it is no longer possible for me to stop the
enemy's advance.

I am with very deep respect, Monsieur, your very humble and very obedient servant, Vaudreuil.
