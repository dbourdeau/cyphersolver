01 The letters

This page covers four cipher letters of Vaudreuil to the minister, all in the same table. Three are of April 1759, sent with the spring despatches before the campaign that ended with the fall of Québec; they had pages of their own in the project’s notes until 24 November 2026, when they were joined to this one under the project’s rule of one key, one target. The fourth, and the longest story, is the report of 13 August 1760.

Pierre de Rigaud de Vaudreuil de Cavagnial was the last governor of New France. By the middle of August 1760 the colony had one town left: Montréal, with the British closing on it from the lake, the river and below Québec. On 13 August he sent the minister two kinds of letter. The clear ones, in the same volume, report the movements of the enemy on lake Champlain. This one is in number groups, because, he says, it holds “des particuliarités dont il seroit dangereux que les ennemis fussent instruits dans ce moment critique”.

The catalogue describers had left it as a title only: “Lettre chiffrée … au ministre”. The ciphertext runs from f.147r to f.152r and ends with the clear formula “Je suis avec un très profond respect, Monsieur, votre très humble et très obéissant serviteur, Vaudreuil”. The scans are microfilm images 1125 pixels wide, and the last group of every line on a left-hand page is cut off by the binding.

02 The cipher and the key

The 1760 letter has two thousand four hundred and twenty-four groups, of 395 distinct numbers, with 35 words in clear (names, titles, “Monseigneur”, the date and the closing formula). The numbers run from 1 to 700, so the table is a nomenclator: whole words (pour, les, poudre), names of places and ships, syllables and single letters for what is not in the table. The commonest items have several numbers (de 309 and 642, t 51, i 661, s 235). Spelling is phonetic: “vi-t-ma-it-res” for maîtres.

The key was in the volume that the catalogue entry pointed to. COL C11E 10 ff.285–290v, “Chiffre ou code de Montcalm et de Vaudreuil” (New France Archives 15213, ten images), holds three tables, each as an alphabetical sheet and a numerical sheet: two for Vaudreuil and the Marine in 1757, one for Montcalm in 1758. The numerical sheet that reads this letter is the second of Vaudreuil’s, e001295274, labelled Déchiffrant. The first, e001295272, gives nonsense. All 700 entries of e001295274 were transcribed from enlarged crops of the cells.

The same table reads Vaudreuil’s three cipher letters of April 1759 in COL C11A 104, so it was his working table from at least April 1759 to August 1760. Another cipher letter of that volume, by Trémais (6 November 1759), does not use it and is in a different code.

03 The three letters of April 1759

They are whole letters in number groups, with “Monseigneur”, the date and the closing formula in clear, and they read with the same table and the same corrections from the first line. Each was compared with its images line by line on 5 November 2026; 26 groups were corrected there, nearly all where the writer’s 5, which has a flat top, had been taken for a 3.

8 April. The winter has been worse than he had written in the autumn: the cattle and horses are nearly all eaten, the people have four ounces of bread a day. A census of the three governments gives 6,356 men for Québec, 5,455 for Montréal and 1,313 for Trois-Rivières. He must defend Carillon against twenty-five to thirty thousand men, the Belle Rivière and Niagara against ten thousand each, La Présentation and lake Ontario; Fort Duquesne has been blown up and abandoned. If Québec is attacked he will go there himself with most of the troops and give battle again and again. He asks for battalions, munitions and, above all, provisions.

10 April. He encloses Montcalm’s letter of 27 February, a memorandum and his own plan for the campaign. Montcalm’s opinion that the colony is lost has been “l’entretien de l’officier, du bourgeois, du soldat, du milicien”; some officers of the troupes de terre speak indecently of the governor to the people. He has asked Montcalm to stop it, or he will have the officers arrested and sent to France, and he has told Montcalm that he will command at Carillon.

16 April. Fearing another year of dearth, he has had the munitionnaire tell his correspondents in France to replace at once any ship taken; he asks that the freight be paid in Canada, and for a supplement to the provisions sent, on which the colony’s survival depends.

What stays open is scattered: ten groups on f.64r of the first letter (the corn taken from somewhere, “toutes les […] des […]”), four on f.60v, eight that describe the memorandum in the second, two in the third (“les […] sont trop grands”). Brice Dudout’s separate readings offer fills for some of them (“toutes les moutures des moulins”); they are not taken in, because they have not been checked on the images.

Francis Parkman, in Montcalm and Wolfe (1884), translates passages of Vaudreuil’s letters to the minister of 8 and 10 April 1759: the census of “a little more than thirteen thousand effective men”, the plan to go to Québec and give battle “again and again”, and “the people are alarmed, and would lose courage if my firmness did not rekindle their zeal”. They are the passages of ff.55v–56r and 62r–62v of the first letter and f.69r of the second as read here. So Parkman saw a clear or deciphered text in the Paris archives. This was found on 24 November 2026, after the readings, and was not used for them.

04 What Vaudreuil says in August 1760

The whole French reading is under Full texts below, with an English translation. In outline:

The ships at Restigouche. Three French ships with effects and troops are bound for Restigouche under M. Danjac (François-Gabriel d’Angeac), and the British have overwhelming force at that post. Vaudreuil hopes Danjac will send the frigate Machault, under the Sieur de La Giraudais, past the blockade by night with his despatches; the schooner l’Extravagante, wintering in the baie des Chaleurs, carries the duplicates. M. de Villemont, who had leave to carry the first news, has been sent back by Danjac to report.

The war on the river. “Nous sommes sans marine ni même rien d’approchant.” The British fleet has passed lake Saint-François; twenty-two British ships have passed Jacques-Cartier, and it is announced that ships laden with the rest of the troops of Louisbourg are already at Cap Rouge. In the north the British pay the Canadians for what they take, make speeches, and warn that the houses of absent men will be burned. Vaudreuil has no food to gather the militia, and the French vessels on lakes Champlain and Ontario cannot match the enemy’s.

No powder, no food, no money. The allies tire of the colony’s want. The English will have some forty thousand men against battalions none of which is half what it should be; the colony has only “dix[…] milliers” of powder (between ten and nineteen thousand pounds; the end of the figure is lost in the binding), which would be used up fast. Murray’s circular to the militia captains, a copy of which is enclosed, was written to discredit the bills of exchange and the paper money that Bigot and Vaudreuil had tried to protect; the munitionnaire and Vaudreuil himself offer bills on their own pay to reassure the habitants, and the Intendant and Lévis follow his example.

The request. “Je vous … Monseigneur, de vouloir favorablement assurer Sa Majesté que si nous succombons ce ne sera qu’à la dernière extrémité.” With the stores waiting at Restigouche he could take the troops to a retreat and hold it as long as possible; without them he cannot. The clear letters beside this one in COL C11A 105 confirm the cast: the Machault, the Bienfaisant and the Marquis de Malauze with the troops, d’Angeac, La Giraudais, Villemont, the Extravagante, Bigot, Amherst.

05 Method

The letter was transcribed from enlarged crops, one token per group, with a mark for digits lost in the binding, then decoded with tools/decode.py from the 700-cell table plus a file of context corrections. The first decode read as French at once. Where a sentence would not form, the group was looked at again on the image. The table was then corrected from the letter, 33 cells in all, each with its reason in key/overrides.json: long s read as f, l as t, c as e and b as h were the usual errors in reading the table, and two cells were settled on the alphabetical sheet e001295269 (rotated and enlarged: moyen 473, ton 152, bateau 671; cell 2 is campagne, not compagnie, which is 102).

Three passages gave way to the sense, not to the table. A small line written between two lines of f.150v has a caret and is to be read inside the line below; read in place it made nonsense, and a second 51 that the transcription carried there is not on the image. “qui~ | z e diver s” is quinze diverses with the last group of the line lost, and “je~ | i s bou r g on” is [Lou]isbourg, on. Twenty-five groups cut by the binding were restored from their visible leading digits and the sense; they are counted separately (97.0% with them, 96.0% without).

No control was run, because the key was found and not recovered from the ciphertext. The check is that a 700-entry table made for another year reads six folios of French, with the right ships, officers and enemy commanders in the right places, and reads three other letters of 1759 as well, two of them with passages that Parkman translated from a clear or deciphered text.

06 What remains uncertain

The three 1759 letters are each above the 95% threshold; their open groups are listed above. In the 1760 letter, of 2,424 groups, 2,326 (96.0%) read from the image as written, 25 (1.0%) were restored from visible digits and the sense, 26 (1.1%) are legible and unread, and 47 (1.9%) are lost in the binding. The reading was built from the table and the context by a model, and no person has checked a passage against the images. Cells read doubtfully from the table and settled by the letter are grade M.

Those are shares of groups. The project’s 95% threshold counts words: of the 1,483 word places of the reading, 1,383 give sense and 100 are supplied, restored or unread, which gives 93.3%, under 95%, so the extent is partial (recounted on 14 November 2026 after Brice Dudout pointed out that the 96% was a share of groups; his own word count over the whole file was 93.7%). His independent decoder, built from the table files alone, found a cell for every one of the 2,401 legible groups and agrees with the letters of the reading at 97.5%; the differences are the clear words, the groups lost in the binding and the open groups, with one exception. The exception is a change the reading had made without marking it: on f.152r the groups 672 672 51 51 494 give “me me t t un”, and the reading has “me mettroit”; the ending is now marked as supplied, “mett[roit]”. The whole of f.152r and seven lines of f.151v, about 240 groups, were compared with the image the same day by the project’s integration check (not a person) and agree with the transcription; the other folios were not compared.

# English rendering: Vaudreuil to the minister, Montréal, 13 Aug 1760 (ANOM COL C11A 105 ff.147-152)

Working translation of `reading.md`. `[...]` = lost in the gutter; `[?]` = legible in the cipher but not read.

I have dealt by ordinary letters with what has happened in the various parts of the Colony since the last accounts I had the honour
of giving you. I have kept for this enciphered letter the particulars which it would be dangerous for the enemy to learn at this
critical moment. You will easily judge, Monseigneur, the danger run by the three ships, the stores and the troops that are coming to
Restigouche under M. d'Angeac's orders. [Given the] preparations, we must indeed fear everything from the extreme superiority of the
naval forces [which] hold this post and [?] ships, [...]. When M. d'Angeac does his best, I think, to follow to the letter the orders
I have given him according to what the cases require, but [?] to the test of the enemy's perseverance and obstinacy, who will seize
those ships, provisions and munitions at whatever price, time will show what he will conquer. The only hope left to me, and which I
desire still more [...] is that M. d'Angeac, having received my despatches for you, Monseigneur, [...] precise orders, without my having
[given] on this occasion, will have decided to send off the frigate Machault, which Sr de La Giraudais [commands], at the first [...] if a
favourable moment offers, to pass the enemy's vessels by night, so that she escapes the frigates sent in pursuit.

The duplicates of these same despatches will reach you, Monseigneur, by the schooner l'Extravagante, which has been in the Baie des
Chaleurs since last autumn. M. de Villemont, to whom I had granted the favour of carrying you the first [news], has been detached by
M. d'Angeac to give me an account of the situation. This officer, who is full of zeal [?], here, and I am well persuaded that he will
seize every occasion to distinguish himself. We are without a navy, or anything like one. The English are masters of our waters; they
can go wherever they please on the river; their fleet has now passed Lake Saint-François; the passage of the allies at the outlet of
that lake cannot be obstructed. We have absolutely nothing in the Colony for that: the King's flûte la Marie and our feluccas have more
to fear than to undertake.

So the English, going by water, will meet at the place General Amherst will have appointed for their junction. The movement I ordered
M. D[...] has contained that fleet; it has always [...] facing it the detachment [...] of that officer, and now that it is decided that
it will not [go up] to Trois-Rivières, I have left M. de Longueuil in this town with a poor garrison. Meanwhile twenty-two English ships have
passed Jacques-Cartier; it is announced that fifteen [vessels?] variously laden [?] and the rest of the troops of [Lou]isbourg, it is claimed they are already at
Cap Rouge. Everything proves to me that the English mean to make a diversion from our [forces], however weak, but the main object being to
keep the heart of the Colony, I am drawing in M. [...], I keep my forces re[united] and ready to seize the favourable moment to give battle.

The English have begun landing on the north shore; they behave there as on the south: they make seductive speeches to the Canadians; they
make a marked point of paying them for the refreshments they need, in salt, brandy and various essential articles of which the Colony is
quite bare. They give them everything cheaper than they have ever had it in time of peace. They seize the arms the people have, gently;
they make much of being alone able to relieve their misery, but warn them at the same time that the houses and farms of men who are absent will
be burnt. It is an evil, Monseigneur, which I cannot remedy. The English, masters of the waters, are always in a position to [...] our
detachments if they [...] drown our small forces piecemeal; all the rest would cost them nothing. If I had had provisions it would have been
easy to gather all the Canadians of the government of Québec; their zeal for the King's service would have made them despise the risks and
the certainty of losing their farms. Our frontier on Lake Champlain [...] by far [?] although it is our best post, the little navy we have there
cannot even be compared with the enemy's, and we lack the indispensable necessities [to] repair it.

one of our corvettes has even run aground and is damaged; we have a third on the stocks which could have put us in a state to dispute the
mastery of the lake, but we have not one of the articles needed to finish it and make it fit to sail. That, Monseigneur, is a very exact

them? Battalions of which the fullest is not half [...] what it should be, militia and Indian nations, the latter [grow] weary of our extreme destitution; it needs

forces united, far from having a single man over the number of our battalions; most of the militia and Indians have no part in the design.
We have only ten [...] thousand pounds of powder in the interior [?] Colony; it will soon be [used up] if we have to use our artillery.

Vaudreuil reports to the minister on the winter and the defence plan: famine and loss of draught animals, a British army of about 20-25 000 against Niagara, Fort Duquesne evacuated, the four points to defend (Carillon, the Belle Rivière, Niagara, La Présentation and Lake Ontario), his plan to fall back on Québec with his troops, and numbered requests for battalions, munitions and provisions.

# English rendering: Vaudreuil to the minister, Montréal, 8 Apr 1759 (ANOM COL C11A 104 ff.54-66)

Working translation of `reading.md`. `[...]` = supplied from the sense; `[?]` = a group read in the cipher but not understood; `[sign ?]` = a written sign that is not a number.

In the letters I had the honour of writing to you in cipher last autumn I tried to give you as exact an account as I could of the sad situation to which this Colony was reduced. What has followed has only
too well proved that it was even more critical than I had had the honour of announcing to you: from day to day its extreme want of everything and the most dreadful misery of the settlers have grown; soon there will be no more hope of
finding resources: the breed of horned cattle, of domestic animals and of horses is almost entirely extinct; there remains, Monseigneur, to several of the better-off inhabitants only the animals of a single plough; the greater
part of the various inhabitants have none at all. These animals have served to make up for the bread cut from the troops; the people have only four ounces of bread a day, with no other matter to sustain themselves.
The English are not unaware of it; they hasten to seize so fine an opportunity to invade this Colony. Foodstuffs, munitions of war and every kind of thing are as plentiful with them as we are short of them; the attacks
with which they threaten us appear most certain. They are no longer in the same forces they employed in the previous campaigns: they are doing more than doubling them; they expect some resistance on our part; they make no secret of it; nor do they make
a secret that nothing will stand against their obstinacy and their determination.

I have not lost time in setting all the forces of this Colony in motion.

I have had a very exact census made of the three governments. That of Québec will furnish me six thousand three hundred and fifty-six men; that of Montréal five thousand four hundred and fifty-five; that of Trois-Rivières one thousand
three hundred and thirteen. Even so, nothing will be left but the infirm and those too old to serve; and from the number of militia fit for war one must also deduct the men indispensably needed for the transports and the various needs of the service.

We have besides the eight battalions of the land troops and the forty companies of the colonial troops, which are not complete.

I must, Monseigneur, provide 1st for the defence of our [?] of Carillon, which will probably be attacked by twenty-five to thirty thousand men. You saw, Monseigneur, from my letters of last autumn that my activity in
relieving that quarter had obliged the English to give up the attack; that it had even hastened their retreat. The Marquis de Montcalm would have made it extremely difficult for them, but I wrote to him in vain, the troops of the marine, the Canadians and the
Indians told him the wish they had to go in pursuit of the enemy, nothing was able to make him yield to my views.

2nd, for the defence of the Belle Rivière against an army of about ten thousand men. I had foreseen the fate of Fort Duquesne; M. de Ligneris, having been obliged to reduce his troop in proportion to the subsistence,
took the wisest course by evacuating, blowing up that fort at the approach of six to seven thousand English, and withdrawing to the post at Machault; he holds there; I have the forces and various reliefs that are near him to support him, but the thing looks very difficult.

3rd, for the defence of Niagara, which is to be besieged by ten thousand men. You have seen that this post and those of the Belle Rivière must help one another and attach themselves above all to the defence of Niagara
as the principal and most essential key of the upper country.

By this reprisal I match the one of our enemies who want to join their army from the Ohio to the one they are raising to send to Niagara. M. Pouchot, captain in the Béarn regiment, is going to take over the command;
I have given him my instructions for every case; he will attend particularly to opposing the enemy's landing.

4th, for the defence of La Présentation, to keep our communication on Lake Ontario and to set bounds to the very ambitious designs of our enemies, who announce ten thousand men for this quarter and who intend by this way to
penetrate as far as Montréal with an even stronger army.

This winter I have had two corvettes built, of ten twelve-pounders each, which will serve for the transports to Niagara and manoeuvre according to circumstances to harass the enemy. I have besides set up a flying camp
to watch the English and to be informed of their movements [and of their] men on that lake; they want to re-establish Oswego and will employ ten thousand men there.

All things considered, I see the same plan reborn which was conceived by the Duke of Cumberland: General Amherst, who was his aide-de-camp, doubtless means to repair General Braddock's mistake.

Québec is also threatened by forces incomparably superior to all those of this Colony; the fortifications of this place need many repairs and even outworks; for all these works four thousand men would be needed, where to find them and how to feed them? Québec, this position,
I have confined myself to having the materials got ready during the winter that it was possible to collect without causing any extraordinary consumption of provisions; M. de Pontleroy will stay at Québec to follow the most pressing works
as our situation allows.

The [?] [?] of this capital greatly occupies the persons who do not enter into my views; from this follow reasonings and remarks that insinuate themselves and gradually become public; but the matters I have in view
have at this moment the most pressing claim.

Indeed, Monseigneur, it is quite certain that our frontiers and the posts I mean to defend will be attacked; it is even to be feared that most of them will succumb to the too great superiority of the enemy; but it is
my policy to do my best to keep them: abandoning them would sound badly in every respect. 1st, all our Indians, seeing no more French, would find themselves obliged to join the English; 2nd, these, no longer occupied
far away, would no longer be subjected to hard and painful marches, to finding expedients to ease and speed the transport of the [?] [?] [?] [?] of their army; they could unite and penetrate quickly as far as they pleased; 3rd, finally, if we had
peace, they would find themselves at little cost in possession of the posts we should have evacuated, and consequently of the immense extent of country that depends on them.

It is not the same with Québec; I greatly fear that the English have a wish to measure themselves against it and to conquer it; but 1st, it is not at all certain that they have to their liking everything they need for so important an expedition; 2nd, I [?] naturally believe myself assured, the King having been informed by you, Monseigneur, both of the insufficiency
of our forces and of that of our means, that His Majesty, knowing of such an armament, will have been pleased to provide against this undertaking; 3rd, supposing that I were in the case of defending Québec
with the weak forces and small means that, at present, our enemies could not arrive there without my being informed long before by the lookouts I have set as far as the mouth of the river; now whatever diligence a fleet may make that sails with weight and measure, it cannot reach Québec before the outcome of the battle the English must give us at Carillon is decided; whatever may be, Monseigneur, the event of that battle I shall always keep the liberty of going myself to Québec with the greater part of our troops, all the militia and the Indian nations that I can assemble; Québec, [and] the three governments, I shall leave at Carillon only what is indispensable and the armed xebecs; being at Québec I shall give the enemy battle and repeat it until I have forced him to withdraw or until, overwhelmed by his excessive superiority, he has entirely reduced me; my obstinacy in opposing the landing will be all the more to the point as I should not have enough; for powder, bombs, cannonballs [sign ?] to sustain the siege for six days. Can I, Monseigneur, have more powerful reasons for wishing the arrival of [your relief as early as] possible, in the good spring season?

2nd, munitions [?] [sign ?];
3rd, provisions, essentially: this item, although placed last, occupies [and] worries the settlers and the troops in general.

I make of it [sign ?], Monseigneur, my most laborious industry and my continual care; I have drawn from the Canadians even their own food;
I have taken by authority all the [?] [?] [?] [?] [?] of the [?] [?] [?] [?] [?]; I appealed in the King's name, with the most pressing urgency, to the tithes of the parish priests; the Bishop was good enough to contribute by his exhortations to these gentlemen.
In spite of that I can so little flatter myself that I shall complete the provisions that are indispensably necessary to take the field that M. Bigot has been forced to have half of these provisions brought down to Québec to keep the people alive. We are totally short of goods. The Indian nations of the upper country in general suffer in their most urgent needs; the English are using every means to seduce them. Can I promise myself that the nations of the south will come to the Presqu'île and that all those of the north will come to this town? I dare not count on it; but if they arrive there, could I keep them, being
bare of everything and especially of subsistence? All of them, it is probable, would withdraw, pressed by their needs; they would have recourse to the English, who, welcoming them, would draw the same services from them as we.

*I am with very deep respect, Monseigneur, your very humble and very obedient servant, Vaudreuil.*

I have the honour to send you herewith copies of: 1st, the letter that the Marquis de Montcalm wrote to me on the 27th of last February; 2nd, a memorandum
containing on one side the reflections of the Marquis de Montcalm [?]; 3rd, the summary of the plan of my general operations for this campaign,

the townsman, the soldier and the militiaman long before it came to my knowledge.

Unfortunately he did not make his opinion known by announcing it by word of mouth and talking to all sorts of people indiscriminately with as much discretion
as he showed in his letter and written reflections. From this a train of arguments followed which quickly circulated through all the parishes; the loss of the
Colony has been regarded as very evident; the measures I take for its preservation have been censured by certain officers of the land troops, who have added
the most indecent remarks about me to the people themselves, a people already only too weary of the war, short of subsistence and of all their various most pressing needs, and who
see at the same time that the goodness and vigour of their constitution are weakening. This people, I say, would be easily persuaded; the evil with which they
are threatened frightens them and would discourage them if I did not reassure them, and if my firmness and the confidence they have in me did not revive their zeal and their ardour
for the King's service.

I have only too well recognised that all these arguments do not displease the Marquis de Montcalm; the progress they have made is unknown to no one, but the officers who are
their authors are only the bolder and the freer in their talk for it. I have asked the Marquis de Montcalm to be so good as to cut off these arguments at the root; otherwise
I could not spare myself from having arrested those officers who take the largest part in them, and from sending them to France to give you an account of [their] conduct.
I would, Monseigneur, have already carried out all that I have said on this point to the Marquis de Montcalm, but in the circumstances in which we are I do myself violence; I use
[?] [?] of gentleness to bring minds round and to revive those who are timid; but I shall have to take a course of rigour if the case demands it, to
forestall a greater evil. That I shall do only in the last extremity.

I do the officers of the land troops in general the justice that is due to them: many of them have a circumspection equal to their zeal. None of this prevents me, Monseigneur, from
seizing every occasion to maintain as much union as understanding between the Marquis de Montcalm and myself, and I shall pass over in silence all the remarks he has made and may make about me.
Far from showing him resentment for it, I give him proofs of my confidence; in short, I am doing everything I can to avoid the scandal of a rupture between him and me; I look only to
the good of the service itself and the glory of His Majesty's arms. I await your first despatches, Monseigneur, with the greatest impatience; I hope you will have been so good as to
enter into the reasons which I have set out to you alone.

However, you see that, so as to have no reproach to make to myself, I have told the Marquis de Montcalm that I expect him to take command of the army at Carillon.

I am with very deep respect, Monseigneur, your very humble and very obedient servant, Vaudreuil.

Montréal, 16 April 1759. M. de Vaudreuil. Monseigneur,

Fearing that we shall find ourselves next year in the same shortage as this one, I have engaged the munitionnaire to order his
correspondents, both in France and, in case they should learn that some of the ships they will have loaded with provisions for him have been taken,
to replace them at once and to send him fresh consignments from whatever ports they can.

I have the honour, Monseigneur, to inform you of this reprisal [replacement] and to beg you to be so good as to have the ships they need furnished
to this munitionnaire's correspondents. Supposing they cannot find them, the munitionnaire will pay the King in Canada the freight of these ships
at this year's rate; the [?] [?] are too great for him to be able to pay, at any price whatever, this freight once earned,
which obliges him to write to his correspondents that he does not intend to pay this freight anywhere but in the Colony.

For the rest, Monseigneur, I can only repeat my wish that you add a supplement to the shipments of provisions that will be made to us this
year: the preservation of the Colony depends especially on it, and without it the Colony would fall of its own accord.

I am with very deep respect, Monseigneur, your very humble and very obedient servant, Vaudreuil.
