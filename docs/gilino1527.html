01 The letter and the key

Gilino’s letter, ff. 214–218 of Cotton Vespasian C IV, is nine pages in Italian, clear passages alternating with cipher. The address leaf reads “Illmo et Exmo Dno Dno Mediolani Duci … Cremone”, and it is signed “Servo el Gilino”. How it came among Wolsey’s papers is not recorded. The volume also holds Ghinucci’s letters to Wolsey, whose cipher also writes digits above the signs; the two are unrelated (Ghinucci to Wolsey, 1527). The key is DECODE R5258, a sheet in the Milan archive (ASMi Carteggio Sforzesco cart. 1591 no. 24) docketed “Cum equite Bilie / extracta ex cifris”: the key of the Cavaliere Bilia, another Sforza agent, which Gilino also used.

02 The reading with R5258

The cipher has barred base signs (G written like a 6, B, D, d, o, H, A, P, R) with numbers from 20 to 80, about forty unnumbered signs, and nulls. A one-sign-one-letter solver run on it gave no Italian, as expected for a syllabary. DECODE holds about 377 Sforza key records from the Milan archive. Their contact sheets were scanned for Gilino’s rows. R5256 is of the same family but its syllables differ. R5258 fits: B.31–50 ba…gu, B.51–54 fa…fo (B.55 and B.56 are title codes in this letter), D.51–65 la…nu, 6.56–80 pa…tu, o.33–47 va…zu, H.27 che, R.23 con, D.40 i in this letter. Applied with a few values added from context, it gives Italian throughout (Italian 4-gram score −1.52 per letter, in the range of real text). The folios run 214r, 214v, 215r, 215v, 216r, 216v, 217r, 217v, 218r; DECODE’s image order mixes them.

What the cipher says, in summary:

  • Gattinara, just landed at Barcelona, is in poor favour with the Emperor over two intercepted letters to the Viceroy of Naples: send the Naples galleys to relieve Genoa, and let Lope de Soria spend what is left of the 100,000 ducats of the exchange on the city. The Emperor’s confessor and the secretary Lalemand are his enemies. Since Bourbon’s death the Duke has only Gattinara to rely on, and should have “the Bilia” keep on good terms with him.
  • The new Portuguese ambassador brought a letter in the Pope’s own hand to the nuncio, and two briefs asking the Emperor to take the Church under his protection.
  • The Abbatino returns to Italy with all he asked for; the Pope had also been negotiating through Gregorio Casale; Paolo Luzzasco is to go over to the Emperor with 300 light horse.
  • The French ambassadors swear that Francis I insists the Duke keep Milan; the nuncio has gone to Palencia to watch them.
  • On the news from Lombardy the Emperor is said to be revoking the Pope’s liberation. The Ferrarese ambassadors and the Colonna agents call the Pope a traitor and a false pope.
  • Andrea da Burgo writes to the Emperor pro opprimenda Italia. The investiture of Milan has been sent to the Infante with 100,000 ducats, for him to come down and defend it.

Measured: 3,111 cipher signs, 122 of them nulls; of the 2,989 that carry text, 9 are not deciphered and about 108 fall in 45 doubtful words, leaving 2,872 (96.1%) that give sense. Measured on the words of the running reading (r8572_reading.md), the letter builds coherent sentences but reads at 94.6%, under the 95% bar; George Lasry’s edition, measured the same way, reads at 94.2%. No decipherment of this letter, on the manuscript or in print, was found.

03 George Lasry’s edition

Contributed by George Lasry. This edition supplies the reconstructed key, complete token transcription, line-by-line key application and Italian reading for the nine photographed text pages, in manuscript order. The original clear passages remain in regular type; deciphered passages are bold.

The research notes dated 7 October 2026 record recovery through Italian cribs, repeated patterns and the ordered syllable rows, with Codex assistance, before a historical key had been found in that work. The reconstruction in section 02, made two days later without knowledge of his, uses the R5258 key found on DECODE. On the numbered signs both editions name, the two keys agree on 101 of 106; the five differences are H.28 (R5258 come, his et), R.22, R.28 and x.22 (words in R5258: e, ancora, pero; presumed nulls in his), and 6.65 (a null in R5258, his tentative qu?). The two are one key reached by two routes. Both routes and their differing transcriptions remain available.

The contributed transcription has 3,105 cipher occurrences and 136 distinct cipher labels, plus two original cleartext abbreviations. It covers 161 cipher lines on ff. 214r–218r, in photograph order P1, P2, P7, P3, P5, P4, P6, P8, P9. Every transcribed group has a working assignment or structural interpretation, including tentative values and presumed nulls; this is not a claim of 100% decipherment accuracy. The 96.1% figure in section 02 belongs to the other transcription and is not a measurement of this edition.

The 11-page Gilino PDF supplied with the contribution guides the presentation. Later corrections from the accompanying edition are retained, including G70 = ru, X26 = quanto, G5000 as a presumed null, and the corrected final-page wording. Five key values remain tentative, several sign classifications need further checking, and the final overwritten sign remains unresolved.

04 Method

The DECODE images were fetched at full size with the project’s login (the record carries no transcription). The letter was transcribed by a sub-agent working from crops and corrected in 24 places against the key. A one-sign-one-letter homophonic solver gave no Italian, as expected for a syllabary. A second sub-agent scanned the contact sheets of the Sforza key records on DECODE (ASMi Carteggio Sforzesco cart. 1591, R5235–R5309) and found R5258. decode_r8572.py applies it with the context values in key_extra.tsv. The reading was checked against an Italian letter model of the period: it scores far above readings from random keys and from keys fitted to shuffled text (validation.tsv). The neighbouring DECODE records were opened; none is in this key.

05 What remains uncertain

  • Gilino: nine signs not deciphered and 45 doubtful words, from the writer’s own a/o and i/e confusions and from signs the image does not separate. They are listed in reading_r8572.txt.
  • Who wrote the Gilino letter: it is signed “el Gilino” and names “the Bilia” in the third person, and the key is the one made “with the Cavaliere Bilia”; a Sforza nomenclator of 1525 (R5287) lists Bilia and Ghilino as two men.
  • George Lasry’s edition: five tentative values (b50 gu?, g65 qu?, b55 imperatore?, b56 sua maesta?, h26 cosa?), the separator ooo, and the overwritten final sign (lasry/NOTES.md).

Measured: 3,111 cipher signs, 122 of them nulls; of the 2,989 that carry text, 2,872 (96.1%) give sense with R5258.

## Translation

I am assured by many that [?His] Lordship stands in little favour with the Emperor for one single thing that is
imputed to him, and on account of two letters of his, intercepted, to the Viceroy, that he should send the galleys of
Naples to the relief of Genoa and order Lope de Soria to use the rest of the hundred thousand ducats of the exchange
for the benefit of that city, both in provision of cash and of victuals, since otherwise the whole would be lost.
[?His] detractors, who openly are the confessor and [?the German] secretary, will have it that Lautrec, on such
information, sent part of the troops [?…] to the siege of Genoa which were destined for another place, and that for
this the said city [?…] was no longer defended, [?for] the great need that I see His Majesty has of him. I cannot
think but that he will keep himself in the Emperor's good favour, and for this I shall not cease to urge «Your
Excellency» to order the Bilia by your letters to keep on good terms with him. Now we cannot hope for anything but
good offi[c]es from His Lordship, above all with the death of Bourbon. I have resolved to go to meet him as far as
three days' journey, and I know that for this I shall not be held suspect by the confederate ambassadors.

The ambassador of the King of Portugal, newly come in the Pope's name, is not sent principally by His Holiness,
[?…], as the Datary writes to the Lord Nuncio; but as he was about to go on to [?his] King, he begged Our Lord that it
might please him [to honour him] with the honour of a brief in [?his] [?…] person to the Emperor, with authority to
negotiate his settlement, promising to have him released at once and restore him to his former friendship with His
Majesty and to the ancient state of the Church; which has not come about for him as he had promised, and [as] said not
only in [?Italy] but also in Spain. Here he has been known for a light man; and with this good opinion of himself he
leaves the Imperial court tomorrow for Portugal. This ambassador has brought a letter in the [?Pope]'s own hand to the
Nuncio, as credence for [?Saporito], chamberlain of His Holiness, [?who] died in Genoa, «so wisely and prudently
written that hardly anything more could be wished», exhorting him not to fail in his due service; likewise two briefs
to the Emperor, full of many fine sentences, and with fine reasons it persuades His Majesty to take up the patronage
of the Church and not to oppress it, above all sub fide, although unlearnedly written.

The Abbatino returns despatched as he desires; the Emperor has granted him all he knew how to desire and ask. It is
true that on many heads, since His Majesty does not know of what moment they are, he writes to the Viceroy of Naples,
as better informed of the affairs of Italy, that he should satisfy the [?aforesaid] Marquis, as far as he can, for such
is his mind. «No particular have I been able to learn beyond what is already written; [?…] at the first opportunity I
shall advise Your Excellency of it; hold this for certain» that the settlement has followed with this Majesty,
although he had come to terms with [?…] [?…], as we have news here that he was negotiating it through[h] [?…] the
Cavalier Casale. The Emperor has given his mind to this settlement through the reminders of the Duke of Ferrara and
the work of the Nuncio. I believe, however, that the Marquis, being obliged to the Florentines, will not show himself
wholly in favour of the Emperor, but will see to it that Pagolo Luzzasco with three hundred light horse passes into
the service of His Majesty, as [?yesterday] the aforesaid Abbatino, where [?…] to leave for your parts in all haste,
and by him I have written to Your Excellency under cover of the Lady Marchioness of Mantua.

Both have also sworn to me that the Most Christian [King] lately writes to them that he absolutely wants Your
Excellency to remain in the state of Milan in the settlement, which does not agree with what he wrote to them lately;
none[the]less [?I] leave it to the truth. The Lord Venetian ambassador and I alone, the day before yesterday, were
with the Lord A[po]stolic Nuncio at [?Usiglios], and begged him to be willing to go to Palencia and stay there while
the Fre[n]ch ambassadors stay, and to use diligence to learn the truth of what they will negotiate, and above all
concerning that matter. He promised us to do it willingly, and so we understand that His Lordship has gone there; and
I am almost certain that he will serve us, both out of respect for Your Excellency, and for what he owes to Italy
[?…], and for the friendship he holds with the said Venetian ambassador.

I understand also that the Emperor, as soon as he heard of the surrender of Genoa, sent a [?new] despatch to
Monseigneur de [?Miliao] [?…], who until that time had not embarked for Rome; and it is hinted to me that His Majesty
is revoking the liberation of the Pope because of the new events in Lombardy. What leads [?me] to believe this are
the bad offices which the Ferrarese ambassadors and the agents of the Colonna publicly do against His Holiness with
the Emperor; of whom, when they speak of him, they cannot name him except as traitor, false pope, bastard, maker
[?of] poisons, and with a thousand other insults which it would be long to write. With him, I am told, they will
despatch Troilo Negrono, and that His Majesty [?…] now says he wants to grant many favours to the Lords Colonna; and
this is the present difficulty of the affairs of Lombardy, and has caused it; therefore they fear that after the one
ruin the other, of the kingdom of Naples, may follow.

There are here letters of Messer Andrea Borgo [?to] the Emperor and Valdes, [?…] formerly his [?servant], full of
counsels pro opprimenda Italia [for crushing Italy] and long discourses for keeping the state of Milan in the
devotion of His Majesty. [?…] At the end they conclude that the Infante [?is] the one who can hold it and defend it
from any violence that [?…] [?might come] to occupy it. After the taking of Genoa it is said that the Emperor has sent
word to have the Infante come down, and [?to give] all [?Italy] as a prey to the Germans; and His Majesty shows greater
heart than ever, and that the conf[ess]or [?…] has come out [with it]: that the loss of Genoa has been a strong spur to
the Emperor; therefore he should show himself wholly bent on war; and how [?powerful] [?…] [?forces] are [?…]. [?…]
here by several ways [?…] I have been advised [that] the Emperor h[a]s sent by three couriers the investiture of the
state of Milan to the Infante, [?…] for the defence of that state, and that at present he is providing him with a
hundred thousand ducats. The loss of Genoa had been [?confirmed] by way of the Lord of Monaco, whom His Majesty is
providing with money so that he may keep himself in his devotion.

# R8572 = BL Cotton Vesp. C IV ff. 214r-218r: "el Gilino" to Francesco II Sforza, Paredes, 15 Sept 1527: English translation of George Lasry's edition

Translation (Claude Opus 5.5, 23 Oct 2026) of George Lasry's Italian working reading, `lasry/plaintext_reading.txt`
(7 Oct 2026, contributed 14 Oct 2026): the whole letter, clear passages and deciphered passages, in its restored
physical order (P1 214r, P2 214v, P7 215r, P3 215v, P5 216r, P4 216v, P6 217r, P8 217v, P9 218r). His marks are kept:
`[word?]` a conjecture (translated as `[?word]`), `[…]` unread; *Emperor?* and *His Majesty?* translate word codes whose
expansion he leaves unproved. His notes on unresolved signs are kept in brackets.

## Translation

### f. 214r

*Clear.* Most Illustrious and Most Excellent, my only Lord, my ever most honoured Lord. I kiss the hands of Your
Excellency.

After visiting these places around Valladolid I came to Paredes on the 9th of this month, where I have in part been
given knowledge of what I write to you below, of which Your Excellency will be able to judge with the prudence that is
natural to you.

The Lord Grand Chancellor, by his letters of the 27th of last month, arrived in Barcelona with Messer Giovanni
Bartolomeo di Gattinara, Martino Centurio[?ne], [?general in the making] of Monseigneur de Bourbon, and a few of his
household. Most stayed in Genoa for lack of ships. His Lordship had a very contrary voyage, both from storms at sea
and from having been followed by some galleys of the French fleet. He is expected at the Imperial court at the end of
this month.

*Cipher.* I am assured by many that His Lordship stands in little favour with the [?Emperor] for one single [?thing]
that is imputed to him: namely that, by two letters of his intercepted to the Viceroy, [he asked] that he send the
galleys of Naples to the relief of Genoa, and order Lope de Soria to use the rest of the [?hundred] thousand ducats of
the exchange for the benefit of that city, both for providing infantry and victuals.

### f. 214v

*Cipher.* Otherwise the whole would be lost. His detractors, who are openly the confessor and the German secretary,
will have it that Lautrec, on such information, sent [?part] of [?his] troops to the siege of Genoa, which had been
destined for another place, and that for this reason the said city was no longer defended.

I, for the great need that I see His Majesty? has of him, cannot think but that he will keep himself in the Emperor?'s
good favour; and for this reason I shall not cease to urge Your Excellency to order the Bilia by your letters to keep
on good terms with him. Now we cannot hope for anything but good offices from His Lordship?, above all with the death
of Bourbon.

I have resolved to go to meet him as far as three days' journey, and I know that for this I shall not be held
suspect by the ambassadors of the confederates.

### f. 215r

*Clear.* Monseigneur de Tarbes has given me a copy of the aforesaid intercepted letters, which I would have sent to
Your Excellency if the present bearer had not shown himself so difficult.

*Cipher.* The ambassador of the King of Portugal, newly come in the Pope's name, is not sent principally by His
Holiness. So [uncertain passage] the Datary writes to the Lord Nuncio. But as he was about to go on to his king, he
begged Our Lord that he would [?do] him the honour of a brief, in his person, to the [?Emperor], with authority to
negotiate his settlement, promising to have him released at once and restored to his former friendship with [?His
Majesty] and to the ancient state of the Church. Which has not come about for him, and he had promised, [as] said, not
only in [?Italy] but also in Spain.

Here he has been known for a light man, and with this good opinion of himself he leaves the Imperial court tomorrow
for Portugal. This ambassador has brought

### f. 215v

*Cipher.* a letter in the Pope's [?own] hand to the Nuncio, as credence for Saporito, chamberlain of His Holiness, who
died in Genoa, which [was]

*Clear.* written so wisely and prudently that hardly anything more could be wished,

*Cipher.* exhorting him not to fail in his due service. Likewise two briefs to the [?Emperor], full of many fine
sentences, and with fine reasons it persuades [?His Majesty] to take up the patronage of the Church and not to oppress
it, above all sub fide, which [?unlearnedly: uncertain passage] written.

The Abbatino returns despatched and desires […], and the [?Emperor] has granted him all he knew how to desire and ask.
It is true that on many heads, since [?His Majesty] does not know of what moment they are, he writes to the Viceroy
of Naples, and better informed of the affairs of Italy, that he should satisfy the [?aforesaid] Marquis as far as he
can, for such is his mind. [to to qu gu: unresolved sequence.]

*Clear.* [?No] particular have I been able to learn beyond what is already written; on learning of any [… a word in
the right margin not read with certainty], at the first opportunity I shall advise Your Excellency of it. Hold this
for certain:

*Cipher.* that the settlement has followed with this Majesty; that he had come to terms with France, and here we have
news that he was nego-

### f. 216r

*Cipher.* -tiating it through the Cavalier Casale.

The Emperor? has given his mind to this settlement through the reminders of the Duke of Ferrara and the work of the
Nuncio. I believe, however, that the Marquis, being obliged to the Florentines, will not show himself wholly in favour
of the Emperor?, but that he will see to it that Pagolo Luzasco, with three hundred light horse, passes into the
service of His Majesty?.

And yesterday the aforesaid Abbatino was to leave for your parts in all haste, and by him I have written to Your
Excellency under cover [?of] the Lady Marchioness of Mantua. [Su: unresolved final fragment.]

*Clear.* The Magnificent Lords the French ambassadors hold letters from the Most Christian King of the 29th of August,
by which they are informed of the [?defeat] at Portofino, the surrender of Genoa, and the hope of soon obtaining
Alessandria. Together they tell me they have a commission from their King that the negotiation of the peace be
conducted on the content of the articles made at [?Madrid], a thing to which up to now the aforesaid Most Christian
[King] had not been willing to assent, and that for this reason they have almost certain hope that an agreement [?will
come to] follow [an interlinear correction not wholly resolved]. They, with the Lords the English ambassadors, have
been at Palencia [?and are staying there] to discuss what their King can or cannot swear of the above-written
articles.

### f. 216v

*Cipher.* Both have sworn to me that the Most Christian [King] lately writes to them that he absolutely wants Your
Excellency to remain in the state of Milan in the settlement, which does not agree with what he wrote to them lately.
Nonetheless I leave it to the truth.

The Lord Venetian ambassador and I alone, the day before yesterday, were with the Lord Apostolic Nuncio at Usiglios,
and begged him to be willing to go to Palencia and stay there while the French ambassadors are there, and to use
diligence to learn the truth of what they will negotiate, and above all concerning that matter. He promised us to do
it willingly, and so we understand that His Lordship has gone there.

I am almost certain that he will serve us, both out of respect for Your Excellency, for what he owes to all Italy, and
for the friendship he holds with the said

### f. 217r

*Cipher.* Venetian ambassador. I understand that the [?Emperor], as soon as he heard of the surrender of Genoa, sent a
new despatch to Monseigneur de Miliao, who [?until] that time had not embarked for Rome. And it is hinted to me that
[?His Majesty] is revoking the liberation of the Pope because of the new events in Lombardy.

What leads [?me] to believe this are the bad offices that the Ferrarese ambassadors and the agents of the Colonna
publicly do against His [?Holiness] with the [?Emperor]; [?of] whom, [?as often as] they speak of him, they cannot name
him except as traitor, false pope, bastard, maker of poisons, and a thousand other [?insults] which it would be long
to write. [Three signs left in the cipher block, o29 vp b+, literally v-v-u, function unresolved.]

*Clear.* Today the Magnificent Messer Ludovico Catto, doctor of laws, the old ambassador of the Most Illustrious Lord
Duke of Ferrara, has gone to Valladolid to take leave of His Imperial Majesty [a word struck out] to return to his
country. I have always found him much inclined to the benefit of Your Excellency, and therefore you [?are] under an
obligation to him.

*Cipher.* With him, I am told, they will despatch Troilo Negrono; [and] that [?His Majesty] now [?says] that he wants to
grant many favours to the Lords Colonna. If this is so, the present difficulty of the affairs of Lombardy has caused
it; they fear that after the one ruin the other, of the kingdom of Naples, may follow.

There are here letters of Messer Andrea Borgo

### f. 217v

*Cipher.* [?to] the [?Emperor] and to Valdesio, formerly his servant, full of counsels pro opprimenda Italia [for
crushing Italy] and long discourses for keeping the state of Milan in the devotion of [?His Majesty]. At the end they
conclude that the Infante is the one who can hold it and defend it from any violence that might descend to occupy it.

Since the taking of Genoa it is said that the [?Emperor] has sent word to have the Infante come down and to give all
Italy as a prey to the Germans; that [?His Majesty] shows greater heart than ever, and that it has come out from the
confessor that the loss of Genoa has been a strong spur to the [?Emperor], [that] he should show himself wholly bent
on [?war], and [?how] powerful their [?forces] are. [The final groups LU-V-I follow, unresolved.]

*Clear.* It is also said that His Imperial Majesty has sold the state of the Count of Caiazzo in the Kingdom for
twenty-eight [thousand] ducats to the son of the Count of Monteleone, Viceroy of Sicily.

In the Lord Marco Pio I see every day his goodwill towards Your Excellency growing, and his desire to do you service;
which has caused in me a certain uncommon regard for His Lordship, which obliges me to be with him many times and to
honour him greatly.

### f. 218r

*Cipher.* Written thus far, I have been advised by several safe ways that the [?Emperor] has sent by three couriers
the investiture of the state of Milan to the Infante [abbassi: word not yet resolved in context] to the defence of
that state, and that at present he is providing him with a hundred thousand ducats.

The loss of Genoa has been confirmed by way of the Lord of Monaco, whom [?His Majesty] is providing with money so that
he may keep himself in his devotion. [Final sign overwritten or corrected, not resolved.]

*Clear.* I would not know what more to write to you for now, except that I commend myself to your good grace as
humbly as I can, praying Our Lord God to give you that happiness which you desire for yourself. From Paredes, the 15th
of September 1527.

Of Your Most Illustrious and Most Excellent Lordship the most humble and perpetual servant, el Gilino
