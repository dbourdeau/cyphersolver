Oxford → The Hague · homophonic numerical cipher with a small code · 1643

Charles I and Nicholas to Boswell

2 November 1643 · The National Archives, SP 84/157 ff. 217 and 219 (State Papers Foreign, Holland)

The letters
Two cipher letters of 2 November 1643: Charles I to the Duke of Courland’s envoy, and Sir Edward Nicholas to Sir William Boswell, the King’s resident at The Hague.
Where
The National Archives, SP 84/157/96 (f. 217) and SP 84/157/97 (f. 219). No images are online; the only text is S. Tomokiyo’s 2021 transcription.
The cipher
A 24-letter row repeated four times over the figures 20–115, supplementary homophones at 116–159, nulls below 20 and four word codes. 455 numeric groups, 171 distinct.
How it was read
Robert Pitt published the alphabet on 14 September 2026 and it was applied here. A permutation control puts his row 9.6 standard deviations above 20,000 random rows. The four graphic signs are word signs, which Pitt’s notes left unexplained.
What it says
The King answers and re-credentials the Duke’s envoy, sent through Boswell in Boswell’s own cipher. Simpson’s 1893 print of the parallel letter to the Duke confirms this and gives 291 = Duke.
Still open
About a dozen word codes that occur once, and some transcription slips; both need the folios, which are not online.
Credit
The alphabet is Robert Pitt’s; the transcription is S. Tomokiyo’s.

01 The documents

TNA Discovery has the items at folio level: SP 84/157/96, f. 217, “Charles I to Boswell — in cipher” and SP 84/157/97, f. 219, “Nicholas to Boswell — in cipher”, both 2 November 1643. No images exist online. Tomokiyo’s transcription (blog, 17 September 2021), with asterisks where he was unsure, is the only text; Pitt’s inputs are line for line the same. Sir William Boswell was the King’s resident at The Hague; Sir Edward Nicholas was Secretary of State at Oxford.

02 The key (Pitt’s alphabet)

plain   a  b  c  d  e  f  g  h  i  k  l  m  n  o  p  q  r  s  t  u  w  x  y  z
1st     20 32 21 33 22 34 23 35 24 36 25 37 26 38 27 39 28 40 29 41 30 42 31 43
2nd     44 56 45 57 46 58 47 59 48 60 49 61 50 62 51 63 52 64 53 65 54 66 55 67
3rd     68 80 69 81 70 82 71 83 72 84 73 85 74 86 75 87 76 88 77 89 78 90 79 91
4th     92 104 93 105 94 106 95 107 96 108 97 109 98 110 99 111 100 112 101 113 102 114 103 115

That is letter = row[(n − 20) mod 24] with the row a c e g i l n p r t w y b d f h k m o q s u x z — the odd positions of the 24-letter alphabet (no j, no v) and then the even ones. Each letter’s four values are 24 apart. It is the same type as the regular Stuart keys Tomokiyo describes for Scudamore (1636), Cave (1638) and Ormonde–Clanricarde (1643), and as the Maltravers–Ormonde key. The supplementary block 116–159 is five alphabetical runs (a d e · m n o r s t y, a d e · n o · t u, …), so even its unseen values are constrained; 142 = s is added here from Nicholas’s opening “[His Majesty] comands”.

03 Verification

The 21 maximal runs of four or more alphabet groups (106 letters) were scored with a 4-gram model built from 2.7 million characters of seventeenth- to nineteenth-century English. The null hypothesis permutes the 24 letter identities over the same periodic structure.

score
Pitt’s row−217.5
20,000 random rows: mean / sd / best−333.7 / 12.1 / −271.1
z; rows scoring at least as well9.6; 0 of 20,000

A free period scan (annealing a 24-letter row for periods 20–32) is not decisive and is recorded as such: at 106 letters the annealer over-fits, and periods 27–32 reach higher scores than the true key. The argument for period 24 is structural (the odd/even construction, the 24-apart homophones, the alphabetical supplementary blocks), and the decryptions that match the cleartext (“A C · · knowledge”, “[835] D E R stand”, “Y O U R E [C] R E D E N T I A L S” beside Simpson’s “Our Re-credentials”) are the confirmation.

04 The four signs

Tomokiyo renders four graphic signs: △, 中, □ and +. Each occurs once inside a spelled word and afterwards on its own, and the stand-alone occurrences come in exactly the combination the embedded ones predict.

So △ 中 873 □ is “good Cousin your Master”. The clerk wrote the sign beside the word where he spelled it out (compare “for whom 270 thus hereafter” in the Ormonde letter, where a code is introduced inline), and the transcription placed the sign in the middle of the word. This is the explanation Pitt’s notes said was missing, and with it 873 = you / your fits all six occurrences.

Nicholas to Boswell, 2 November 1643 (f. 219):

Sir, [His Majesty] comands [me] to signify his pleasure to you, that you use all possible diligence & industry by your meanes of the [170] & otherwise [secret]ly to hinder the coming ouer of the [181]s from the [726], for it is here apprehended that they [will] doe more hurt then good, for that they [are] understood here not to affect Monarchy. Word codes with a value: 800 to, 588 of, 873 you/your, 291 Duke (from Simpson’s printed cipher line 291 588 C U R L A N D on the letter to the Duke), 516 letter, 835 un-, 749 selves, 750 send, 205 assure, 223 by, 629 pray, 640 port, 636 pro-, 406 gener-, 484 His Majesty, 746 secret-. Sorted, the readable codes run alphabetically (a 170–228 … d 291 … o 588–591 … s 726–755, t 800, u 835, y 873), so the nomenclator was probably an alphabetical list; a second letter in the key would test that.

06 What the letters are

  • The King’s letter is addressed to the envoy. It answers the envoy’s letter of 6 October, calls the Duke “your Master” three times, sends “your re-credentials”, and wishes him a safe return to his master. Simpson’s Civil War Papers (Scottish History Society Miscellany I, 1893) prints the King’s letter to Duke James of Courland of the same day, calling itself “these Our Re-credentials”, and the envoy’s later memorial: the passage was unsafe and “the ministers of their Majesties at the Hague” dissuaded him from crossing and took his letters. Nicholas tells Boswell that the King’s two letters are “put into your character” and that Boswell is to “decifer & interpret them”. Hence the catalogue’s “Charles I to Boswell”.
  • “Monsieur F I R X S S appeares not here.” Simpson names Major Georg von Firks as the Duke’s agent. A man expected at Oxford who has not come is the envoy’s own account of himself.
  • The arms. Muskets, match and powder to Weymouth, Dartmouth, Exeter, Falmouth or a fifth port: the Duke’s memorial says he was told their Majesties needed munitions rather than men, and Nicholas wrote to Roe in December 1643 that “arms and powder are arrived at Dartmouth for his Majesty” (CSPD).
  • The funerals of Duke Friedrich (d. 1642) and his brother Wilhelm (d. 1640), held together in February 1643: “the Late Dukes his Father & Unkle”.
  • Nicholas’s instruction to hinder “the coming ouer of the [181]s from the [726]”, people “understood here not to affect Monarchy”, fits the States General’s embassy of mediation resolved on in late 1643 and received at Oxford early in 1644, which the King did not want: 181+S = Ambassadors, 726 = States. These two values rest on the history; the cipher alone does not fix them.

  2. Band statistics and the decoder (targets/boswell/key.py); 4-gram model (lm4.py); permutation control (permtest.py); period scan (periodscan.py).
