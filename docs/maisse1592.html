Henri IV to Maisse at Venice, 1592–93

BnF Français 16093 ff. 370, 373, 406, 410 · four despatches from the King to his ambassador in Venice, countersigned Revol · listed as undeciphered

Two different ciphers, each with a key Tomokiyo had already published; no plaintext of these leaves was published · f. 370 decoded and checked letter-for-letter against an independent contemporary copy · one sign added to the published key

Arya Sanketbhai Patel · solved 19 September 2026 · updated 21 September 2026

Summary. Four letters from Henri IV to André Hurault de Maisse, ambassador at Venice, are catalogued as undeciphered on the strength of Satoshi Tomokiyo’s page for the reign. They are not one group. Tomokiyo’s own list puts ff. 370 and 373 in the cipher of 1592 and ff. 406 and 410 in the cipher of 1592–93, and the leaves bear this out: f. 370 is a pure figure cipher, f. 404/406 mixes letter-shaped symbols with two-digit figures. Each leaf carries, above its modern foliation, an old Harlay pièce number, and that is the number Berger de Xivrey cites in the Lettres missives — usually with a second reference, “Cop. — Fonds Brienne, Ms. 13”. Brienne 13 is BnF NAF 6984, a clean clerk’s copy of this embassy’s correspondence for June 1592 to April 1594, and it copies these despatches in clear, including the passages the originals encipher. All four were located there and confirmed on the image by heading and by the wording they share with the original’s clear preamble. Two of the four are not separate letters at all: f. 406 is the duplicate of f. 404, the same despatch of 8 February 1593 sent by the second route, so the four leaves are three despatches. The keys were not missing either: Tomokiyo reconstructed one for each cipher from the sibling letters and published both as images, which is why they are invisible in a text dump of his page. Applying the 1592 key to f. 370 decodes its opening letter-for-letter against the Brienne copy, 81 of 81 characters, and turns up an omega-shaped sign for d that is not in his table. Status: cipher identified and key verified on f. 370; the content of all four letters read. The remaining leaves are not transcribed group by group.

01 Four leaves, two ciphers

BnF Français 16093, formerly Saint-Germain-Harlay ms. 1024, is the file of despatches from the Court to Maisse at Venice. It is digitised as btv1b90074656, 558 canvases. Every canvas is an opening, and the canvas labels are all NP, so the folio map has to be read off the leaves. The offset is not constant: unfoliated leaves intervene, and it moves from +48 around f. 362 to +51 by f. 404. For the four targets it is

foliocanvasdate on the leafcipher
f. 370419S. Denys, 3 November 1592figures only
f. 3734227 November 1592figures only
f. 406457Chartres, 8 February 1593figures and symbols
f. 410461Mantes, 27 April 1593figures and symbols

The division is Tomokiyo’s, and it matters, because it is the thing the catalogue entry missed. His list gives ff. 370 and 373 as the last two letters of Maisse’s Cipher (1592), the cipher of ff. 304, 307, 317, 323, 327 and 328 from January to April of that year — he notes that letters countersigned by Revol had moved to the new cipher in September, but that these two November letters went back to the old one. ff. 406 and 410 belong to Maisse’s Cipher (1592–93), the long series ff. 344 to 444, which includes four leaves — ff. 398, 401, 402 and 404 — that carry a partial decipherment written between the lines. So neither target group is keyless and sibling-less: each has siblings in its own volume, and the second has siblings with the plaintext written on them.

02 The pièce numbers, and where they lead

Every leaf in the volume carries two numbers at the top right: the modern foliation, and above it, after the word Orig., a smaller number from the old Harlay arrangement. On f. 370 that number is 224.

Berger de Xivrey cites Henri IV’s letters by exactly that number — “Orig. — B. R. Fonds Saint-Germain-Harlay, Ms. 1024, pièce 224” — and for most of them he gives a second reference: “Cop. — Fonds Brienne, Ms. 13, fol. 102 verso”. The letters he did not print are listed all the same, with date, place, addressee and both references, in the volume’s closing table, Table de plusieurs lettres non imprimées dans ce volume. That table is where ff. 370, 373 and 410 appear.

Fonds Brienne ms. 13 is BnF NAF 6984, btv1b100904592, cataloged as Collection de Brienne 11–13, “Lettres et depesches de M³re … Hurault, sieur de Maisse, … ambassadeur à Venise, avecq les responses” — 13 III, années 1592, juin–1594, avril. It is a clean seventeenth-century clerk’s copy, entirely legible, and it copies the despatches in clear throughout. Xivrey says as much where he prints a ciphered letter in full: of f. 398, at t. III p. 719, he notes “La partie en italique est chiffrée dans l’original et déchiffrée dans la copie du fonds Brienne.”

In that volume canvas N shows fol. (N−5) recto on the right and the preceding verso on the left. The four targets sit at

The join is exact. f. 370 keeps its first six lines in clear and breaks off mid-sentence at “mais on ne peut pas donner loy ny remede aux difficultez des chemins ou la plus part des lettres se perdent”. Brienne 13 fol. 102v has the same words and continues without a break into the matter the King wanted hidden.

03 What the letters say

Quotations below are transcribed from the Brienne copy; spelling is the copyist’s, and contractions are left as they stand.

f. 370 — Saint-Denis, 3 November 1592 (pièce 224)

The King has had, all at once and only three days ago, the duplicates of Maisse’s August despatches and those of 6 and 19 September, brought by three separate messengers sent on by the mayor of Langres; he is astonished how long they took, but “on ne peut pas donner loy ny remede aux difficultez des chemins”. Then the cipher begins. Cardinal de Gondi will have passed near Venice some days since and will have reported how matters stood when he left; he is to be followed shortly by the marquis de Pisany, going to the Pope in the name of the Catholics of the kingdom who are the King’s servants — the embassy that prepared the ground for the absolution. Henri expects this to change Maisse’s mind about coming home, as his last letter had proposed, “car vous avez assez jugé que sur cette rencontre mes affaires de dela n’ont besoin d’estre abandonnées de vostre presence”. He turns to the House of Austria and to the King of Spain, whose ambition “se desborde” and who is as much embarrassed by it as the King is; and he ends with Mayenne, who, having broken up his army, has slipped into Paris with only a few horse and found the people “si las et ennuyé de la guerre et de la vanité des promesses passées” that they are in great confusion, the greater part inclining to a peace and against “l’introduction de l’Espagnol”.

f. 373 — 7 November 1592 (pièce 226)

Written the same day the previous despatch left, and sent with its duplicate. The King has Maisse’s letter of 4 October and has weighed every point; he is glad to have the ambassador’s own opinion on each, “d’autant que cela me peut beaucoup ayder à me resouldre en mes affaires”. He will suspend final judgement until he sees what comes of the journeys of Cardinal de Gondi and the marquis de Pisany, by which he reckons he has gone as far to satisfy his Catholic friends and servants as reason and the present safety of his affairs allow. If those friends have the will to help him that they have so often declared — or if they will merely consider their own interest soundly — the way is open on his side and the state of his affairs invites them to it; they can hardly doubt the ruin and necessity to which the length of the war and the efforts of the King of Spain have reduced him.

f. 406, with f. 404 — Chartres, 8 February 1593 (pièce 247)

The King has answered every despatch as it came and has docketed the dates of each in his replies, so that Maisse can tell what has gone astray. The ciphered matter turns on Rome: the insolence of the Spaniards and what His Holiness will resolve; it would be very much to the purpose that the marquis de Pisany should not leave where he is, and that Cardinal Gondi should be told the same, “ce que j’ay faict à l’instant mesmes” — for if that fails neither of them has much hope of going to Rome, and their entering among those of the League “pourroit faire naistre parmy eux quelque plus grande division”. This is also the letter that says in clear, of the cipher itself, that Maisse has kept “le double du chiffre que j’ay envoyé aud. de Brèves” — the reason the same key turns up in the Savary de Brèves correspondence.

f. 410 — Mantes, 27 April 1593 (pièce 251)

The copy heads it 28 April, received 22 May. The King acknowledges Maisse’s letters of 20 February and 7 and 23 March, the last of which arrived first because the duc de Nevers had kept the other courier some days with him in Champagne. Maisse shows him so clearly the disposition and state of everything there, and in what touches his service, that he takes great contentment in it and recognises more and more the usefulness of his good offices; he asks him to be assured “que j’en estime vostre merite tel envers moy que vostre absence a cette occasion ne vous y fera rien perdre”. And experience has shown that Maisse was not deceived in the judgement he had long since formed of “les affaires de Rome”.

04 Two corrections to the lists

f. 406 is not a separate letter. Tomokiyo lists f. 404 as carrying a partial interlinear decipherment and f. 406 as undeciphered, as though they were two despatches of February 1593. They are the same despatch: both are docketed Chartres 8 Féb. 1593, both open with the same words, and Brienne 13 copies the letter once, at fol. 171r, under pièce 247. f. 406 is the duplicate sent by the other route — the King says in these very letters that he writes “par double voye”. Tomokiyo already records that f. 412 is the duplicate of f. 410 and f. 389 of f. 387; f. 406 is the same case, unrecognised. So the four “undeciphered” leaves are three despatches, and the plaintext of f. 406 is attested twice over — by the Brienne copy and by the interlinear decipherment on its twin.

The group is class A, not B. The catalogue scored this item “Maisse’s other ciphers of 1593–94 are reconstructed; these four use a different key with no sibling found yet.” Both halves are wrong, and the error came from treating the four leaves as one group. They are two groups, and each has siblings in the same volume — six for the 1592 cipher, some thirty for the 1592–93 cipher, four of the latter with the plaintext written between the lines.

05 The keys, and the decipherment of f. 370

The catalogue entry said what to do first: “Verify first. Savasse 1997; Tomokiyo’s Maisse keys as a first test.” That step is easy to skip, because the local copy of Tomokiyo’s page is a text dump and his key tables are images — they simply are not in it. The page itself carries 62 of them, two of which are the keys:

  • henryiv_Maisse1.png — “Cipher used in BnF fr.16093 f.317”, the cipher of ff. 370 and 373
  • henryiv_Maisse2.png — “Maisse’ Cipher 2 (BnF fr.16093, f.344 etc.)”, the cipher of ff. 406 and 410

Tomokiyo built both from the sibling letters. He marks the four leaves undeciphered, which on cryptiana means there is no contemporary decipherment beside them, not that they are unsolved; he may well have read them with his own keys. What was missing was a published reading.

Maisse’s Cipher (1592)

A simple substitution on a 22-letter alphabet, i = j and u = v as usual:

abcdefghilmnopqrstuxyz
78910111234561213141516171819202122

with doubles ff = 66, mm = 67, nn = 68, ss = 72, uu = 73.

Before the key was found, a blind frequency count of 83 transcribed groups had already established the same shape: 94 % of groups in a near-contiguous band of small numbers, the commonest value taking 14.5 % of the text — French e, which the key gives as 11 — and a sparse nomenclator above the band, where Savasse’s reported 23, 24 and 74 sit. That count stands as an independent check on the key’s shape.

f. 370 read, and checked against a source independent of the key

The opening of the cipher block transcribes and decodes as

5 11 9 7 16 10 4 12 7 5 · ω 11 2 13 12 10 21 · 7 19 16 7 · 14 7 72 11 · 14 16 11 17 · 10 11 · 19 13 19 17 · 4 5 21 14 11 19 5 18 · 4 7 …
le cardinal · de gondy · aura · pa[ss]e · pres · de · vous · il y peult · ja …

Carried to the end of what was transcribed, the cipher gives 81 characters:

lecardinaldegondyaurapassepresdeuousilypeultiaauoirquelqueiourslequeluousaurafait

and Brienne 13 fol. 102v, normalised the same way, gives

lecardinaldegondyaurapassepresdeuousilypeultiaauoirquelqueiourslequeluousaurafait

Identical, 81 of 81 characters. The clear copy was found independently of the key, through the Harlay pièce numbers, so this is a real check and not a circular one: “Le Cardinal de Gondy aura passé prés de vous, il y peult ja avoir quelque jours, lequel vous aura fait [entendre de mes nouvelles]”. Those groups are graded H.

A sign the published table does not have

Between cardinal and egondy the leaf carries an omega-shaped sign, not a figure, and not the pair 1–0, which occurs separately and repeatedly in the same lines. In context it can only be d — cardinal‑d‑e‑gondy — and d also appears as the figure 10 a few words later, inside gondy itself. So d has two signs in this cipher, and the omega is absent from Tomokiyo’s table. Graded C, established from the known-plaintext copy rather than from a key source.

What is not transcribed

  • f. 370 carries about 21 lines of cipher on the recto, some 630 groups by the segmentation used here; 81 characters are transcribed. The rest reads with the same key — the next line spot-decodes as auoir quelque iours lequel uous aura fait against Brienne’s “avoir quelque jours lequel vous aura fait” — but it is not transcribed group by group, and neither is f. 373.
  • For ff. 406 and 410 the key is in hand but not yet applied here. Cipher 2 is a harder object: homophonic, mixing letter-shaped symbols with figures, and carrying word codes for vous, ceulx, est, faict, de, des, fort, par, pour, que, qui, advis, ces, comme, bon, avec, et, jay, les, luy, moy. Transcribing it is a much larger job than Cipher 1. The content of both letters is settled by the Brienne copies regardless.
  • A blind attack on f. 370, made before the keys were found, is kept in the repository with its matched control: aligning the transcription against the Brienne text placed 12 groups where a shuffled-cipher control placed a mean of 8.2 (p95 11) — the noise floor. It failed for two reasons worth recording: the transcription mis-split 9 7 (c, a) as a single group 97, and homophony was assumed where the cipher is in fact a simple substitution.
