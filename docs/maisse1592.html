the Court → Venice · numerical cipher (Maisse’s Cipher of 1592) · November 1592

Henri IV to Maisse at Venice, November 1592

Go to the full text ↓

BnF Français 16093 ff. 370, 373 · two despatches from the King to his ambassador in Venice, countersigned Revol · listed as undeciphered (no contemporary decipherment on the leaves)

Contemporary clear copies of both letters in Brienne 13 (BnF NAF 6984) give the text, ciphered passages included; it was transcribed from them. Tomokiyo had published the key; no decipherment of these leaves had been published. The whole cipher of both leaves was then transcribed and decoded with his key and matches the clear copies at 97.6% and 97.8% of letters; the readings follow the figures, and the key gains two doubled letters and four codes.

01 The leaves and the cipher

BnF Français 16093, formerly Saint-Germain-Harlay ms. 1024, is the file of despatches from the Court to Maisse at Venice. It is digitised as btv1b90074656, 558 canvases. Every canvas is an opening, and the canvas labels are all NP, so the folio map has to be read off the leaves. The offset is not constant: unfoliated leaves intervene, and it moves from +48 around f. 362 to +51 by f. 404. For the two letters it is

f. 370419S. Denys, 3 November 1592figures only

The division is Tomokiyo’s, and the catalogue entry missed it. His list gives ff. 370 and 373 as the last two letters of Maisse’s Cipher (1592), the cipher of ff. 304, 307, 317, 323, 327 and 328 from January to April of that year. He notes that letters countersigned by Revol had moved to the new cipher in September, but that these two November letters went back to the old one. ff. 406 and 410 belong to Maisse’s Cipher (1592–93), a different cipher, presented on their own page. So ff. 370 and 373 have six siblings in their own volume.

02 The pièce numbers, and where they lead

Every leaf in the volume carries two numbers at the top right: the modern foliation, and above it, after the word Orig., a smaller number from the old Harlay arrangement. On f. 370 that number is 224.

Berger de Xivrey cites Henri IV’s letters by that number (“Orig. — B. R. Fonds Saint-Germain-Harlay, Ms. 1024, pièce 224”), and for most of them he gives a second reference: “Cop. — Fonds Brienne, Ms. 13, fol. 102 verso”. The letters he did not print are listed all the same, with date, place, addressee and both references, in the volume’s closing table, Table de plusieurs lettres non imprimées dans ce volume. That table is where ff. 370 and 373 appear.

Fonds Brienne ms. 13 is BnF NAF 6984, btv1b100904592, cataloged as Collection de Brienne 11–13, “Lettres et depesches de M³re … Hurault, sieur de Maisse, … ambassadeur à Venise, avecq les responses” — 13 III, années 1592, juin–1594, avril. It is a clean seventeenth-century clerk’s copy, entirely legible, and it copies the despatches in clear throughout. Xivrey says as much where he prints a ciphered letter in full: of f. 398, at t. III p. 719, he notes “La partie en italique est chiffrée dans l’original et déchiffrée dans la copie du fonds Brienne.”

In that volume canvas N shows fol. (N−5) recto on the right and the preceding verso on the left. The two letters sit at

Brienne 13 fol. 102v has the same words and continues without a break into the matter the King wanted hidden.

03 What the letters say

On 23 October 2026 the whole cipher of both leaves was transcribed group by group from the full-size Gallica images (2,298 groups on f. 370, 3,958 on f. 373), decoded with Tomokiyo’s key and aligned letter by letter with the Brienne copies: 97.6% and 97.8% of the copy letters the figures cover agree. Where the figures and the copy differ in wording or spelling, the readings (texts/f370_reading.md, texts/f373_reading.md, with English) follow the figures; on f. 370v about 147 letters lie in the binding gutter and rest on the copy alone. The alignment also extended the key: 71 = rr, 73 = tt, and the codes 23 = pape, 24 = Roy d’Espagne, 48 = ambassadeur, 49 = guerre. Quotations below are from those readings.

f. 370: Saint-Denis, 3 November 1592 (pièce 224)

The King has had, all at once and only three days ago, the duplicates of Maisse’s August despatches and those of 6 and 19 September, brought by three separate messengers sent on by the mayor of Langres; he is astonished how long they took, but nothing can be done about the difficulty of the roads. Then the cipher begins. Cardinal de Gondi will have passed near Venice some days since; he is to be followed shortly by the marquis de Pisany, going to the Pope in the name of the Catholics of the kingdom who are the King’s servants, the embassy that prepared the ground for the absolution. Henri expects this to change Maisse’s mind about coming home, “car vous avez assez iugé que sur cette rencontre mes affaires de dela n'ont besoing d'estre habandonnées de vostre presence”. Lambert goes with Pisany, bound for the Levant with the despatches for Brèves, which Maisse is to read and amend. The King thanks him for his warnings to the English ambassador and to Brèves, and for urging on the moves against the House of Austria, which the King of Spain feels as if they were in his own lands; “Et puis que c'est contre moy que son ambition se desborde, j'ay occasion de desirer et rechercher que tous moyens luy soient retranchez par lesquelz il me peult endommager.” Lesdiguières has opened war in Piedmont, and the Pope will have no more excuse for dissimulation. The clear copy then turns to Mayenne, who has broken up his army and slipped into Paris and found the people weary of the war and inclined to peace, against the bringing in of the Spaniard (summarised from the Brienne copy; this part is not in the transcribed passage).

f. 373: 7 November 1592 (pièce 226)

Written the same day the previous despatch left, and sent with its duplicate. The King has Maisse’s letter of 4 October and has weighed every point; he is glad to have the ambassador’s own opinion on each, “d'autant que cela me peut beaucoup ayder a me resouldre en mes affaires”. He will suspend final judgement until he sees what comes of the journeys of Cardinal de Gondi and the marquis de Pisany, by which he reckons he has gone as far to satisfy his Catholic friends and servants as reason and the present safety of his affairs allow. If those friends have the will to help him that they have so often declared, or if they will merely consider their own interest soundly, the way is open on his side and the state of his affairs invites them to it; they can hardly doubt the ruin and necessity to which the length of the war and the efforts of the King of Spain have reduced him.

04 A correction to the catalogue

The group is class A, not B. The catalogue scored this item “Maisse’s other ciphers of 1593–94 are reconstructed; these four use a different key with no sibling found yet.” Both halves are wrong, and the error came from treating the four leaves as one group. They are two groups in two ciphers, and each has siblings in the same volume: six for the 1592 cipher of ff. 370 and 373, some thirty for the 1592–93 cipher of ff. 406 and 410 (see that page).

05 The keys, and the decipherment of f. 370

The catalogue entry said what to do first: “Verify first. Savasse 1997; Tomokiyo’s Maisse keys as a first test.” That step is easy to miss, because the local copy of Tomokiyo’s page is a text dump and his key tables are images, which it does not contain. The page itself carries 62 of them, two of which are the keys:

  • henryiv_Maisse1.png: “Cipher used in BnF fr.16093 f.317”, the cipher of ff. 370 and 373
  • henryiv_Maisse2.png: “Maisse’ Cipher 2 (BnF fr.16093, f.344 etc.)”, the cipher of ff. 406 and 410 (their own page)

Tomokiyo built both from the sibling letters. He lists the four leaves as undeciphered, which on cryptiana means that no contemporary decipherment stands beside them; he may have deciphered them with his own keys. No decipherment of them had been published.

Maisse’s Cipher (1592)

A simple substitution on a 22-letter alphabet, i = j and u = v as usual:

abcdefghilmnopqrstuxyz
78910111234561213141516171819202122

with doubles ff = 66, mm = 67, nn = 68, ss = 72, uu = 73.

Before the key was found, a blind frequency count of 83 transcribed groups had already established the same shape: 94 % of groups in a near-contiguous band of small numbers, the commonest value taking 14.5 % of the text (French e, which the key gives as 11), and a sparse nomenclator above the band, where Savasse’s reported 23, 24 and 74 sit. That count stands as an independent check on the key’s shape.

le cardinal · de gondy · aura · pa[ss]e · pres · de · vous · il y peult · ja …

and Brienne 13 fol. 102v, normalised the same way, gives

lecardinaldegondyaurapassepresdeuousilypeultiaauoirquelqueiourslequeluousaurafait

Identical, 81 of 81 characters. The clear copy was found independently of the key, through the Harlay pièce numbers, so the check is not circular: “Le Cardinal de Gondy aura passé prés de vous, il y peult ja avoir quelque jours, lequel vous aura fait [entendre de mes nouvelles]”. Those groups are graded H.

A symbol missing from the published table

Between cardinal and egondy the leaf carries an omega-shaped sign, not a figure, and not the pair 1–0, which occurs separately and repeatedly in the same lines. In context it can only be d (cardinal‑d‑e‑gondy), and d also appears as the figure 10 a few words later, inside gondy itself. So d has two signs in this cipher, and the omega is absent from Tomokiyo’s table. Graded C, established from the known-plaintext copy rather than from a key source.

What is not transcribed

  • f. 370 carries about 21 lines of cipher on the recto, some 630 groups by the segmentation used here; 81 characters are transcribed. The rest decrypts with the same key (a spot check of the next line gives auoir quelque iours lequel uous aura fait against Brienne’s “avoir quelque jours lequel vous aura fait”), but it is not transcribed group by group, and neither is f. 373.
  • A blind attack on f. 370, made before the keys were found, is kept in the repository with its matched control: aligning the transcription against the Brienne text placed 12 groups where a shuffled-cipher control placed a mean of 8.2 (p95 11), which is chance level. It failed for two reasons: the transcription mis-split 9 7 (c, a) as a single group 97, and homophony was assumed for what is a simple substitution.

## Reading

Monsieur de Maisse, le mesme jour que partit la derniere depesche que je vous ay faicte, de laquelle la duplicata va avec la presente, je receuz la lettre du quatriesme du passé et ay veu et bien consideré tous les poinctz y contenuz, esquelz j'ay esté fort aise que vous m'aiez representé si particulierement avecque vostre advis sur iceulx, d'autant que cela me peut beaucoup ayder a me resouldre en mes affaires selon le fondement que je puis faire sur ce qui peut dependre des moiens et resolutions de dela.

Combien que j'estime devoir atendre d'en faire le dernier jugement, sçavoir qui succedera du voiage du Cardinal de Gondy et marquis de Pisany, en quoy je pense avoir si avant satisfaict a ce que mes amys et mes serviteurs Catholiques pouvoient desirer de moy autant que la raison et seureté presente de mes affaires me [?peut] permetre. Que si lesdictz amys ont la volonté qu'ilz ont declaree assez souvent de me vouloir ayder, ou si seulement ilz veulent bien sainement considerer leur propre interestz, le chemin leur en est ouvert de ma part et l'estat de mes affaires les y convie. Car ilz ne peuvent doubter de la ruine et necessité ou la longueur de la guerre m'a reduit et les effortz que le Roy d'Espagne faict pour m'acabler, que je n'aye besoin d'estre secouru, dont peu me peult plus aisement faire subsister que beaucoup davantage me relever d'une grande cheute, si par faulte d'estre assisté j'y estois tombé. Et diray de plus, sans flaterie de moi mesme et sur les discours qui se font qu'il leur suffit pourveu que ceste Couronne soit conservee, que cela ne se peult en autre personne que la mienne, dont les raisons sont si cogneues, oultre le droict et le moien que j'ay de l'empescher a tout autre, qu'il n'est besoin m'en expliquer davantage. Mais de vous mesme vous pouvez tascher de les insinuer dextrement es espritz desditz amys et les animer a conforter le naturel du Pape, puis qu'on cognoist en estre besoin, a embrasser un oeuvre si saint et si necessaire que la restauration de cest estat, et asseurer ce faisant le repos de tous les autres de la Chrestienté.

Cependant je trouve fort bon ce que vous avez faict pour faire continuer une diversion d'une partie des effortz que les ennemys pourroient faire contre moy, et aussi que vous aiez rejecté sur Verac sa partialité ce qui se publie par dela du tiers party, en quoy vostre opinion n'est mal fondee que qui pense le pouvoir retenir peult estre soupçonné d'y avoir bonne part et intelligence. Mais je loue la façon de laquele vous vous y estes conduict de monstrer tout autre creance que cele la et de recevoir tout ce qu'ilz en disent a office d'amitié.

Vous n'avez aussi esté deceu du jugement que vous avez faict des belles promesses du duc de Retz, duquel vous aurez entendu le retour par dela avec son frere le Cardinal, ce qu'il veult que je croye estre pour donner plus d'avancement a mes affaires. Mais le pis est que je me trouve descheu de quelque esperance qu'il m'avoit donnee d'aider a ceulx que j'ay en Suisse, et specialement pour une nouvelle levee qui n'est si necessaire, s'en estant allé sans y avoir donné provision a chose quelconque pour mon service, comme je ne doubte que le sieur de Sillery ne vous en ait adverty. Cela me vient tres mal a propos veu le licenciement que j'ay esté contraint faire des Suisses que j'avois, en retenant seulement une petite partie, et qu'au lieu que je demeure affoibly les ennemis atendent en brief un bon renfort, lequel combien qui ne puisse estre tel qu'ilz le veulent faire a croire a ces peuples de la ligue pour leur tenir tousiours les espritz charmez, et que le duc de Parme ne puisse si tost venir en personne pour estre encores fort mal disposé, si est ce qu'il ne sçauroit estre si petit qu'en l'estat ou je me trouve cela ne me donne de la peine.

Vous adviserez d'en donner soubz main tout le sentiment que vous pourrez aux amys afin de les inciter a m'y subvenir de quelque chose de leurs moiens, et taschez aussi de les faire resouldre s'il est possible a ne laisser perdre le fruict du beau commancement que le s.r Desdiguieres a donné du costé de Piemont, ce qu'il seroit contraint d'habandonner comme infaliblement il adviendra s'il n'est assisté. Se seroit eslever de tant l'insolence et audace de cet ennemy que peu de ses voisins se [?pourroient promettre] de demeurer en paix et repos avecq luy, et ne faut doubter qu'il ne remuast en Italie s'il y voyoit tant soit peu de subiect, comme il a commencé de faire ailleurs. Mais avec quelque petit secours qui soit donné audict s.r Desdiguieres, il luy plantera la guerre si avant dans son pais qu'il luy sera malaisé d'aller travailler ses voisins.

Touttes ces nouvelles occurrences et la direction qu'il est besoin que vous donniez pour le commancement a mes affaires de levant, en la charge que j'en ay commise au sieur de Breves, vous feront assez juger le prejudice qui seroit a mon service que vous desempariez encores cele ou vous estes, qui me faict vous prier d'y avoir encores un peu de patience, asseuré que je le vous prolongeray le moins qu'il sera possible, cognoissant bien qu'il est raisonnable que vous ayez le loisir et la commodité de venir pourveoir a voz affaires particulieres. Et quoy neantmoins la saison est encores si mal propre par deça que vous moins regretterez quelque peu de temps que vous pourrez encores donner par dela a mondict service.

Sur ce je prie Dieu, Monsieur de Maisse, vous avoir en sa saincte garde. Escript le [?vij.e] jour de Novembre 1592. Henry. Revol.

## Translation

Monsieur de Maisse, on the very day the last despatch I made to you left (its duplicate goes with this one), I received your letter of the fourth of last month, and have seen and well considered all the points in it, on which I was very glad that you set them out for me so particularly together with your advice on them, since that can help me a great deal to make up my mind in my affairs according to the reliance I can place on what may depend on the means and resolutions over there.

Although I think I must wait before making a final judgement, to know what comes of the journey of Cardinal de Gondi and the marquis de Pisany, in which I believe I have gone as far to satisfy what my Catholic friends and servants could want of me as reason and the present safety of my affairs allow. If those friends have the will they have so often declared to help me, or if they will only soundly consider their own interest, the road is open to them on my side and the state of my affairs invites them. For they cannot doubt the ruin and need to which the length of the war has brought me, nor the efforts the King of Spain is making to crush me, nor that I need to be relieved: a little can more easily keep me on my feet than a great deal more could raise me from a great fall, if for want of help I had fallen. And I will say further, without flattering myself, and on the talk that is going round that it is enough for them if this Crown is preserved, that this cannot be done in any other person than mine; the reasons are so well known, besides the right and the means I have to prevent it going to anyone else, that I need not explain myself further. But you can of your own accord try to instil this skilfully into the minds of those friends, and encourage them to strengthen the Pope's natural disposition, since it is known to be needed, to embrace a work so holy and so necessary as the restoration of this state, and thereby to secure the peace of all the rest of Christendom.

Meanwhile I think it very well that you have worked to keep up a diversion of part of the forces the enemy could bring against me, and also that you have laid on Verac and his partisanship what is being put about over there concerning the third party; in which your opinion is not ill founded, that whoever thinks to hold it back may be suspected of having a good share and understanding in it. But I commend the way you have conducted yourself, showing an entirely different belief from that one, and receiving all they say about it as an office of friendship.

Nor were you deceived in the judgement you formed of the fine promises of the duc de Retz, whose return over there with his brother the Cardinal you will have heard of, which he wants me to believe is meant to further my affairs. But the worst of it is that I find myself let down in some hope he had given me of helping those I have in Switzerland, and especially for a new levy, which is not so necessary, since he went away without having made provision for anything at all for my service, as I do not doubt the sieur de Sillery has told you. This comes very badly for me, given the discharge I have had to make of the Swiss I had, keeping only a small part, and given that, while I remain weakened, the enemy expect shortly a good reinforcement; which, although it cannot be as large as they would have these people of the League believe, to keep their minds ever under the spell, and although the Duke of Parma cannot come in person so soon, being still very unwell, still it could not be so small that, in the state I am in, it does not trouble me.

You will see to giving the friends, discreetly, every sense of this you can, so as to move them to help me with something of their means; and try also to bring them, if possible, not to let the fruit of the fine beginning the sieur de Lesdiguières has made on the Piedmont side be lost. He would be forced to abandon it, as he infallibly will if he is not supported; it would raise the insolence and boldness of that enemy so high that few of his neighbours could promise themselves to remain in peace and quiet with him, and there is no doubt that he would stir in Italy if he saw the least occasion, as he has begun to do elsewhere. But with some small help given to the said sieur de Lesdiguières, he will carry the war so far into his country that it will be hard for him to go troubling his neighbours.

All these new events, and the direction you must give at the outset to my affairs in the Levant, in the charge I have entrusted to the sieur de Brèves, will make you judge well enough the harm it would do my service if you were to leave the post you hold yet; which makes me ask you to have a little more patience, assured that I will prolong it for you as little as possible, knowing well that it is reasonable you should have the leisure and convenience to come and see to your private affairs. And yet the season is still so unsuitable here that you will regret less some little time you can still give over there to my said service.

With that I pray God, Monsieur de Maisse, to keep you in his holy care. Written the [?7th] day of November 1592. Henry. Revol.

I will add the good news […] of the defeat and death of the duc de Joyeuse before Villemur, which he was besieging, […] six thousand foot [?before it], of whom more than two thousand were left dead on the field, the rest put to rout, [and] many [?drowned] in the river Tarn; and five cannon and twenty-six standards were [?taken]. It is true […] find who were there, so that […] for […], until I send you a more particular account of it.
