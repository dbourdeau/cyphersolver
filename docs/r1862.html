Rome → the Doge and Senate of Venice · numerical syllabary with word codes · 29 April 1628 · key rebuilt from a sibling, read in part

A Venetian ambassador at Rome to the Doge, 29 April 1628

Archivio di Stato di Venezia, IT ASVe 0045 010 (Serie) Busta 27 f. 354 · DECODE R1862, four pages, catalogued Non-decrypted, with no transcription on the record and no sender, receiver or reading recorded

The key was rebuilt from the sibling record R1874, whose contemporary decipherment is imaged beside its cipher, and applied here unchanged · the despatch reports an audience with Cardinal Barberini in the first weeks of the War of the Mantuan Succession

Summary. The catalogue listed this as an anonymous ciphered letter from Rome, 29 April 1628, with nothing known about what it hides: DECODE holds four images, no transcription, and no sender or recipient. The address “Ser.mo Principe” makes it a Venetian ambassador’s despatch to the Doge. Its cipher is the same one used in another ASVe record, R1874 (Alvise Mocenigo from Madrid, 26 October 1628), and that record is imaged together with the chancery’s own decipherment of it. Transcribing that decipherment as known plaintext and aligning it to the cipher by a soft-EM forward–backward pass recovered the key: a syllabary grid in which the prefix gives the vowel and the last two digits the consonant, with single letters, a small word list and paragraph marks beside it. Applied to R1862 unchanged, it reads. The ambassador had spoken with Cardinal Barberini, who had twice asked him whether Palma (Palmanova) was well fortified, what garrison it had, and whether the pass of the Pontebba was secured — questions the ambassador answered by asking, “con intiera confidenza”, to what end they were put. The rest relays the Cardinal on the Emperor, on Wallenstein’s price for coming into Italy, and on the imperial aulic council; and the ambassador pressing that the Pope go in person as legate to settle Mantua and Savoy together. Status: cipher broken, key rebuilt and extended, 98.8% of the code groups given a value, and 91% of them standing in lines that read as continuous Italian. What is still open is thirteen word codes of the nomenclator band and the four lines that turn on them.

01 The record, and the sibling that carried the key

DECODE R1862 is four images of a despatch at ASVe, Busta 27 f. 354, dated 29 April 1628 and placed at Rome. The record carries no transcription, no sender and no receiver, and the archive’s images are not in the public domain, so nothing of them is reproduced here. Cipher and clear text alternate on the page: page 1 and page 2 are almost entirely in figures, page 3 runs clear for eleven lines and then ciphers again, page 4 opens and closes in the clear with two ciphered blocks between.

The way in was a sibling. DECODE R1874 (ASVe Busta 30 f. 174) is a despatch of Alvise Mocenigo from Madrid, 26 October 1628, in the same cipher; its sixteen images hold the ciphered despatch, a second enciphered copy of it, and — on images 8955–8959 — the chancery’s own decipherment, paragraph by numbered paragraph. That decipherment had never been used to recover the key: an earlier attempt on R1874 had read the letter from the decipherment and left the key unrebuilt.

02 Rebuilding the key by alignment

The decipherment of R1874’s paragraphs 5 to 16 was transcribed here, about 4,800 letters of plaintext. DECODE’s transcription of R1874’s cipher was then split into five stretches matching those paragraphs — the ratio is a steady 1.84 letters per group — and each stretch aligned to its plaintext by a soft-EM forward–backward pass over monotone segmentations, sharpened each round until every group settled on one value. The solver was checked first on a synthetic syllabic cipher built over the same text, which it recovered exactly (97 of 97 groups). The first run on the real data collapsed, because a group was allowed to stand for nothing and the frequent groups went null; capping values at four letters and forbidding nulls fixed it.

One detail decided the shape of the answer. DECODE’s transcriber wrote the figure 5 in two forms, as “13” and as “21”. Folding both to a single 5 gave a muddled key; keeping them apart — they are 51 and 53 in R1862’s clearer hand — made the table regular at once, because they are two different rows of it.

03 The key

The cipher is a syllabary laid out as a grid. The prefix gives the vowel and the last two digits the consonant:

  • vowels: 6 = a, 51 = e, 62 = i, 53 = o, 64 = u (61 is a second i/e row)
  • consonants: 07 b, 08 c, 09 l, 10 d, 11 f, 13 g, 14 gn, 17 gr, 19 l, 20 m, 21 n, 22 p, 23 pr, 24 qu, 26 ss, 27 r, 28 s, 31 st, 33 t, 34 tr, 37 v, 38 z — so 6210 di, 5133 te, 5333 to, 6433 tu, 5308 co, 6220 mi
  • single letters: 680 a, 6280 e, 6480 i, 5190 o, 6490 u, 692 n, 6292 r, 6482 l, 5390 s
  • word codes: 670 che, 672 con, 6270 in, 6249 il, 5102 del, 5302 nel, 5124 and 5387 quel, 5384 qual(che), 5374 per, 5142 duca, 6440 sua, 6442 suo, 6240 ave, 5373 part, 6477 poc, 6287 non, 5347 papa, 6247 Cesare
  • paragraph marks: 698, 5198, 5199 stand at the edges of the ciphered passages, as 6499 does in R1874

The word band is alphabetical. Above column 39 the codes of each prefix row run in alphabetical blocks — row 53 gives papa 47, part 73, per 74, piu 77, qual 84, quel 87, ques 88; row 64 gives signor 39, sua 40, suo 42. That brackets every code that is still open, and it is how several were read: 6274 falls just after in and is “molto” (“star molto oculati et avvertiti”), 5140 falls just below duca and is “dov” (“dovrian star”). It also caught three values inherited from the R1874 alignment that were wrong: 628 is sa, not “n” (“non sarian giamai”, “fatto sapere”); 6439 is signor, not “mini” (“Sua Signoria Illustrissima”, “il Signor Cardinal Barberino”); 6481 is a second i.

The grid cells that R1874’s decipherment never reached were filled by the structure itself (a cell’s value is its column’s consonant plus its row’s vowel), and the word codes were read from context in this letter: 5384 by “cavar anche qualche intimo particolare”, 5347 by “a fin che il Papa deliberasse”, 6240 by “avendomi ricercato”.

04 What the despatch says

It opens in the clear and turns to cipher in the middle of the first line:

Opportunissima occ.ne ho io havuta hieri col Cardinal Barberino di parlargli di quel negotio che lei mi si è commesso con cotesto Eccelso Conseglio, et di cavar anche qualche intimo particolare desiderato da lei…

What the ambassador had been asked to find out, and what the Cardinal had asked him, are the heart of it. Twice the Cardinal had wanted to know

…se Palma si era ben fortificava, […] o presidio aveva, se il passo della Pontebba si era ben assicurato…

— that is, whether the Republic’s great fortress of Palmanova and the Alpine pass above Venice were ready. Hearing from other quarters that “Imperiali uniti con Spagna parlavano con molto sprezzo” of the Republic, the ambassador answered by asking, “con intiera confidenza”, whether the questions had been put “a qualche fine”, and begged that anything of the kind be told him, so that the Serenissima might know in time “perché da lei fossero trovati i rimedii oportuni” — but without naming “quelli che trattassero simili” matters.

The rest of page 2 is the substance of the audience, and it turns on secrecy: such dealings “non sarian giamai […] palesi, ma ben sotto profondissima segretezza”, and the Cardinal spoke “aprendosi in co[nfidenti]ssima confidenza”. He had come by “particolar notitia de pensieri vasti”; of arms and men “che si trovavano in piedi nella Germania”, which left him “dubitando avessero qualche pensiero di molestare la Republica”; and he told the Cardinal that “quelli Signori dovrian star molto oculati et avvertiti”, so that he “non potei far di meno di non dimandarle” more. The Cardinal “interrogavami per dir il vero”, and the ambassador “mi sono andato certificando” that even if Spaniards and Imperialists had set “l’animo contro la Republica”, they “non sono in tu[tto]… effettuar i loro… disegni”.

The clear paragraph at the foot of page 2 and the top of page 3 carries the Cardinal’s argument that Venice need not fear an imperial descent: the Emperor ha pur troppo che fare in casa propria, having to watch the King of Denmark, Bethlen Gabor, the Dutch and perhaps the Turk; Wallenstein would not come into Italy without his whole army, and quartering it would take gran summa d’oro, di che l’Imp.re n’ha gran carestia. The ciphered part of page 3 reports the Cardinal on the imperial aulic council, “nel qual, se ben la maggior parte de’ consiglieri sia aderente al Re di Spagna”, and that he shows himself “più tosto amorevole che difidente”. It ends with the Cardinal offering “un particolare da esserne gran ri[f]lesso” and asking, in the line that had been missed out of the transcription altogether, “la prego non nominarme” — do not name me as the source.

Page 4, in the clear, lays the attempt on Genoa at the Duke of Savoy’s door, and argues that Spaniards and Imperialists, ill satisfied with him, will trust him less. Then, in cipher, the ambassador’s own move:

…ringratiai il cardinale di co[sì] confidenti communicatione, et sopra […] l’ultimo del Duca di Sivoia presi ocasione di caricar la mano a fin che il Papa deliberasse mandar la sua persona legato […] per acomodar gli interessi di Mantova e di Savoia insieme…

— pressing “la facilità che già si averia trovata nel Duca di Savoia di ridursi”, and the glory for His Holiness of “questa opportunità di dar pace”. The last ciphered lines return to the Cardinal, who “have dato il sudetto aviso… di Genova”. All of this is late April 1628: Vincenzo II Gonzaga was dead, Nevers was in Mantua, Spain and Savoy had agreed to partition Monferrato, and Urban VIII’s nephew was running a papal policy of professed neutrality.

05 How the transcription was made, and what is still open

The record has no transcription, so the 1,274 code groups were read off the images here and then checked, line by line, with the key in hand: a line that does not decipher into Italian is a line to look at again. Five passes of that kind were run. Two traps in this hand mattered: a final n-shape is a 2, not a 1, and a 7 is a “>” with a long open tail while an 8 is a hook plus a closed loop. Page 2’s lines bow by up to 35 px, and two of its transcribed rows turned out to have been blended from the head of one physical line and the tail of another, with a third row belonging to no line at all; tracking the baselines and re-splitting fixed the alignment, and the join it produced — “andavo dubitando avessero qualche pi | nsiero” across two lines — is its own confirmation.

Eleven passes of that kind were run in all, and two of them changed the shape of the text: one manuscript line on page 3 had never been transcribed at all (it reads “la prego non nominarme” — I beg you not to name me), and the lines slope enough that a fixed horizontal band picks up the neighbour’s ink on the right-hand side, which is what produced the blends.

What remains: fifteen group tokens (1.2%) have no value — 6478 and 5386 twice each, and 5349, 5389, 5370, 51708, 683, 5378, 5379, 6279, 5189, 699, 6241 once each. All of them sit in the word and name band, the part R1874’s decipherment does not cover, so only another letter in this cipher will settle them; none is digitised on DECODE, and the Venetian key registers that are (the Codice Amadi and the Busta 4 registers) are all of the previous century. Four lines — P1 L17, P2 L17, P2 L22, P2 L26 — are image-true after three independent readings each and still give no sense: they turn on those codes, or on the clerk’s own slips, for he writes sivoia for Savoia, germinia for Germania, dovore for dovere. The ambassador himself is still anonymous: he does not name himself, and the Calendar of State Papers Venetian, which calendars only the England-related despatches, has nothing from Rome for 29 April 1628.

The reading was measured, not asserted: each line’s decipherment is scored against an Italian character model of the period, calibrated on genuine Italian of the same length taken from R1874’s decipherment (real text of this length falls below −2.25 per character only 5% of the time), and scored with the edges of its neighbours so that a word split across a line end is not counted against it. By that measure 91% of the code groups stand in lines that read; a stricter 25-character sliding window gives 86%, the difference being the clerk’s own spelling.
