01 The documents

BnF Français 3829 (formerly Béthune 9323) is a volume of original letters from 1629–1630, mostly from Louis XIII, Richelieu and Gaston d'Orléans to the Queen Mother, Marie de Médicis. Items 37 and 38 are letters from Richelieu "à monsieur de Rancé": Denis Bouthillier, sieur de Rancé, councillor of state and secretary to the Queen Mother, and Richelieu's trusted man in her household. Item 37 is dated Saint-Privat, 7 July 1629; item 38 is undated (Avenel places it in mid-July). Both were written days after the Peace of Alès ended the Huguenot war, while the King and the Cardinal were touring the submitting towns of Languedoc.

Sensitive words and names are replaced by two-digit numbers, some carrying a diacritic; the rest is in clear. The transcription used here is the one published by S. Tomokiyo (richelieu1629.txt); the manuscript images are not freely available online.

02 The cipher system

A homophonic substitution with a small nomenclature, typical of French chancery practice under Louis XIII.

2.1 Letters (10–28): the 19-letter alphabet in scrambled order

10111213141516171819202122232425262728
cbgplorftadi/jnhmsu/veq

No symbol for x, y or z was observed; i/j and u/v are not distinguished, as usual in 1629.

2.2 Vowel homophones (29–38): a e i o u twice over

2930313233343536373839
aeiouaeiouet

So e can be written 27, 30 or 35; a 19, 29 or 34; and so on. 39 was first taken as a sixth a; checked against Avenel’s text on 23 October 2026, it is et at both its occurrences (“frontière et menée”, “Chevreuse et autres”). The regular a-e-i-o-u cycle makes the table easy to memorise. 33 does not occur in these two letters but is confirmed as u by Avenel's "ne piquer" (22 37 13 21 28 33 30 16) in a letter of 21 March 1629.

2.3 The diacritic: a doubling mark

A tilde or stroke over a figure doubles the letter: ~14 = ll, ~22 = nn, ~25 = ss, ~30 = ee, as in d'elle, bonne, donner, Brassac, ambassade, maistresse, amenée.

3.1 Letter of 7 July 1629 (f. 87), French

[Le roy] est fort en colère de l'action de Boneuil. Il remet à [la reyne mère] de le chasser, comme il (elle) l'estimera à propos; si l'affaire est certaine, il mériteroit pis.

Castor voudroit bien que la [duchesse de Chevreuse] peust estre atrapée près de la frontière et menée [?au] bois de Vincienes, auquel cas il faudroit que personne de dehors ne la vist.

Les cabales de [la Lorraine] avec [l'Espagne] par le moyen de la [duchesse de Chevreuse] et autres, sont insuportables.

La paix aura surpris ces esprits malins. A la vérité elle est miraculeuse, car elle couppe les racines du mal pour le présent et l'avenir. Anduze, Sauve, Le Vigan, Gange, Uzez, Aimargues et Nismes ont desjà obéy avec grande joye, et chaque place a donné ses ostages pour seureté de la démolition de leurs fortiffications. Il ne restera plus que le haut Languedoc, qui suivra bientost, Dieu aidant.

J'eusse volontiers servy Mr Vautier, mais l'abbaye estoit de long temps promise à [le cardinal de la Valette]. S'il se rencontre autre occasion je m'y emploieray volontiers avec affection.

J'ay parlé à Marcheuile de ce que Andreni a dit: il le nye tout à fait, et, en vérité, je ne croy pas qu'il voulust rien faire contre le service de [le roy].

Le roy accorde que Mrs des Estats puissent faire une recreue de 1500 ou 2000 hommes, mais il désire que ce soit en Normandie et non en Picardie et Champagne, parce qu'il a besoin de gens en ces deux provinces-là pour s'oposer à ses ennemis, au cas qu'il y soit ataqué. On représentera, et fera-on bien considérer à ces Mrs que c'est une grâce très particulière que Sa Majesté leur faict. Et si [l'Espagne] s'en plaint, il faut dire qu'on n'a donné ceste permission que lorsqu'on a veu que, contre sa parole donnée, il a fait entrer les Allemands en Italie.

3.2 Letter of 7 July 1629, English

[The King] is very angry at the action of Boneuil. He leaves it to [the Queen Mother] to dismiss him as she thinks fit; if the matter is certain, he would deserve worse.

Castor [the King] would much like [the Duchess of Chevreuse] to be caught near the frontier and taken [?to the] wood of Vincennes, in which case no one from outside must see her.

The cabals of [Lorraine] with [Spain], by means of [the Duchess of Chevreuse] and others, are unbearable.

The peace will have surprised those evil spirits. In truth it is miraculous, for it cuts the roots of the evil for the present and the future. Anduze, Sauve, Le Vigan, Ganges, Uzès, Aimargues and Nîmes have already obeyed with great joy, and every place has given its hostages as security for the demolition of its fortifications. Only upper Languedoc will remain, which will soon follow, God helping.

I would gladly have served Mr Vautier, but the abbey had long been promised to [the Cardinal de La Valette]. If another occasion arises I will gladly use myself for it with affection.

I have spoken to Marcheville about what Andreni [Anderny] said: he denies it entirely, and in truth I do not believe he would want to do anything against the service of [the King].

The King grants that the Messieurs of the States [the Dutch] may raise a levy of 1,500 or 2,000 men, but he wishes it to be in Normandy and not in Picardy and Champagne, because he needs men in those two provinces to oppose his enemies in case he is attacked. It will be represented to these gentlemen, and they will be made to consider well, that it is a very particular grace that His Majesty does them. And if [Spain] complains of it, it must be said that this permission was given only once it was seen that, against his given word, he had sent the Germans into Italy.

3.3 Letter "pour M. de Rancé", mid-July 1629 (f. 89), French

Nous sommes en peine de quoy la pais de [l'Angleterre] ne s'achève point; mandez-nous si l'on n'en a point de nouvelles.

[Le roy] est d'advis que [la reyne mère] prépare une armée pour la Champagne de dis mil hommes et huit cens chevaux. Il en fist dresser l'estat avant que partir. Mr de Beauclerc est là pour faire ce qu'il faut. La reyne fera, s'il luy plaist, délivrer ces commissions dès ceste heure, si elle apprend le moindre bruict que les ennemis pensent à s'approcher de la frontière. Elle fera délivrer l'argent de la levée afin qu'on ne soit pas surpris. Quand les ennemis de l'Estat penseront que de tous costés l'on est préparé à se deffendre, ils ne seront pas hardis à entreprendre. Partant, quand mesmes on n'auroit aucun mauvais advis de la frontière, il est toujours expédient de délivrer ces commissions, mais non de donner argent qu'on n'ayt lieu de soubçon. Le tout deppendra du jugement de la reyne; mais en matière de précaution il vaut mieux trop faire que trop peu.

On a fort bien fait d'envoyer Mr d'Ebeuf en Picardie, et tous les gouverneurs. Il en faut faire autant en Champagne.

[La reyne mère] doibt, renvoiant ce gentilhomme de la [l'Angleterre], luy escrire des lettres obligeantes et pleines d'amour, et qui dient aux termes qu'il faut que [la reyne mère] se portera tousjours, pour l'amour d'elle ainsy que elle l'en a prié, à une bonne union entre [le roy] et [l'Angleterre].

[La reyne mère] peut passer jusques là de dire qu'il (elle) peut l'asseurer qu'outre l'evesque et les pretres qui doivent estre pres d'elle il ne voit pas qu'on ait dessein de luy donner d'autres François que ceux qui sont pres d'ele; mais il faut que ce soit une lettre secrete, ou bien le dire en grande confiance à Vantelet qui aura créance par la letre de [la reyne mère], ce que j'estime plus à propos; luy recommandant de ne le dire qu'à sa maitresse; et cependant il le dira aus autres, qui est ce qu'on doit desirer. Il est à souhaiter que cette pais se face et promptement.

J'ay proposé au roy Mr de Brassac pour l'ambassade de Rome. Vous sçaurez de [la reyne mère] s'il (si elle) aura agréable cette proposition; et, au cas qu'il (elle) l'agrée, vous irez trouver ledit Sr de Brassac, et luy direz la proposition que j'ai faite à [le roy] et [la reyne mère] de luy, en ceste occasion, et que si c'est chose qu'il désire, il peut dès ceste heure, s'asseurer des meubles qui luy sont nécessaires pour cet ambassade.

3.4 Letter "pour M. de Rancé", English

We are anxious that the peace with [England] is not being concluded; let us know whether there is any news of it.

[The King] is of the opinion that [the Queen Mother] should prepare an army for Champagne of ten thousand men and eight hundred horse. He had the list of it drawn up before leaving. Mr de Beauclerc is there to do what is needed. The Queen will, if she pleases, have these commissions issued at once, if she hears the least rumour that the enemy intends to approach the frontier. She will have the money for the levy paid out so that we are not taken by surprise. When the enemies of the State think that on all sides we are ready to defend ourselves, they will not be bold enough to attempt anything. Therefore, even if there were no bad news from the frontier, it is always expedient to issue these commissions, but not to give money unless there is cause for suspicion. Everything will depend on the Queen's judgement; but in a matter of precaution it is better to do too much than too little.

It was very well done to send Mr d'Elbeuf to Picardy, and all the governors. The same must be done in Champagne.

[The Queen Mother] must, in sending back this gentleman of [the Queen of England], write her obliging letters full of love, saying in so many words that [the Queen Mother] will always strive, for the love of her as she has asked her, for a good union between [the King] and [England].

[The Queen Mother] may go so far as to say that she can assure her that, besides the bishop and the priests who are to be near her, she sees no intention of giving her other Frenchmen than those who are near her; but this must be a secret letter, or else it should be said in great confidence to Vantelet, who will be given credence by the letter of [the Queen Mother] — which I think more appropriate — recommending him to tell it only to his mistress; and meanwhile he will tell it to the others, which is what is to be desired. It is to be wished that this peace be made, and promptly.

I have proposed to the King Mr de Brassac for the embassy to Rome. You will learn from [the Queen Mother] whether she approves of this proposal; and if she approves, you will go and find the said Sieur de Brassac and tell him of the proposal I have made to [the King] and [the Queen Mother] concerning him on this occasion, and that if it is something he wishes, he can from this moment provide himself with the furniture he needs for this embassy.

3.5 Historical context

The ciphered items are the sensitive ones. The Duchesse de Chevreuse, the court's most dangerous intriguer, then in Lorraine and in contact with Spain, was to be seized at the border and shut up in the château of Vincennes, out of sight. The Anglo-French peace (signed at Susa, 24 April 1629) was stalling over Charles I's treatment of his French queen Henrietta Maria, whose Catholic household he had expelled in 1626; Richelieu wants the Queen Mother to reassure her daughter, by a "secret" letter or through the queen's maître d'hôtel Vantelet (which he expects to leak), that France will not press to reinstall a large French entourage beyond the agreed bishop and priests. Finally, Jean de Galard de Béarn, comte de Brassac, is proposed for the Rome embassy; he took up the post in 1630, an external check on the decipherment.

04 Method

  1. Parsing. 421 cipher symbols, 46 distinct, in 58 runs interleaved with cleartext. Numbers above 40 were treated as whole-word codes and used as context breaks; cleartext words adjacent to cipher runs were kept as known plaintext.
  2. Language model. French letter quadgram statistics were built from ~1.8 million characters of Project Gutenberg text (Sévigné, Retz, Pascal, Corneille, Dumas), normalised to a 22-letter alphabet with j→i, v→u and accents removed.
  3. Search. Simulated annealing over the symbol→letter map, scored by quadgram log-probability plus a unigram-frequency penalty (needed because an unconstrained homophonic search collapses onto e/s/r/t). An early, wrong constraint (forcing 10–31 to be a bijection with the alphabet) still produced readable fragments (LA{58}, TRES, UNE, FRANC…).
  4. Cribs. From those fragments: d'autres (fixing d a u t r e s and exposing 30 as a second e); d'elle … elle (revealing the doubling mark); then Brassac and l'ambassade after "Mr de" and "pour". With those fixed, every remaining letter was fixed from huit cens, la paix, esprits, secrète, se face, Vincennes, frontière, Bonneuil.
  5. Structure. The recovered values showed 10–28 to be a complete 19-letter alphabet and 29–38 two a-e-i-o-u cycles; 33 = u was predicted from the pattern before being confirmed.

05 Verification: Avenel, 1858

While searching for the meaning of the nomenclature codes, the letters were found already printed, with cipher numbers and footnote decipherments, in Lettres, instructions diplomatiques et papiers d'État du cardinal de Richelieu, ed. D.-L.-M. Avenel, vol. III (Paris, 1858): no. CXCIX, pp. 368–369 (f. 87) and no. CCV, pp. 381–383 (f. 89). Avenel writes (p. 262, n. 2) that he did not find the key among the Béthune manuscripts "mais nous l'avons recomposé à l'aide de documents que nous avons trouvés aux archives des Affaires étrangères".

His readings and the independent reconstruction agree in every ciphered word. Avenel's transcription also settles a handful of digits misread in the modern transcription (38 for 28 in l'évesque; 25 for 28 in souhaiter que; 35 for 25 in près d'elle; 25 for 26 in Vautier; 16 for 26 in Vantelet; 14 for 24 in de Rome; 22/11 for 23/13 in Champagne; 47 for 17 in frontière), none of which affected the alphabet. It also identifies the nomenclature: 51 le Roi, 52 la Reine mère, 58 Mme de Chevreuse, 66 La Valette, 83 l'Espagne, 84 l'Angleterre, 92 la Lorraine.

Correction to the catalogues. BnF Français 3829 ff. 87 and 89 (DECODE R9461, R9462) should be marked as deciphered, with the reference Avenel III (1858), pp. 368–369 and 381–383. The key table above, which Avenel never printed as a table, is offered as a convenience for anyone meeting the same cipher in Richelieu's 1629 correspondence with the Queen Mother's household.

## Translation

**f. 87 — To Monsieur de Rancé, councillor of the King in his Council of State and secretary of the commands of the Queen his mother, at Paris. 7 July 1629.**

{The King} is very angry at the action *of Boneuil*. He leaves it to {the Queen Mother} *to dismiss him* as she thinks fit; if the matter is certain, he would deserve *worse*.

Castor [the King] would much like {the Duchess of Chevreuse} to be *caught near the frontier and taken* [?to the] *wood of Vincennes*, in which case no one *from outside must see her*.

*The cabals of* {Lorraine} with {Spain}, by means of {the Duchess of Chevreuse} *and* others, are unbearable.

The peace will have surprised *those evil spirits*. In truth it is miraculous, for it cuts the roots of the evil for the present and the future. Anduze, Sauve, Le Vigan, Ganges, Uzès, Aimargues and Nîmes have already obeyed with great joy, and every place has given its hostages as security for the demolition of its fortifications. Only upper Languedoc will remain, which will soon follow, God helping.

I would gladly have served Mr *Vautier*, but the abbey had long been promised to {the Cardinal de La Valette}. If another occasion arises I will gladly use myself for it with affection.

I have spoken to *Marcheville* about what *Andreni* [Anderny] said: he denies it entirely, and in truth I do not believe he would want to do anything against the service of {the King}.

The King grants that the Messieurs of the States [the Dutch] may raise a levy of 1,500 or 2,000 men, but he wishes it to be in Normandy and not in Picardy and Champagne, because he needs men in those two provinces to oppose his enemies in case he is attacked. It will be represented to these gentlemen, and they will be made to consider well, that it is a very particular grace that His Majesty does them. And if {Spain} complains of it, it must be said that this permission was given only once it was seen that, against his given word, he had sent the Germans into Italy.

**f. 89 — For M. de Rancé (undated; mid-July 1629).**

We are anxious that *the peace with* {England} is not being concluded; let us know whether there is any news of it.

{The King} is of the opinion that {the Queen Mother} should prepare an army for *Champagne* of *ten thousand* men and *eight hundred* horse. He had the list of it drawn up before leaving. Mr de Beauclerc is there to do what is needed. The Queen will, if she pleases, have these commissions issued at once, if she hears the least rumour that the enemy intends to approach the frontier. She will have the money for the levy paid out so that we are not taken by surprise. When the enemies of the State think that on all sides we are ready to defend ourselves, they will not be bold enough to attempt anything. Therefore, even if there were no bad news from the frontier, it is always expedient to issue these commissions, but not to give money unless there is cause for suspicion. Everything will depend on the Queen's judgement; but in a matter of precaution it is better to do too much than too little.

It was very well done to send Mr d'Elbeuf to Picardy, and all the governors. The same must be done in Champagne.

{The Queen Mother} must, in sending back this gentleman of {the Queen of England}, write her obliging letters full *of love*, saying in so many words that {the Queen Mother} will always strive, for the love *of her* as *she* has asked her, for *a good union between* {the King} *and* {England}.

{The Queen Mother} may go so far as to say that she can assure her that, besides *the bishop and the priests* who are to be *near her*, she sees no intention of giving her *other Frenchmen than* those who are *near her*; but this must be *a secret letter*, or else it should be said in great confidence to *Vantelet*, who will be given credence by *the letter of* {the Queen Mother} — which I think more appropriate — recommending him *to tell it only to his mistress*; and meanwhile he *will tell it to the* others, which is what *is to be desired*. It is *to be wished that* this *peace be made, and* promptly.

I have proposed to the King Mr de *Brassac* for *the embassy to Rome*. You will learn from {the Queen Mother} whether she approves of this proposal; and if she approves, you will go and find the said Sieur de *Brassac* and tell him of the proposal I have made to {the King} *and* {the Queen Mother} concerning him on this occasion, and that if it is something he wishes, he can from this moment provide himself *with the furniture* he needs for this *embassy*.

## Translation

**f. 87 — To Monsieur de Rancé, councillor of the King in his Council of State and secretary of the commands of the Queen his mother, at Paris. 7 July 1629.**

{The King} is very angry at the action *of Boneuil*. He leaves it to {the Queen Mother} *to dismiss him* as she thinks fit; if the matter is certain, he would deserve *worse*.

Castor [the King] would much like {the Duchess of Chevreuse} to be *caught near the frontier and taken* [?to the] *wood of Vincennes*, in which case no one *from outside must see her*.

*The cabals of* {Lorraine} with {Spain}, by means of {the Duchess of Chevreuse} *and* others, are unbearable.

The peace will have surprised *those evil spirits*. In truth it is miraculous, for it cuts the roots of the evil for the present and the future. Anduze, Sauve, Le Vigan, Ganges, Uzès, Aimargues and Nîmes have already obeyed with great joy, and every place has given its hostages as security for the demolition of its fortifications. Only upper Languedoc will remain, which will soon follow, God helping.

I would gladly have served Mr *Vautier*, but the abbey had long been promised to {the Cardinal de La Valette}. If another occasion arises I will gladly use myself for it with affection.

I have spoken to *Marcheville* about what *Andreni* [Anderny] said: he denies it entirely, and in truth I do not believe he would want to do anything against the service of {the King}.

The King grants that the Messieurs of the States [the Dutch] may raise a levy of 1,500 or 2,000 men, but he wishes it to be in Normandy and not in Picardy and Champagne, because he needs men in those two provinces to oppose his enemies in case he is attacked. It will be represented to these gentlemen, and they will be made to consider well, that it is a very particular grace that His Majesty does them. And if {Spain} complains of it, it must be said that this permission was given only once it was seen that, against his given word, he had sent the Germans into Italy.

**f. 89 — For M. de Rancé (undated; mid-July 1629).**

We are anxious that *the peace with* {England} is not being concluded; let us know whether there is any news of it.

{The King} is of the opinion that {the Queen Mother} should prepare an army for *Champagne* of *ten thousand* men and *eight hundred* horse. He had the list of it drawn up before leaving. Mr de Beauclerc is there to do what is needed. The Queen will, if she pleases, have these commissions issued at once, if she hears the least rumour that the enemy intends to approach the frontier. She will have the money for the levy paid out so that we are not taken by surprise. When the enemies of the State think that on all sides we are ready to defend ourselves, they will not be bold enough to attempt anything. Therefore, even if there were no bad news from the frontier, it is always expedient to issue these commissions, but not to give money unless there is cause for suspicion. Everything will depend on the Queen's judgement; but in a matter of precaution it is better to do too much than too little.

It was very well done to send Mr d'Elbeuf to Picardy, and all the governors. The same must be done in Champagne.

{The Queen Mother} must, in sending back this gentleman of {the Queen of England}, write her obliging letters full *of love*, saying in so many words that {the Queen Mother} will always strive, for the love *of her* as *she* has asked her, for *a good union between* {the King} *and* {England}.

{The Queen Mother} may go so far as to say that she can assure her that, besides *the bishop and the priests* who are to be *near her*, she sees no intention of giving her *other Frenchmen than* those who are *near her*; but this must be *a secret letter*, or else it should be said in great confidence to *Vantelet*, who will be given credence by *the letter of* {the Queen Mother} — which I think more appropriate — recommending him *to tell it only to his mistress*; and meanwhile he *will tell it to the* others, which is what *is to be desired*. It is *to be wished that* this *peace be made, and* promptly.

I have proposed to the King Mr de *Brassac* for *the embassy to Rome*. You will learn from {the Queen Mother} whether she approves of this proposal; and if she approves, you will go and find the said Sieur de *Brassac* and tell him of the proposal I have made to {the King} *and* {the Queen Mother} concerning him on this occasion, and that if it is something he wishes, he can from this moment provide himself *with the furniture* he needs for this *embassy*.
