Lwów → an envoy bound for Holland · Latin syllabic cipher with homophones · 27 June 1711 · existing decipherment

Ferenc Rákóczi II to an envoy, Lwów, 27 June 1711

Rákóczi’s own letter against the Peace of Szatmár, in a Latin syllabic cipher, was deciphered between the lines by his clerk; the decipherment was transcribed here, and the lost key was rebuilt from it to check it.

Daniel Bourdeau · posted 15 October 2026

Method: read from existing decipherment · Extent: complete

Credits · Contemporary key or decipherment (key or decipherment used)

At a glanceExisting decipherment · 2,524 of 2,531 groups under the clerk’s interlinear

The letter
Ferenc Rákóczi II to an unnamed envoy on his way to Holland, Lwów, 27 June 1711, six pages signed in autograph “Franciscus Princeps”.
Where
Magyar Nemzeti Levéltár, P237 Festetics 10. d. 2. 42–44; DECODE R483, dated there 7 June. It was catalogued with three French letters to Rákóczi (catalogue 44).
The cipher
A numerical Latin syllabary with homophones: several numbers for each common letter, codes for open syllables, a few for words and names. Cipher and clear alternate within the lines.
How it was deciphered
A clerk wrote the decipherment above every ciphered stretch. It was transcribed with the cipher, and an alignment of the two rebuilt 224 codes of the key, which reproduce 70% of the clerk’s letters.
What it says
Rákóczi rejects the Peace of Szatmár and a king proclaimed outside a free diet, asks the Tsar and Poland to mediate, and reports Pálffy’s guns before Munkács.
Still open
An edited Latin text line by line, and the clerk’s doubtful words where the key does not settle them.

This letter was filed with three French reports sent to Rákóczi, which use the Bonac–Groffey key DECODE R639. It is in a different cipher; those letters have their own page: three letters to Rákóczi, 1706–1707.

01 The letter

The letter is signed in autograph “Franciscus Princeps” and dated in clear “Leopoli die 27. Junii Anno 1711”; DECODE’s 7 June is a misreading. It is addressed to “Fidelis nobis sincere dilecte”, an envoy on his way to Holland, who is in poor health and handles money and the mathematical instruments ordered from Mangold. A clear postscript deals with those instruments. The six pages carry 2,531 cipher groups in 180 lines, transcribed here from the images; cipher and clear alternate within the lines.

02 What it says

Rákóczi sends a copy of the “Tractatus sic dictae Pacis Carolianae”, the Peace of Szatmár, which he calls a fraud worked out in Vienna, and reports that Archduke Charles was proclaimed king in every Hungarian county, contrary to the 1687 article of the Pozsony diet. He will not recognise a king proclaimed outside a free diet:

nos Regem extra dietam promulgatum, non deposito [juramento], agnoscere non posse sine … ruina [libertatum nostrarum].

He appeals to the Tsar and the Polish Republic to mediate. He offers to come to a free diet in person or by plenipotentiaries and to recognise a lawfully crowned king, provided the confederates recover their estates. He calls the Allies’ assurances about Hungarian liberties a “diversio armorum” for Austria and asks for the war to go on. Page 4 reports Pálffy’s artillery before “arcem nostram Munkatsiensem” and the restitution of Transylvanian liberty. Page 5 turns to the general confederation in Poland, letters to go “per Saxoniam”, the Muscovite advance, a warning to trust only news sent directly from Rákóczi, and complaints about the Grand Chancellor’s “indiscretum procedendi modum” and about Ráday. Page 6 concerns receipts left with a Jew and a member of the Economic Council who took treasury money for his own use.

03 The decipherment and the key

The clerk wrote the decipherment above every ciphered stretch. Of the 2,531 groups, 2,524 lie on a line with an interlinear; the other seven continue words written on the neighbouring line (a one-group ending, a catchword repeated at the head of the next page, and three groups whose words stand on the line above).

The key is not preserved. Each cipher line was aligned with the Latin above it by an alignment algorithm (hard expectation-maximisation), with each code taking up to four letters. The result is a syllabic cipher with homophones. Single letters have several values (190 and 430 s; 140 and 380 n; 60 and 300 e; 20 and 260 a; 130 and 370 m), open syllables have their own codes (257 re, 428 ti, 68 se, 362 de, 96 no, 457 ri, 148 si, 92 ce, 152 co, 59 tu, 378 ta), and a few words and names are coded whole. Of 224 codes, 156 occur at least twice. Decoding the letter with this key reproduces 3,180 of the clerk’s 4,547 letters (69.9%). The mismatches are rare codes, homophones seen once and the clerk’s own abbreviations. Where the clerk’s handwriting is doubtful, the cipher often settles the word, as in the sentence quoted above and in “ne fors etiam”.

The Bonac–Groffey table R639, which deciphers the French letters once filed with this one, gives nonsense on it.

04 What remains uncertain

  • The clerk’s doubtful words, marked in the transcription; the key settles some of them.
  • 68 of the 224 rebuilt codes occur only once (C).
  • The recipient is not named.
  • A line-by-line edited Latin text has not been made yet; the clerk’s text and the key’s reading are both in the repository.
