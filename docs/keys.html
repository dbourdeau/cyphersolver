The Key Web

A cipher is rarely broken in isolation. A key rebuilt from one letter's margin is tried on its sibling letters. A contemporary key turns up filed under the next record number. A table published by Lasry or Tomokiyo is turned on a letter they never saw. And many keys are tried and do not fit. This page follows every key behind the site's decipherments: who made it, which letters it was used on, and where it failed.

One key, one letter. keys used on a single letter that no other key touches. Each mark is one pair at its date: the key on the left, the letter on the right.

Letters sit roughly by date, left to right. Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan or to move a node. Hover a key to trace it; click to pin it and open its card. Turn on tried, did not fit to add the keys that were tested and rejected: the web redraws, and any key and letter joined by a failed test move up from the strip.

Keys tried and ruled out

Testing a key that does not fit is half of the work. These are the named keys the notes record as tried on a letter and rejected, with the reason.

    The key web

    Pick a key

    Click a key to see where it came from, the letters it was used on and those it failed on; click a letter to see which keys were used on it. The biggest keys did the most work: made for one letter, they went on to open the rest of its series. Every key has its own link: copy it from the card.

    About the data

    Every link is taken from an explicit statement in the target's notes: that a named key was used on a letter, unchanged or adapted, that a key was rebuilt from that letter's own material, or that a named key was tested on it and did not fit. Links are not inferred from resemblance. A key counts as rebuilt here when this project recovered it, from the ciphertext, from glosses or from a decipherment; keys recovered by other people (Lasry, Tomokiyo, Somogyi, and outside contributors) are scholars' keys; keys of the period are those that survive in the archives or on DECODE. A scholar's key is placed at the date of the cipher, not of its publication, which the card gives separately. Several of George Lasry's tables for the French correspondence of the 1520s and 1530s appear as one key. Letters taken straight from a contemporary decipherment, with no key rebuilt, are not shown. A key and a letter that connect to nothing else sit in the date strip under the web. The file is keys.json; the site build checks it.
