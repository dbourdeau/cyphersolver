LES ANGLOIS N’EMPIETENT AVCVNE CHOSE EN ESCOSSE

French court → Scotland · homophonic nomenclator · 1565–67 or 1572

Charles IX to Philibert du Croc

Archives départementales de la Nièvre, fonds du Bourg de Bozas, exact cote unreported · two pages, French, wholly ciphered · DECODE R2789, no decipherment on the record · catalogue item 34

George Lasry deciphered the letter, and his full-page plaintext overlays were published on Satoshi Tomokiyo’s Cryptiana in 2022. They were compared with the DECODE images and Destray’s facsimile and transcribed; no key was rebuilt here. Measured on the words of the transcription, the letter builds coherent sentences but reads at 92.8%, under the 95% bar.

01 The document and the date correction

Paul Destray printed the address as “A monsr du Croq, mon conseiller et me d’hostel ordinaire et mon ambassadeur en Escosse” and catalogued the item as document XLIII, S. l. n. d.: without place or date. He noted that the letter, its addition, and the King’s second signature were in cipher; Pinart countersigned it. The two following facsimile leaves are the same images now served by DECODE.

DECODE’s structured date spans 1550–1574 but supplies day and month as 1 January; the catalogue flattened that into “1 Jan 1550.” That cannot be a document date: Charles IX was born on 27 June 1550. Cryptiana’s cautious “1565–1567 or 1572” follows the periods in which du Croc served in Scotland and is retained here. The contents fit the minority of James VI, but they do not distinguish those missions securely.

02 George Lasry’s prior solution

Tomokiyo’s page George Lasry’s Solutions of Unsolved Ciphers has a section headed “Charles IX to Philibert du Croc (1565–1567 or 1572).” Beneath the manuscript it says “This is solved as follows” and reproduces Lasry’s plaintext in red directly over the signs on both pages. The overlay lies on the same two pages: page geometry, line breaks, signatures and the postscript agree throughout.

The system is a French homophonic nomenclator: several graphic signs can represent letters, while other signs stand for whole words or names. Lasry’s displayed key includes values such as de, et, que, par, pour, je, vous, la Royne d’Angleterre, la Royne d’Escosse, and royaulme. It is not the symbol system used in Catherine de Médicis’s separate letter to du Croc of 27 April 1567.

03 What the cipher says

The opening tells du Croc that he knows both the man and those with whom he must deal, and that his demonstrated devotion makes further advice unnecessary. He is to follow the instructions already given and those now sent, especially “le bien de la paix du dict pais.” Charles then turns from what can presently be done for “ma seur” to the young King:

Je desire que mon nepveu aussy sur toutes choses soit bien nourri, qu’il ait tousjours, à l’imitation de ses predecesseurs, à moi et aux miens sa principalle affection.

Du Croc is to attend closely to the settlement of Scotland’s peace, government, and council. Without revealing that the direction comes from Charles, he must ensure that James neither marries nor concludes a marriage before Charles has been informed and can send his intention. Above all, he is to retain the hearts of the Scots so that their accustomed affection for the French kings does not diminish.

The postscript is blunter:

Ce que vous avez principalement affaire par delà est de donner ordre que les Anglois n’empiètent aucune chose en Escosse…

Du Croc must act prudently, make the Scots recognize that France seeks their preservation, and avoid occasions that could obstruct the friendship. The closing lines mention the English ambassadors and a possible treaty, but the gaps in the overlay are too large for a continuous translation. The repository preserves a line-by-line diplomatic transcription and a normalized summary.

04 What remains uncertain

  • The original has no surviving date or place; 1565–67 or 1572 is a range of possible dates.
  • Destray names the Nièvre archive and the Bourg de Bozas collection but gives no exact cote.
  • Lasry’s overlay has several explicit `????` groups. Other short phrases are partly obscured by the signs at the available resolution.
  • No independent symbol-by-symbol transcription was made and no complete key was rebuilt here. The outcome is an existing decipherment, transcribed and checked against the images.

## Normalized sense

Charles says that du Croc knows the men with whom he must deal and needs no further advice. He should follow his earlier and current instructions, especially where the peace of Scotland is concerned. Because pressure cannot presently be applied on behalf of “my sister” without harming her, Charles turns to the welfare of “my nephew”: James VI is to be well brought up, retain the traditional affection of his predecessors for the French king and his house, and be surrounded by a peace, government, and council favorable to that end.

Du Croc is to ensure discreetly that James neither marries nor concludes a marriage before Charles is informed and can communicate his wishes. He must retain the hearts of the Scots and prevent any diminution of their accustomed attachment to France.

The postscript makes the strategic aim explicit: the English must not be allowed to encroach on Scotland. Du Croc is to act prudently so that the Scots recognize France's desire for their preservation, while avoiding incidents that could obstruct the friendship. Several final clauses—including part of the passage about the English ambassadors and a possible treaty—remain too incomplete in the published overlay for a responsible continuous translation.

# Charles IX to Philibert du Croc, undated (1565-67 or 1572): English translation

I know […] you know […] well the man […] those with […] you have to deal; and being assured of the affection you have
for my service, [I know] there is no need to give other advice […] you will have to deal in Scotland [for] the good of my
affairs, also referring myself to the instruction that was given separately and to the one now sent […] you will well
know how to follow likewise in what concerns the good of the peace of the said country; since […] at this hour no
instance can be made for my sister […] for fear of harming her, and […] I shall say […] opening it. I wish that my nephew
also, above all things, be well brought up; that he should always, in imitation of his predecessors, bear his principal
affection to me and mine; and that you conduct yourself […] well in the things you will see that are of importance in
the negotiation of the establishment of the said peace, order, government and council of that country; and you will
see to it, without showing […] that it comes from my part or from me, that he does not marry […] before I am first
informed of it, as will be easy by way of […] or through Normandy […] to see that there is safety, so that he may send
him my intention before the conclusion is reached. But above all act so that […] can have no distrust of your actions,
and nevertheless do not fail to serve me and always win the hearts of the Scots, so that they lessen nothing of the
affection they have been accustomed to bear my predecessors and me.

What you have principally to do over there is to give order that the English encroach on nothing in Scotland, but that
[…] remain always entire. You are so well advised that you will know how to conduct yourself very prudently in this as
is needed, so that the Scots know that […] desires their preservation, and that […] and her ministers cannot take any
bad occasion from it that would hinder the effect of our friendship. […] The ambassadors of England have been satisfied
according to the […] […] instruction only, at which I have been very glad […] will come to conclude […] treaty that you
will make […] wishes […] you have one; that will serve you as a pretext to send to ask me for one; and meanwhile you will
inform me of everything […]
