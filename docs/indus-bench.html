Indus script · a tool

Test a decipherment of the Indus script

Paste a sign key. The bench reads the corpus with it, and with 100 copies of the same key whose values are shuffled among signs of similar frequency. A real decipherment should read its language better than its own shuffles do.

Loading the corpus and the lexicons…

Values are written in Latin transliteration (IAST or similar). They are reduced to coarse consonant classes (k/g, c/j, t/d with retroflexes, p/b, the nasals, m, y, r, l, v, s/ś/ṣ/h) with the vowels dropped, as the script is taken to leave them to the reader. A text reads to the extent its consonants parse into words of the lexicon, plus one case-ending consonant. The score is the share of consonants covered by words of three or more consonants.

Three warnings from the full bench (the write-up). A key fitted blindly to the corpus reads about 93% of it in any language, so a high reading rate means nothing on its own. The consonants-only score cannot even recognise the accepted decipherment of Linear Elamite; keeping the vowels can, so use that. And beating the shuffles is necessary, not sufficient: a key whose author tuned it to give pronounceable syllables beats its shuffles on any lexicon of short words, in whatever language. A key needs about twenty signs before the test says anything.

How the numbers are made

Two real decipherments were used as checks. The Linear Elamite key (Desset and colleagues 2022), with Hallock’s Elamite glossary, beats 96 of 100 shuffles with the vowels kept. The Linear B key (Mycenaean Greek) does not beat its shuffles in any setting, even with the Greek word list adjusted to Linear B spelling. So a key that fails here is not shown to be wrong; what the bench does show is that a high reading rate, on its own, proves nothing.

The corpus is the 2,088 lines of three or more signs in the ICIT-derived dump of the Indus inscriptions, in reading order, on ICIT (Wells) glyph ids. The lexicons are the headwords of Monier-Williams (Cologne Digital Sanskrit Dictionaries), the forms of the Dravidian Etymological Dictionary (DEDR, all languages), and the nouns of the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary (ePSD2). Only their consonant skeletons are bundled. The shuffles permute the key’s values among keyed signs in bands of ten by frequency (three for keys of fewer than sixty signs), so each shuffled key has the same values on signs of about the same frequency. The anchor test counts, for the eight copper-tablet texts written on the back of a picture, how often the reading contains a word for that animal in the claimed language. The code is indus/bench.py; this page is its in-browser version.
