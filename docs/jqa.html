John Quincy Adams at St Petersburg: the lines of No. 88 that Ford left out

→

Go to the full text ↓

Adams wrote in his own legation code, not Armstrong’s; its key was rebuilt from the State Department clerk’s decodes and Ford’s printed decipherments, and the passages Ford left out of despatch No. 88 now decrypt, 380 of 390 groups.

The letter
John Quincy Adams, American minister at St Petersburg, to the Secretary of State (James Monroe), despatch No. 88, 25 June 1812, the day after Napoleon crossed the Niemen.
Where
National Archives, RG 59, Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Russia, microfilm M35 reel 3, frames 0206–0207 (the triplicate). Ford’s Writings of John Quincy Adams 4:355–58 prints it with “[Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” and the remainder “undecyphered”.
The cipher
A numbered syllabary of about 1,600 numbers (syllables, word-parts, common words), in which the is 1385 and of 1576. It is not the Armstrong code (the = 972), which the Madison editors name.
How it was decrypted
The key was recovered to 1,166 numbers: from the clerk’s pencil decodes on fourteen frames of the same reel, and by aligning coded blocks of 1811–1812 with the Department’s decipherments that Ford prints.
What it says
Chancellor Rumyantsev had opposed the Emperor’s journey to Wilna, had two apoplectic strokes there, and could not stay long in office; France called Kurakin’s note a proposal it “had never thought of making to Russia even after the battle of Friedland”.
Still open
Seven groups cut by the binding gutter on the film (need the original) and three single groups (1508, 1185, 570).
Credit
The State Department clerk’s interlinear decodes of 1811–12 and Worthington Chauncey Ford’s printed decipherments (1914) supplied the plaintext the key was rebuilt from.

This page was split on 14 October 2026 from a combined page (legation.html) that also held George W. Erving’s Madrid letter of 1807, which is in Pinckney’s legation code, a different cipher with its own page: Erving to Madison, 24 March 1807.

01 The premise checked

The starting point was a note the Madison editors attach to Armstrong’s letters (Founders Madison/02-10-02-0369 and 02-12-02-0197): the code was “also used by John Quincy Adams at St. Petersburg and by Jacob Lewis at Saint-Domingue; key not found but substantially reconstructed by the editors”. On Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811 (03-03-02-0121), the editors speak of “a code provided by the State Department to John Armstrong in France and to both William Short and Adams”, citing Ford 3:328. If that held, the 580-group Armstrong table already rebuilt on this site (Armstrong’s postscript) would decrypt Adams’s coded despatches. It does not.

  • What Ford’s own text says. Secretary Smith’s instructions of 1809 (Ford 3:327–28) say that “the cypher with which you are furnished” is “the same with that of our minister at London” (William Pinkney), and tell Adams to “obtain at Paris, a copy of General Armstrong’s cypher also, for the like purpose”, that is, to correspond with Armstrong. In 1810 Adams acknowledges “the copy of General Armstrong’s cypher” (Ford 3:370). He held both. The despatches to Washington use the other one.
  • What the microfilm shows. Every coded despatch checked on M35 reel 3 (1811 and 1812) uses a code in which 1385 = the, 1576 = of, 1384 = that, 668 = and, 1401 = I. In Armstrong’s code the is 972 and of is 1354. Run through the Armstrong table (592 entries as it now stands), these pages come out as noise. Reel 2 frame 0155, of 1810, is in the same Adams code.

So the table already held cannot decrypt Adams’s despatches. The editors’ note on the 7 January 1811 letter concerns a private letter to Madison, not a despatch, and was not checked against its manuscript, which is in the Forbes collection and not online; it may be right for that letter.

02 Adams’s code rebuilt

The method is the one used for Armstrong on M34 roll 13, with a second source added. On M35 reel 3 the Department clerk wrote the decode above the groups of several despatches; every pair on frames 0047, 0048 (1811), 0182 (No. 80, 29 February 1812), 0183, 0184, 0185, 0189, 0190, 0194, 0198, 0206 (No. 88), 0210–0211 and 0247 was recorded with frame, page side and line. For most pages there is no interlinear decode, but Ford printed the Department’s decipherments. Coded blocks were transcribed from the film and aligned with Ford’s plaintext, group against syllable, and checked by eye: 39 blocks of 1811, and then eight despatches of October 1811 to September 1812, No. 95 against the Department’s own clear copy on frames 0230–0232. All the pairs, 3,127 from the clerk (with the earlier pair list) and 2,785 from Ford, were merged into one table of 1,166 numbers, each with a grade:

GradeMeaningNumbers
Hthe clerk’s interlinear decode over the group431
Cfixed by Ford’s printed decipherment between known neighbours312
Mthe split of the syllables or the digit is a judgement307
Iinferred from the alphabetical slot and the sense only20

The code is a numbered syllabary of about 1,600 numbers: syllables, word-parts and common words, with 1–100 mostly whole words (13 observation, 16 Emperor, 29 United States, 44 negotiation, 78 President). An earlier version of this page described it as alphabetical blocks of fifty; the larger table does not bear that out. Values climb alphabetically only inside short runs, such as 1384 that, 1385 the, 1387 them, 1388 then, 1391 there, 1392 these, 1393 they, 1394 think, 1399 this, or 1592 on, 1594 one, 1595 ong, 1597 only, and then the run breaks (1304 tain and 1312 form sit among 1305 Bal, 1310 be, 1317 been). The alphabetical slot therefore brackets a value only where the neighbours on both sides are in order, and inference from it is kept to grade I. A superscript o after a group adds -s; a mark under a group changes it (a doubled letter, -ed, or another word: 1107 paper, 1107 with the mark well; 1457 last, with the mark ll; 1535 al, with the mark all). A few groups carry two values in the sources (508 rect and ear; 1570 of and occurr; 1173 when and would).

In this hand 5 and 6, 7 and 9, and 0, 6 and 8 are easily confused. Re-transcribing No. 88 from zoomed crops corrected seven groups over two passes (168, not 158; 1426, not 1425; 302, not 502; 792, not 492; 1061, not 1081; 588, not 888; 1384, not 1385), showed that two groups of the last line are digits Adams himself overwrote (1383 than and 1097 sho, each with its last digit written over another), and showed that the underlined 1102 at the foot of the left page of frame 0206 is a catchword repeating the first group of the next page, not a group of the text. The table, its sources and the conflicts are in the repository (targets/jqa/code_table.tsv, targets/jqa/consolidate.py, targets/jqa/code_conflicts.txt, targets/jqa/overrides.tsv, where every hand decision carries its reason).

03 No. 88, 25 June 1812: the nine lines

No. 88 was written at St Petersburg on 25 June 1812, the day after the Grande Armée crossed the Niemen, when that news had not yet reached the capital. Ford prints it at 4:355–58, addressed to James Monroe. After Adams’s account of Prince Kurakin’s passports and the French ambassador’s demand for his, the paragraph ends: “On the other side I have reported to you what Count Romanzoff said to me the day before his departure for Wilna. Certainly negotiation then was his wish and expectation. [Nine lines of this paragraph not decyphered.]” The microfilm has the triplicate (M35 reel 3, frame 0206), and on it the clerk’s pencil stops exactly where Ford’s gap begins.

The block runs from the last four decoded groups to the end of the paragraph: 133 groups, 129 of them in Ford’s gap, with the clear words scarcely (twice), undoubtedly and In the German Gazettes written in the line. The rebuilt table gives every group a value (python render.py gap88.txt), and all 133 give sense in the running text (python measure.py): 106 on clerk-attested values, 10 on Ford-fixed, 13 on uncertain and 4 on inferred ones; without the inferred values, 129 (0.970). In the decryption below, words in square brackets rest on grade-I values; groups in braces have a value that does not make sense in the sentence. The opening words in brackets are the clerk-decoded close of the sentence that Ford prints.

[Certainly negotiation then was his wish] and ex-pect-ation. But the [journ-ey] it-self of the Emperor Alex-an-der to Wil-na was in opposition to the Chan-ce-ll-or and adv-ice, and if his ac-tive in-flu-ence had not been im-pa-ir-ed before it, it can scarcely fa-il to have been af-fect-ed by his ill-ness im-medi-ate-ly after his ar-[riv]-al [thither], which was undoubtedly an a-po-ple-ct-ic str-oke. Since then he has had a second and a more sever-e one. In the German Gazettes even his de-a-[th] has been an-no-un-ced; but that was a mis-take. At all events it is scarcely pos-sible that he should rem-ain much long-er in the depart-ment of foreign affairs.

im-pa-ir-ed: the groups on the page are 1424, then the catchword 1102 at the foot of the left leaf, then 1112 1433 520 at the top of the right leaf. The catchword is the group Adams meant to begin the page with, 1102 pa (Ford-fixed nine times), and in No. 83 (frame 0190) he codes un-im-pa-ir-ed as 1477 1424 1102 1435 520 (1435 ir, Ford-fixed three times). The 1112 1433 on the new page are his own copying slips, one digit each, for 1102 1435: taken as he intended, impaired. Chan-ce-ll-or is 1385 1025 1016 1457 908 with the doubling mark under 1457, the same five groups Adams wrote for “the Chancellor” in No. 73 (frame 0165, Ford 4:256). The group before his illness is 792 by: the earlier transcription 492 use took an open hook for this hand’s looped 4.

Most of what the first pass of 18 September inferred is now attested: itself (1081 self, clerk 0184, 0185, 0190), advice (1575 adv, Ford 0132; 1405 ice, Ford 0036 and clerk 0190), apoplectic (1141 ple; 1060 ct, clerk 0182), stroke (1586 oke), second (1077), severe (1091 sever), remain much longer (743 rem, 162 much, 888 long), mistake (134 mis, clerk 0182), announced (479 un), At all events (283 event, clerk 0048), foreign affairs (68, 1517), and 1186 is attested as will and Wil- (clerk 0182, 0184). Opposition (83), the key word of the first sentence, is now grade C from No. 73. Five groups of the passage remain inferred: 1434 journ and 168 ey, 1479 riv, 1398 thither and 1382 th.

3.1 What it says

“He” is Count Nikolai Rumyantsev (Adams’s Romanzoff), Chancellor and minister for foreign affairs, who had gone to Wilna with Alexander. Adams reports that the Emperor’s journey to Wilna itself went against the Chancellor’s advice; that whatever influence he still had would in any case have been affected by his illness, an apoplectic stroke soon after his arrival there; that since then he has had a second and worse one; that the German gazettes had reported him dead, wrongly; and that he can hardly remain much longer at the head of foreign affairs.

This fits what is known: Rumyantsev had a stroke in 1812 that cost him his hearing, and he left office in 1814. Adams’s own diary for September 1812 records Romanzoff speaking of two strokes of apoplexy suffered with the Emperor at Wilna. It differs on the timing from standard accounts (Britannica, Wikipedia), which tie the stroke to the news of the crossing of the Niemen on 24 June. Adams, writing at St Petersburg on 25 June before that news could have reached him, places the first stroke soon after Rumyantsev reached Wilna and a second, worse one before the 25th. Ford printed none of this.

04 The rest of No. 88

Ford ends the despatch with “[remainder of letter with exception of few lines undecyphered.]”. That remainder is on frame 0207, left leaf: thirteen lines of code, then clear sentences (manifestos of both parties expected; peace with Turkey signed a third time; “It is not General Kutuzoff but Count Rostopchin who is appointed Governor, civil and Military, of Moscow”), then five more lines of code. It has 257 groups, 7 of them with digits lost in the binding gutter at the line ends; 249 have a value and 247 give sense (0.961): 195 clerk-attested, 20 Ford-fixed, 17 uncertain, 15 inferred; 232 (0.903) without the inferred values. Square brackets are grade I, braces a value that gives no sense, and angle brackets a gutter-cut group restored from the context only, which is counted as open:

The ambassador is Lauriston, the French ambassador at St Petersburg; Kurakin’s note is the Russian ambassador’s demand at Paris in April 1812 for the evacuation of Prussia, which Napoleon treated as a declaration of war. “Even after the battle of Friedland” (June 1807, before Tilsit): France had never proposed evacuating Prussia even when Russia was beaten. The last lines expect Napoleon, if the war opens well for him, to strike directly at the centre of the empire rather than along the Baltic shore. The groups are in targets/jqa/rem88.txt, the groups that give no sense, with the reason for each, in targets/jqa/sense88.tsv.

05 What remains open

Grades as in section 02: H clerk’s decode, C Ford-fixed, M uncertain, I inferred. No. 88 is decrypted; the groups below keep it from 100%. Measured on the words of the readings, the nine lines read at 98.0%, and the remainder builds coherent sentences but reads at 93.8%, under the 95% bar.

  • Seven gutter-cut groups (needs the original). 142x, 21x, 15xx, 11xx, 10xx, 55x, 13xx: the last group of seven lines of the remainder lost digits in the binding gutter on the microfilm of frame 0207. Context restores most of them (in-, pre-, sa-id, at-tem-p-t), but they are counted as open. The original and the duplicate of No. 88 are not on reel 3, and Adams’s letterbook copy at the Massachusetts Historical Society is not online; with either the letter would be at about 98%.
  • 1508, remainder l. 5. le/bable (M) where “as a pre[liminary]” wants a; no other occurrence in the decoded pairs.
  • 1185, remainder l. 9. “He [took?] it as a demonstration”: in no decoded frame or Ford block examined so far.
  • 570, remainder l. 11. Valued tol (C) but it gives no sense in “it tol he said”; the image shows 570, not 500 was.
  • Inferred values (I) that the reading rests on. Nine lines: 1434 journ, 168 ey, 1479 riv, 1398 thither, 1382 th. Remainder: 1864 tain, 143 mod, 350 fl, 1403 ib, 815 def, 239 sive, 108 comply, 821 dem, 549 min, 426 negotiate, 1309 bat, 432 net, 726 rate, 927 pire, 1540 along. Without them 362 of the 390 groups of No. 88 give sense (0.928).
  • The route to the open groups. 1185 and 1508 each need one more occurrence: the 1812 blocks of Nos. 77, 78 and 81 and the remaining lines of No. 85 (frame 0196, left, lines 12–23) are not yet aligned with Ford. Frames 0056–57 are a clear decyphered copy, and 0228–0233 No. 95 in code only.
  • Ford’s other gaps. No. 51 (26 May 1811, “[One line and a half of cypher not decyphered]”, frame 0085, transcribed and partly rendered) and No. 55 (22 June 1811, “[one-half line of cipher not deciphered]”), both on reel 3; and reel 2 frame 0155 (1810), a long coded passage with no decode on the NARA copy.
  • The Forbes-collection letter. Adams to Madison, 7 January 1811, for which the editors name the Armstrong code, has not been checked against its manuscript.

## Reading

If this proposition has in any form been disclosed to the Russian government, it was certainly neither unreasonable
nor [?immoderate] nor inconsistent with a system rigorously and [?inflexibly] defensive, to meet it with a proposition
for the evacuation of Prussia by the French troops as {1508} [?preliminary] to negotiation. But the very point upon

Alexander had determined not to [?negotiate] at all - it {tol} he [?said] - such a proposal as France had never
thought of making to Russia even after the [?battle] of Friedland. [...] An army of reserve is forming there, and it is
[...] probable that if great success on the, on the side of the French should mark the commencement of the war, the Emperor
