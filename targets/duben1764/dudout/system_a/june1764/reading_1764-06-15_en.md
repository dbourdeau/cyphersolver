## Where the cipher is
108R.01-15 + 109L.01-17 (the measured portion, 511 groups ≥ 100) cover the clear from "primo parceque" to 110L.03
"n'ont eu en vüe que". The rest ("de la tromper ... conscience" and the paragraph "Je pourrai bientot quitter Pettersbourg ...
un homme ruiné") is enciphered on **110 right**: three number lines at the top, and four number lines inside the clear
paragraph "Permettés moi, mon cher Comte ... et combleroit mes voeux si [cipher]". Decoded after the measure with key_c01
unchanged (our preview reading of 110R, one pass, not frozen): "de la [?]per ri te ... s doute tre[s] ... ai taire pour [l'ac]quit
de ma conscience" and "... malheureusement ... l'antre du lion ... d'où l'on ne sort ... tachés de [m']en tirer par [votre ad]resse
... [sans] cela je suis un [homme ruiné]". This places the end of the June letter (and probably its clear postscript) on 110R;
the Aug 1764 letter begins on 111R.

## English translation (of the contemporary clear, 109R-110L)
[Opening, in clear on 108R: "In reply to your dear letter of 11 May, I have the honour to tell you that you must be indulgent and
excuse me if I do not often send you news of myself,"]
first, because I do not wish to wake the Ministry's attention to our correspondence at the wrong moment, and secondly because
there is nothing of interest to send you from here in the present circumstances. Take it as a fact, my dear Count, that the
Empress means to change the King's situation and that she will apply herself to it as soon as the affairs of Poland are settled.
I am much mistaken if she is not concerting with the King of Prussia the lowering of the French party in Sweden; from what she
is doing in Poland one can judge what she will be able to do with us; judge then how important it is to handle her carefully.
She likes the King and seems very satisfied with his proposals, but I must not hide it: she is not pleased with Her Majesty the
Queen. Everything is known here, down to the smallest trifles; a word let fall without intent before people who ask nothing
better than to make bad use of it produces an effect that no negotiation can undo. They [the negotiations] will be altogether
useless if Count Osterman keeps reporting the opposite of what Düben says of them. So if Her Majesty the Queen believes she
needs the Empress, I do not doubt that she will hasten as far as possible to keep up that Princess's good dispositions. As for
me, I see no other resources at all that one could have elsewhere, being more certain than ever that there will be nothing to
gain on any other side, and that all those who have so far entertained Her Majesty with the finest hopes of the contrary had no
aim but to deceive her; it is ‹...› very disagreeable, which I shall keep silent for the discharge of my conscience.
I may soon leave Petersburg; it is unfortunately the lion's den, from which one hardly comes out. Try to get me out of it by
your skill, for without that I am a ruined man.
