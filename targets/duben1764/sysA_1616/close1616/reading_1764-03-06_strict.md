## English translation (annotated)

Translated from the decipherment of the time. The part with surviving cipher ends at "He repeated"; the rest is
translated from the clear alone. Notes in brackets are ours, not the document's.

My dear Count, — Two days before I received your letter of 7 February, Monsieur Panin [1] forestalled me on the
matter it concerned. He had sent for me expressly, and began by telling me that he had been sent a piece of news from
Stockholm which troubled him, and about which he wished to speak to me beforehand, knowing that it would not fail to
come to my knowledge. Monsieur d'Ogier [2], he said, is supposed to have written to Breteuil [3] that his friend
Moltke [4] had warned him of a concert and alliance between the Court of Denmark and Russia to prevent any change in
the form of your government: a false report, resting only on circumstances, which I shall set out for you. Some time
ago the Danish ministry, alarmed by the rumour then going round [5] of an extraordinary Diet [6] in Sweden, applied to
Monsieur de Korff [7] at Copenhagen to let him know that it was time Russia paid attention to the affairs of that
kingdom, which stood on the eve of a total revolution that ought to be forestalled, it being no less in the interest
of Russia than of Denmark to prevent the introduction of sovereignty [8] in Sweden. Upon this insinuation, orders are
said to have been given to Monsieur de Korff to reply that quite different notions of the affairs of Sweden were held
here, and that, since in Denmark they seemed to see them from another point of view, his Court would much like them
to open their minds to it in confidence; he, Korff, would then be able to give the necessary explanations on that
point. "There," added Monsieur de Panin, "is a perfectly innocent step, which may be given an unfavourable
interpretation unless the King [9] is truly informed of how the matter went." He repeated

[no cipher survives from here] on this occasion the strongest assurances of the Empress's [10] intention to act
together with the King in everything that might make his position more favourable and put our affairs on a solid and
lasting footing. I thanked him warmly for the confidence he had shown me, which I promised to make use of, adding,
however, that as the King had acted so frankly in communicating his true aims, which the Empress herself had found as
just as they were moderate, he hoped for his part that this Princess would act with the same sincerity and without
any evasion, which could only have the worst effect. Monsieur Panin protested that nothing was more real than the
Empress's intention to restore the King's authority and to remedy the defects of our constitution, which he admits to
be faulty in every way. Through these assurances, however, I made out some fear of a total revolution in our country,
an apprehension I tried to dispel [11] as far as I could, saying even that the Court [12] is losing more and more the
hope it had formed of winning over, little by little, some members of the Senate and of the French party, so as to
keep the ministry here in the belief that the King seeks and expects no support but that of Russia. With a little
patience we shall know where we stand; and I promise you not to let myself be put off for long with fine words. One
must take a side, and the one in which the King will find his greatest security must be preferred even to the
Empress's friendship, which becomes useless if this Princess is too slow in keeping her promises.

[1] Count Nikita Ivanovich Panin, who directed Russian foreign affairs. [2] Probably Jean-François Ogier, French
minister at Copenhagen. [3] Louis-Auguste Le Tonnelier, baron de Breteuil, French ambassador **at Stockholm** in 1764
(he had left St Petersburg in 1763; from standard reference works, not checked in a source here); this fits "une nouvelle de Stockholm": the report went from Copenhagen to
Stockholm and from there to Panin. The 14 Nov summary (`../reading_1764-03-06.md`) places him at St Petersburg; we
think that is a slip. [4] Probably Count Adam Gottlob Moltke, at the head of the Danish court. [5] The cipher has the
singular, "du bruit"; the decipherer wrote "des bruit". [6] An extraordinary session of the Riksdag. [7] Johann
Albrecht von Korff, Russian envoy at Copenhagen. [8] *Souveraineté*: royal absolutism, the end of the constitution of
the Age of Liberty. [9] Adolf Frederick of Sweden. [10] Catherine II. [11] "de la dissiper": "la" is overwritten in
the clear (103R.03) and left doubtful; it does not change the sense. [12] The Swedish court party, the King and Queen.
The identifications are ours, from the names as the clear writes them; none is in the document.
