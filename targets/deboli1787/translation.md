## Deboli to the King

### p01 (29 Dec 1786)
Deboli tells the King that the Vice-Chancellor's (Ostermann's) evasive answer about the Russian regiments stationed in Poland kept him from asking formally, for fear of an explicit refusal; he considers an approach through Potemkin and mentions abuses committed by the regiments. Last Wednesday he pressed the Vice-Chancellor, who said neither yes nor no, so the silence has to be read. Since the King's despatch of the 16th, he sees that Russia is not disposed to withdraw the regiments. At the next conference he will use the Department's despatch of the 9th and the argument that the Emperor, travelling to Kiev, will see Russian regiments in Poland with his own eyes. Partly read passages: Buchholtz (the Prussian envoy) spreading a "falsehood"; a visitor explaining his interests; a count's memorial on estates of Polish families in Ukraine. To the Department he writes only pro forma.

### p04 (9 Jan 1787)
Deboli expects an answer at tomorrow's conference on the King's letter of the 16th (Dec.). The aim is finally to be rid of the regiment in question (cf. piece 1). The Empress's departure remains as announced. By the last Vienna courier, Joseph II will come to Kherson rather than Kiev and cannot stay long because the Neapolitan sovereigns are expected in Vienna. Potemkin will go to Krichev (his estate) to receive the Empress and will wait for her at Kiev; otherwise he could not finish his tours. The affair of the Princess of Württemberg (Augusta of Brunswick) troubles the grand-ducal couple: there was talk of sending her to Reval during the Empress's absence pending an answer from her father the Duke of Brunswick, who reportedly wrote urging her to win her husband's affection and declines to take her side or recall her; since yesterday it is said the Empress will take her along. A general, taken seriously ill on Saturday, is better. Deboli received the King's letter of 7 December yesterday.

### p07 (18 Jan 1787)
Deboli reports a talk with Vice-Chancellor Ostermann about the Russian regiment stationed in Poland. Deboli presses for its withdrawal, as the Sejm instructed him. Ostermann answers that the Poles complain now of the Emperor, now of the King of Prussia, while also demanding the withdrawal. He adds that the regiment keeps the peace and that the Hetman himself complained of it. He blames Deboli's vehemence and tells him to put these points to the King. Deboli notes that he has waited for an answer for almost a year. For three years, he says, he has answered foreign envoys vaguely. The French minister, speaking to the Dutch one, assumed the King would raise the matter when he met the Empress.

In Deboli's view everything depends on Potemkin, who wants the affair sent to a Commission. Deboli fears such a Commission: the grievances (Igelström, a detachment entering a district in 1783) would be turned back against Poland. He suggests the King write to Potemkin before Kiev. He will not use the King's letter of the 3rd. He also mentions Potemkin's plan to buy an estate in Poland ("S mila", probably Smiła).

The letter then covers:
- the affair of the Palatine of Ruthenia, with Chernyshev, General Komarzewski and Razumovsky;
- a young castellan's son who came to St Petersburg via Riga with neither his father's leave nor a passport. He claimed to carry a letter from the Hetman to Potemkin. Deboli refuses to protect him, lends him 300 roubles against a bond and sends him home via Połock.

### p13, p15, p21
- **13 (27/7)** — Foreign ministers await an audience with the Empress. She received three of them at Tsarskoye Selo, in a small circle and in very good humour. Hetman Branicki and his wife are to come to St Petersburg this winter (news from Cobenzl). There are hopes he will regain the King's favour. Deboli answers that the King does not push him away.
- **15 (31/8)** — Three posts came with no letter. The answer to the King's letter will come from the Empress herself, since Ostermann's chancery has no orders. The British envoy (Fitzherbert) has left and was given the Empress's portrait. On Tuesday Ségur's tragedy "Coriolan" was staged at the Hermitage, with only Cobenzl and the author invited. Comedies and proverbs are played in the small theatre; the other ministers come on Thursdays. Ségur leaves within a week with the Prince de Ligne, after M. de Sainte-Croix's arrival. The letter is left unsigned as Deboli leaves for Pavlovsk.
- **21 (17/8)** — Deboli asks how the King's letter and its allegorical idea were received. The Empress came back to town, without saying whether she would return to Tsarskoye Selo. A ceremony was held at the Summer Palace for the Preobrazhensky regiment's feast. She then moved into the Winter Palace, to everyone's satisfaction.

### p23, p28, p30
- **23 (14/9)** — Courier to Warsaw; Cobenzl and the Austrian courier; the Turks have declared war; French mediation; Ostermann on France and Berlin; Sardinia; Prussian recruitment (2 × 500); the prince de Ligne wishes to write to the king.
- **28 (25/9)** — Back from Gatchina; the king's letter of the 12th; Cobenzl says the alliance binds the Emperor to war; Council debate on peace with the Turks; Ligne's letter sent with the Austrian courier; Ligne asks about an extraordinary sejm; Deboli jokes he will propose him as deputy for Wilno.
- **30 (3/8)** — Pisarz Wielhorski has arrived on business of the castellan's wife Ogińska of Wilno (probable), with the chancellor's letters to the vice-chancellor, Count Bezborodko and Prince Vyazemsky (probable); Deboli has introduced him. Starosta Potocki: 200,000 roubles would have been lost had they not been withdrawn, merchants having failed (partly unresolved); creditors will get barely 5 %. Potocki still loses through the exchange rate, down to 36 stuivers (probable) per rouble against 42 at the start of winter, on the 400,000 roubles he sent.

### p33, p37
- **33 (7 Oct.)** — At tomorrow's conference Deboli will carry out the king's order (dispatch of the 24th of last month): Field Marshal Rumyantsev's division has hastened its entry into Turkish territory to screen Poland; he will ask when to expect the answer on the alliance project with Russia ("in hand"); there is hesitation here about starting anything in Poland and fear of trouble from Prussia; Deboli answers that inaction would be taken as abandoning Poland and would help Prussian intrigues. Three conclusions: 1) no long war is wanted: take Ochakov (this autumn or rather in winter), then make peace; 2) according to Bezborodko the Emperor will do little; Herbert has not yet sent a courier because Joseph II wants to be ready and to concert with Russia; the attack will come when the Russian army enters Ochakov Tartary and Moldavia, the Emperor also attacking from Pokutia if Kamieniec is secured; the theatre of war will move to the Danube; 3) a Franco-Russian union is hard: England would join Prussia; the Danish minister (Saint-Saphorin, probable) foretells an English fleet in the Baltic, but has a second plan: Russia will humour both England and France. Ségur is almost the only one to speak of the prince of Nassau with respect; ministers here mock him. 200,000 roubles in gold and silver sent under escort to Rumyantsev to pay for provisions in Poland. Rumour of a 5-million loan (probable) at the Berlin bank, spread on purpose by Berlin, and false.
- **37 (16 Oct.), 444 part** — Written in the morning before leaving for Gatchina; the courier announced in the king's letter of the 3rd has not arrived. With the vice-chancellor away, a long talk with Markov on the dispatch: he is heard kindly and told Russia still wants to shield Poland from any damage in this war and to come to an agreement; but on guaranteeing a territory for Poland (asked in the king's letter) Markov protests and gives a "dissertation": in the last Turkish war circumstances forced Russia into the partition; if Poland makes difficulties Russia might again have to consent to its loss; the aim should be keeping, not enlarging, the country; Russia cannot promise unconquered land; anything would rather come from conquests over the Turks; above all Polish possessions must be preserved; spreading the plan would be most harmful. Deboli answers in two parts: 1) troubles in Poland will be prevented if Russia joins in the means of keeping Poland under the watchword of union; the nation, used to fear, would read Russian inaction as abandonment, and individuals would scatter "like lost men who fall into snares while seeking help"; 2) the idea of gains from the Turks is no sine qua non; he asks that it not be misconstrued; the proposal, known to few, is to come through Stackelberg and stay secret, as the vice-chancellor urged. He must now treat the matter with the vice-chancellor on these lines; Markov repeats that the disposition to an agreement remains and calls the use of the Polish army against the Turks a way to occupy minds and turn them from harmful ties. ## Gołuchowski to a minister of the King

### g1 (13 Feb 1787)
Gołuchowski has nothing to add on the Empress's journey beyond his report to the Department. He denies what was reported about Prince Repnin (a sum of roubles and a place in the suite): it is untrue. Buchholtz reportedly announced the coming entry of 10 Russian regiments, causing alarm in Warsaw; questioned by the Prussian minister in St Petersburg, Gołuchowski said what he knew and refrained from saying more, since the matter was not clear enough in the report of the Palatine of Ruthenia.

### g3 (30 Mar 1787)
On Sunday at court the foreign ministers (Danish, St-Saphorin; Portuguese, Horta?) pressed him about the 10 regiments said to be entering Poland; he answered he had nothing certain. The Danish minister then asked about Şahin Giray: Hamburg papers said he would be sent to Persia and later return to the Crimea, where the Empress is to go. Gołuchowski said he knew no more than the Hamburg papers and ended the conversation to avoid further questions.

### g4 (18 May 1787)
Charged (by Prince Stanisław Poniatowski's household) with finding out Grand Duke Paul's taste in saddle horses to be sent from Poland, Gołuchowski questioned the riding master who had been at Tsarskoye Selo. The horse must be: 1) very calm; 2) unafraid of firing, attacks, arms drill, soldiers' shouts and cannon — not even moving its head when the Grand Duke, as he does, rides up to the guns; 3) lively when needed and well schooled; 4) sound and strong-legged, since the Grand Duke rides his chosen horse every day: he sets off at a gallop to a fixed point, then walks, out twice a day, sometimes up to 40 versts. The Grand Duke complained he had no good horse to sit before his regiment's front without fear and wants a Polish horse for the May review (Old Style). Gołuchowski objects that horses sent on 15 May will only arrive on 26 June; and he doubts that one horse can have all these qualities (the rest of the reading is in reading/g4.md, in Polish).
