# AGAD 1/338/0/1/131: English translation

From `reading.md`, with the same holes. The first two paragraphs and the paragraph on Bouwen translate clear Polish. The rest translates the cipher French.

Sir, my especial Benefactor,

Everywhere I carry in my obliged heart a lifelong gratitude to Your Honour. I do not fail to present my humblest submission to Your Honour.

As to my affair, I do not want to trouble Your Honour with long reading. Time also runs short. I inform His Honour the Starost of Brańsk and ask him to share my letter and its contents with Your Honour.

The Castellan of Brzesc and Count Pociej, Strażnik of Lithuania, are very obliged to His Excellency [?Branicki: 185], Grand General of the Crown.

I told them that His Excellency declares that he will give his protection and obtain the subsidy, the [190], for the coming Tribunal.

They said that France gave a subsidy. The Prince Grand General of Lithuania also gave a thousand ducats. The Strażnik of Lithuania, the Castellan of Brzesc and his brother [?borrowed] thirty thousand francs from Mr Białłozor, Wojski of [?Pinsk]. This was at the start of the Tribunal. With what they borrowed from the [?others], the money reached two hundred thousand francs. [The French says “lent” for the first sum. The later borrowing passage supports the interpretation here.]

[?For this reason] they beg His Excellency to have a large subsidy granted to them through his protection. I ask you very humbly to propose this skilfully, so that I do not fall into disgrace.

The Castellan of Brzesc begs His Excellency to recommend his nephew's memorial to the Count de L'Hôpital, ambassador of France. His nephew is Mr Ciechanowiecki, Starost of Opsa, a lieutenant of His Excellency. The memorial is of the following tenor.

Mr Strutyński, Starost of Szakinów, is known in Lithuania for his disorderly actions. The text singles out the affair with Mr Ciechanowiecki, brother of the Starost of Mścisław. It refers to his lawsuit and to his full brother, Mr Ciechanowiecki, Starost of Opsa, a colonel of His Excellency the Grand General. [The source leaves the connection incomplete.]

This Mr Strutyński gathered all sorts of his men. He came, arms in hand, to the dietine of Brasław with the intention of massacring Mr Ciechanowiecki, Starost of Opsa. And [?through/of] this family he carried out this [?massacre] on the said Mr Ciechanowiecki, brother of the Starost of Mścisław. [The family connective remains unclear. The French has “par de” and “mattare”.]

They carried the dead man's body to Wilno. They presented it in law before the Grand Tribunal of Lithuania last year. His family brought suit against Mr Strutyński, Starost of Szakinów. They then tried to convict him of the crime committed.

Mr Strutyński, Starost of Szakinów, had another lawsuit over boundaries. It was with Mr [?Szkultyn], a gentleman of Samogitia. Strutyński secretly commissioned someone to kill him.

Because of the [?outcries], the said Mr Strutyński withdrew [?to the army] of Russia. He did not trust the courts. He sought the protection of His Excellency Count Apraksin. The count was Field Marshal of the armies of Her Majesty the Russian Empress. [The French “cries” may mean outcries or conceal “crimes”. The word before “de Russie” has an unread sign.]

The Tribunal of Lithuania had already begun proceedings against the said offender. Count Apraksin prevented them from continuing. He even used his own authority to oppose this gentleman's conviction. He granted him protection under the pretext [?of having entered] the service of the Russian army. [The French “de trentre” does not fully spell the proposed phrase.]

They say he committed a black deed at St Petersburg about 1730. The Palatine of Bełz saved him from losing his life.

They also say that Mr Strutyński at [?Danzig: 275] tried to betray King Stanisław.

They say that the court of the Grand Marshal of the Crown has issued decrees against [?all] his disorders.

Mr de [?Bruwen], secretary to His Excellency [?the envoy], my Benefactor, excused himself for having no time to write to Your Honour by this post. When I asked whether he would write if he had time, he went to His Excellency in the same room. He spoke quietly. I therefore do not know whether he wrote to Your Honour by this post. He is a plain man,

and he is often [?under] Count Apraksin. [The French says “sous”. It may conceal “soûl” (drunk), with a new sentence about Apraksin. The secretary's name may be Bruwen, Buwen or Bouwen. His title may mean envoy or prince.]

[?He] already announces the governorships of Keynixbergue and the other lands of Prussia. He publicly [?assigns] this province's palatinate to Lithuanian lords. He [?gave an answer] to the Castellan of Witebsk at Kowno that he should preserve the tables for the memory of future ages. The council of war took place on those tables. [Two proposed words and the subject remain uncertain. Pinsk probably means Pińsk. Keynixbergue probably means Königsberg. Both identifications remain tentative.]

I give myself to your unchanging favour. I am, for my whole life, Your Honour's, my especial Benefactor's, obliged servant, known by my hand. [The closing honorific has an uncertain extra M.]

11 July 1757, from Wilno.
