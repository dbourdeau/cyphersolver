# T-S NS J479 (PGP 8804): dating evidence from formulas, vocabulary and texts

Research pass, 20 Nov 2026. Web sources, the Princeton Geniza Project (PGP) full-text search, the Arabic Papyrology
Database (APD) and OpenITI text files of Mamluk and Ottoman-period Egyptian authors. Counts from OpenITI are from
edited texts. Editors sometimes normalise month names, so they show tendencies and are not exact usage.
Strength scale: **strong** / **moderate** / **weak** / **none** (no dating value).

## 0. What the two published datings say (checked)

- PGP 8804: "no earlier than mid-13th century, based on handwriting and formularies used, plus the citation of a
  poem by al-Būṣīrī (1213-95)". The PGP record also gives the formula as "jarā dhālika fī ākhir jumādā al-thāmī(!)
  sanat ar[...]" and notes the word כסתבאן. Its only bibliography item is a Goitein index card (#12204).
  https://geniza.princeton.edu/en/documents/8804/
- Alan Elbaum, "Q&A Wednesday: secret codes and erotic prose", Cambridge Genizah blog, 22 Jun 2022. The quote is
  confirmed in the archived copy (web.archive.org, April 2023, of https://www.lib.cam.ac.uk/node/1355111): "It's
  Mamluk or perhaps Ottoman era." Elbaum says the Burda verses help "to date the fragment somewhat". No other
  argument is given. The live URL now redirects to the library home page.
- This project's year check (`tr_year.md`): the formula continues to סנת אר and stops. "ar-" can only begin arbaʿ
  (a year ending in 4) or arbaʿīn (a round x40), because Arabic gives the units first. That leaves dozens of
  possible years in any range, so it does not narrow the date.

## 1. "جمادى الثاني" (masculine) for Jumādā al-ākhira

**Classical norm and how grammarians described the other forms.** al-Qalqashandī, *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* (written 814/1412),
II, 403-404 (OpenITI `0821Qalqashandi.SubhAcsha`):
- He quotes Ibn Makkī (*Tathqīf al-lisān*) that one should not say *jumādā al-awwal* in the masculine.
- He quotes al-Naḥḥās: the Arabs said *rabīʿ al-ākhir* and *jumādā al-ākhira*, not *rabīʿ al-thānī* or *jumādā
  al-thāniya*, because "second" implies a third.
- He then notes that "most usage of the people of the West [the Maghrib] is *rabīʿ al-thānī* and *jumādā
  al-thāniya*".

So in Mamluk Cairo, the *thānī/thāniya* forms were seen as Maghribi or incorrect.

**Usage counted in Egyptian chronicles (OpenITI; diacritics stripped):**

| Text | author's death | ākhir/ākhira | thāniya (fem.) | **thānī (masc.)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| al-Qalqashandī, *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* | 821/1418 | 32 | 2 (in the grammar passage only) | 0 |
| al-Maqrīzī, *al-Sulūk* | 845/1442 | 433 | 0 | 0 |
| Ibn Taghrībirdī, *al-Nujūm al-zāhira* | 874/1470 | 547 | 0 | 0 |
| Ibn Taghrībirdī, *Ḥawādith al-duhūr* | 874/1470 | 12 | 0 | 0 |
| al-Sakhāwī, *al-Ḍawʾ al-lāmiʿ* | 902/1497 | 306 | **286** | 0 |
| Ibn Iyās, *Badāʾiʿ al-zuhūr* (vol. 1, poor OCR) | c. 930/1524 | 38 | 0 | 0 |
| al-Jabartī, *ʿAjāʾib al-āthār* | 1237/1822 | 26 | **61** | **1** (II, 49) |

**Documents.**
- PGP (all Geniza documents with a transcribed Jumādā, about 50 Arabic-script and 50 Hebrew-script hits, Fatimid
  to 1821): every second-Jumādā date uses ākhir/ākhira/ukhrā (אלאכר, אלאכרה ...). No hit has thānī or thāniya.
  Masculine *jumādā al-awwal* occurs from 1123 (ENA 2131.7) and AIU XII.69 (1198) onward.
- APD (all hits for جمادى/جمادي/جماد, 7th to 16th c.): ākhir/ākhira/ukhrā throughout. That includes the Mamluk
  Ḥaram documents of 1372-1395, the Sinai deeds of 1461-1515, and P.RichardsKaraite 1517.
- There is **one masculine جمادى الثاني**: P.BaudenAchat 2a. This is the sale of a Christian captive, Alexandria,
  15 Jumādā II 818 / 22 Aug 1415 (APD; F. Bauden, *Mélanges de l'Université Saint-Joseph* 58 (2005), pp. 305-307:
  "خامس عشر جمادى الثّاني عام ثمانية عشر وثماني مائة").
  - The notary is Muḥammad al-Rīghī, of an Alexandrian qāḍī family descended from the Banū Rīgha, a Maghribi
    (Maghrāwa) tribe. The deed also uses Maghribi *ʿām* for "year". So the masculine form here goes with the
    Maghribi usage Qalqashandī describes.

**Reading.**
- The masculine *jumādā al-thānī* does not appear in Fatimid, Ayyubid or Mamluk-Cairo chancery or court Arabic in
  any sample checked.
- The feminine *al-thāniya* enters Cairene writing in the later 15th century (al-Sakhāwī) and dominates by the 18th
  century (al-Jabartī). The masculine form appears once in Mamluk Alexandria (1415, Maghribi notary) and once in
  al-Jabartī.
- J479's form looks like a colloquial analogy with *rabīʿ al-thānī*. It fits a writer for whom "al-thānī/al-thāniya"
  was the everyday name of the month.
- **Strength: weak to moderate.** It points to the 15th century or later rather than the 13th-14th. Limits: the
  samples are small, the chronicle texts are edited, and the masculine form is rare in every period, so the
  argument rests on absence. Ottoman sijills of Egypt were not searched; the one Ottoman Cairo ḥujja in PGP
  (NLI Ms. Ar. 276.20, 1641) has جمادي الآخرة.

**"جرى ذلك".** al-Asyūṭī, *Jawāhir al-ʿuqūd* (Mamluk notarial manual, 9th/15th c.), II, 297-298 (OpenITI):
- The qāḍī writes "جرى ذلك" or "جرى الأمر كذلك" in the place of the ʿalāma, then the date.
- PGP's Mamluk court record T-S Ar.38.131 + Ar.42.212 (870/1466) opens with الحمد لله وحده جرى ذلك.
- Hebrew-script גרי דלך in 11th-century deeds is the ordinary verb ("if this happened"), not a formula.
- So "jarā dhālika fī ..." is court language of Mamluk (and probably later) Egypt. Its use in Ottoman sijills was
  not checked. **Strength: weak** (it gives a floor in the Mamluk period and no ceiling).

## 2. The petition opening "يقبل الارض و[...] المواقف العاليه اعلاها الله تعالى وشرفها"

- **The taqbīl formula alone has little dating value.** PGP has 57 Geniza documents with يقبل الارض.
  - Most are Fatimid (e.g. T-S Ar.30.273, 1123-27; T-S AS 150.195, 1130-49), a few are Ayyubid, one is probably
    Mamluk.
  - ENA 3754.11 is late 16th / early 17th century.
  - Khan (BSOAS 1990, "The historical development of the structure of medieval Arabic petitions") traces the
    formula to the Fatimid period (article not read in full).
- **"al-mawāqif" as the addressee is Fatimid and Mamluk.** T-S Ar.38.138 (Fatimid, 1101-30): "المملوك يقبل الارض
  امام المواقف الشريفة المعظمة". Qalqashandī uses *al-mawāqif al-sharīfa* (15 times) for the sultan's presence.
  - At VI, 193-194 Qalqashandī gives the petition (qiṣṣa) model: "المملوك فلان يقبل الأرض وينهي كذا". He adds that
    *al-mamlūk* may be replaced by *al-faqīr ilā Allāh taʿālā*, in which case *yabtahil ilā Allāh* stands instead
    of *yuqabbil al-arḍ*.
  - *aʿlāhā Allāh taʿālā* is Mamluk chancery language (Qalqashandī VI, 253: "الإشارة العالية ... أعلاها الله
    تعالى").
- **"al-mawāqif al-ʿāliya" is the late form.** It occurs 0 times in Qalqashandī, al-Maqrīzī, Ibn Taghrībirdī,
  al-Sakhāwī and al-Jabartī.
  - In the Geniza it occurs in exactly two petitions. T-S Ar.41.14 (PGP 21062, dated only "Late"): "يقبل الارض
    لدى المواقف العالية وينهي".
  - **ENA 3754.11** (PGP 12757, c. 1600, dated by its mention of R. Ḥayyim Kapusi, 1540-1631; ed. Umrethwala and
    Elbaum 2022): "يقبل الارض بين يدي الموافق [sic] العالية اعلاها الله تعالى". This is the closest verbatim
    parallel to J479's "المواقف العاليه اعلاها الله تعالى", and it too is a petition by a Jew to the Muslim
    authorities.
- **Signature "al-faqīr Isḥāq al-yahūdī" / "anā al-faqīr".** This fits the petitioner's self-designation that
  Qalqashandī describes (VI, 194). It is not datable on its own.
- **Strength: moderate.** The exact pairing *al-mawāqif al-ʿāliya* + *aʿlāhā Allāh taʿālā* is matched only in a
  c. 1600 document and an undated "late" one. The Mamluk formulary uses *al-sharīfa*. This points to late Mamluk or
  Ottoman Egypt, but the sample is two documents.

## 3. Colloquial vocabulary

- **bitāʿ (בתאע, possessive): no dating value after c. 1200.**
  - PGP shows it already in T-S 8J8.5, a court notebook dated 25 Iyyar 1528 Sel. / 4 May 1217, "אלעהדה בתאע
    אלגאריה" ("the warranty of the slave-girl").
  - Also BL OR 10589.17 (early 13th c.), T-S 10J13.10 (13th c.), and the Arabic-script JCC document of 825/1422
    ("الكنيسة بتاعهم").
  - It is common in 17th-19th c. Geniza documents (Yevr.-Arab. II 1433, 1690; AIU VII.E letters 1797-1807, etc.).
  - **Strength: none** beyond a floor of c. 1200.
- **كستبان / כסתבאן (kustubān, "thimble").**
  - A Persian loanword (J. D. Latham, "Keshtebān: a Persian loan-word in Arabic", JRAS 1968, 65-67; not read).
  - Dozy, *Supplément*, s.v. كستبان, gives variants كشتبان، كستوان، انكشتوانة and cites the *Vocabulista* (13th c.,
    al-Andalus) and Bocthor. So the word is medieval.
  - The only PGP hit is J479 itself. **Strength: none.**
- **juwwa (גואה "inside"), yā khayy, yā khillī.** These are Egyptian colloquial, but no dated first attestation was
  found. Ibn Dāniyāl (d. c. 1310) and Ibn Sūdūn (d. 1464) were not checked for these words. **Strength: none.**
- Overall, the colloquial layer shows Egyptian speech and a low register. It does not separate the Mamluk and
  Ottoman periods.

## 4. The Burda

- al-Būṣīrī was born in 608/1213 and died c. 694-696/1294-97. The Burda has no fixed date; it is a late work, and
  the "1250" floor used by PGP is generous.
- The earliest dated copies found are Egyptian, from the 1360s. A Mamluk copy by Riḍwān b. Muḥammad al-Tabrīzī,
  c. 767/1365, carries al-Fayyūmī's takhmīs (Khalili Collections record; a sale catalogue misprints the date as
  "694/1366"). Other copies are dated 798/1395-96 (Christie's, 2011), 846/1442-43 and 853/1449 (sale records).
- Sale catalogues call it very popular in Mamluk Egypt and Syria in the 14th and 15th centuries.
- **No other Geniza copy or quotation of the Burda is in PGP.** A full-text search for تذكر جيران / תדכר גיראן /
  Burda returns only J479. No study of Jewish knowledge of the Burda was found.
- A Jewish youth quoting the first two verses from memory, with phonetic spellings, suggests the poem was common
  oral property. That is more plausible from the later 14th century onward than in al-Būṣīrī's lifetime.
- **Strength:** strong as a floor (after c. 1260-90); weak as a pointer to the 14th century or later.

## 5. Published work on J479 and similar cipher sheets

- On J479 itself, nothing beyond PGP 8804 and Elbaum's 2022 blog post was found. The PGP record is signed "ASE"
  (Elbaum) and its scholarship tab lists only Goitein's index card.
- In the blog, Elbaum says Gideon Bohak has written on a Genizah cipher and Oded Zinger has found a letter partly in
  cipher. He also mentions Amir Ashur on the India Book merchants' Coptic-numeral code and Arianna D'Ottone Rambach
  on an encrypted Qur'an manuscript.
- PGP 26158 (T-S NS 70.1, a Judaeo-Arabic letter in cipher, with an Arabic prescription on the recto) says "cracked
  by Gideon Bohak in 2025", to be published by Bohak, Zinger and Elbaum. It is undated in PGP and is the obvious
  comparison once published.
- No dated Geniza cipher practice sheet was found to compare with J479.

## 6. Orthography (dots, ض/ظ, tanwīn)

From this project's reading (`NOTES.md`):
- ض and ظ are both written with dotted ט (אצם for iḍam, ואומט for awmaḍa, אלטלמא for al-ẓalmāʾ).
- ذ is written with a dotted ד and خ with a dotted כ (the notes say "under").
- tanwīn is written נ and the rhyme vowel is written out.

How the stages of Judaeo-Arabic spelling compare (S. Haffner, "Stages of Judeo-Arabic orthography", WorldPhilology,
LMU; E.-M. Wagner):
- Early Judaeo-Arabic (papyri, before c. 900-1000) spells phonetically, and ד can stand for ض/ظ.
- Classical Judaeo-Arabic imitates Arabic spelling: ض as dotted צ, ظ as dotted ט, dots placed above.
- Late Judaeo-Arabic (from the 15th century) returns to phonetic spelling and shows vernacular influence. In the
  Egyptian Late Judaeo-Arabic folk tale AIU VII.C.16 (Connolly), ظ is written with dotted צ, the reverse of J479.

What J479's spelling suggests:
- J479 marks the classical distinctions with dots, but merges ض/ظ under ט and spells tanwīn phonetically. That is a
  mixed spelling.
- In a verse quotation, tanwīn-as-nūn and the written rhyme vowel follow the scansion (metre), not the period.
- The ض/ظ merger is found in Middle Arabic of every period.

**Strength: weak.** At most a slight lean to post-classical usage. It does not fix a century.

## Conclusion

**Supported range: c. 1400-1650 CE (c. 800-1060 AH), with the 15th to 16th centuries most likely.**
**Firm outer limits: after c. 1290 (the Burda) and before the Geniza's later layers.**

What each line of evidence gives:
- **Floor (strong): the Burda.** After c. 1260-1290, and more plausibly after c. 1350, once the poem was in wide
  circulation.
- **Start of the range (moderate): the petition formula.** *al-mawāqif al-ʿāliya aʿlāhā Allāh taʿālā* is matched
  verbatim only in ENA 3754.11 (c. 1600), and the Mamluk formulary uses *al-mawāqif al-sharīfa*. This favours the
  late Mamluk or Ottoman period.
- **Start of the range (weak to moderate): the month name.** *jumādā al-thānī* is absent from Fatimid, Ayyubid and
  Mamluk-Cairo usage in every sample checked. The *thānī/thāniya* forms appear in Cairo from the later 15th century
  and dominate by the 18th.
- **End of the range: soft.** It rests on the one c. 1600 parallel and on the hand, not on a clear terminus. Nothing
  checked excludes the 17th-18th centuries.
- **No dating value:** the colloquial words (bitāʿ attested from 1217, kustubān medieval), the "jarā dhālika"
  formula, and the orthography.

**Weakest link.** The upper bound, and the move away from the 13th-14th centuries, rest on two small negative
samples: Mamluk documents and chronicles without *al-mawāqif al-ʿāliya* or *jumādā al-thānī*. Both features could
turn up in an unpublished 14th-century document, and the chronicle counts come from edited texts.

**What would narrow it further:**
1. Palaeography. Compare the Arabic-script petition lines and the cipher hand with dated Mamluk and Ottoman Geniza
   hands; the Ottoman-period Hebrew cursive of the PGP late corpus is distinctive.
2. The paper. Look for watermarks (European watermarked paper is rare in Egypt before c. 1400 and usual by the 16th
   century), chain lines and laid lines. This needs the original or a transmitted-light photograph from CUL.
3. A year. The formula breaks off at סנת אר, so none survives on the leaf (`tr_year.md`). A join, or a sibling leaf
   by the same Isḥāq al-yahūdī, could supply one; a PGP search for the signature in Arabic script is worth doing.
4. Ottoman sijill samples from Cairo (16th-17th c.). Check how often جمادى الثاني and المواقف العالية occur there;
   this would test the upper bound.
5. The forthcoming Bohak, Zinger and Elbaum edition of T-S NS 70.1, if that cipher or hand is related.
