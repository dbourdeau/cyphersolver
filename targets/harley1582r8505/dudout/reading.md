## English translation

264r. … they nonetheless have an order and a form of conduct, though more secret: there is no corner so small that there is not some capable and notable gentleman, to whom business is addressed and who in need would be followed and obeyed by the others. … a council already set up … who report …

In this matter the causes of distrust still last: the edict is not carried out in a single article; of all the breaches one complains in vain; every day one sees disloyalty practised against those of the religion; preparations are already being made in Switzerland and Germany; munitions are being gathered at Lyon to come down the Rhône all at once; troops are being sent into Gascony. So the causes of distrust last and multiply, and the reason for keeping [the towns] remains whole:

to put off the surrender of the said towns, if one could be sure of a good army of reiters able to hold the field and to carry the war away from the centre of our provinces to the circumference of France … to put off handing them over; and when our enemies … if he sees no clearer about help from outside than he has so far, he will resolve … since he cannot keep it by stronger and surer [means] … if the means are refused him by those for whose preservation he works hardly less than for his own, and who are hardly less concerned in his than he is.

264v. … by keeping up and stirring up among them [troubles] that turn their minds and means elsewhere … and [Monsieur] will espouse the inclinations and factions of the Pope, who will henceforth make him his right arm and the executor of his most [pernicious] designs. … It is to make himself the head or the chief instrument of the ruin of those of the religion, since he cannot otherwise disguise the Antwerp affair … [without the victory] of Monsieur … they were going to negotiate it with the Prince of Parma, to hand back to him the towns he held …

… where no doubt the [king his] brother will fear that he may raise against him a party of malcontents … some design in England and Scotland … an Italian who serves as an engineer at Lisbon, whose letters have come into my hands … to join him to that house, the enemy of Christendom and particularly of the state of England; which, given the usual practices of those of Guise in Scotland, … must remedy.

… head of the war against those of the religion … of the King his brother … the command of an army devoted to him, which has [made him hope] that the King would allow it … the mere marriage of … remains a prince who … bring an army of reiters out of there to throw and spread it over the country of the one who …

… as long as the party of those of the religion stands firm, [codes 99, 81, 36] will have little means of harming him; and yet she has an interest in not letting them be lost.

As for France … in truth she has treated them neither according to her rank nor to theirs; she has abandoned them in their need, left them, as far as lay in her, to be mocked and preyed on by their enemies, has even treated them shamefully in their persons and those of their people — [which is free and honourable] towards all others, and not of such a quality as they. [So they can] no longer expect anything from her friendship, which they have sought so long in vain, and they do not know whether it pleases her that they offer her their service.

[They say the Queen has done wonders… But she may remember that since the year 1570] she has not spent a penny on them … in return for certain … [As for the money lent in 1576, it is for Monseigneur, who had it, to answer for it …] which might well have been as usefully employed elsewhere. Still, the …

[To return to the two points proposed:] namely the means that [the King of Navarre must] take to strengthen himself with alliances, and to divert the efforts of his enemies without putting himself to much inconvenience.

## Notes

- Date (hypothesis). “Ce faict d’Anuers” (264v) very probably means the “French Fury”, Francis of Anjou’s failed coup on Antwerp on 17 January 1583; the text also speaks of Monsieur as alive and unmarried (before June 1584), of Épernon and Joyeuse, and of an Italian engineer at Lisbon (after 1581). Hence c. 1583, rather than the 1580 of the catalogue title. The setting may be Ségur-Pardaillan’s missions to England and Germany (1583–1584); this is not verified.
- “The Queen”. The reproaches of 264v (“she has not spent a penny on them” since 1570; “money lent in 1576”) probably aim at Elizabeth I, whose 1576 loan paid for John Casimir’s army. That is precisely the part the author put in cipher. A reading hypothesis, not checked in the English papers.
- The code numbers (6, 21, 36, 51, 57, 72, 81, 99, 721) are not read. 81 = the King (Henry III), 21 = Monsieur, 51 = the King of Navarre have been suggested, but these values are not established.
- What the re-reading changed. The working transcription confused neighbouring signs: closed o and open cup (n / i), plain and barred loop (n / u), lunate ε and two-bowled ε (a / s), barred ε and delta (t / a), X and hourglass (l / p). It also filed several different signs under one name (a lying ⊣ = c, a ⅃ = c, a rounded U = h, a mere lead-in stroke). On 264v a line given to the foot of the page was in fact the rising end of the next line (“d’aliances”): it is counted once. An isolated dot attached to the ʃ (“qu’ele”, “esté”, “tenoit”, “traiter”) is read as a mark, not as m: “tenoit” and “traiter” count as probable; for “qu’ele” and “esté” the blind re-reading (next note) confirmed the mark.
- Targeted blind re-reading. Twelve doubtful signs of 264v were shown to a reader (Brice Dudout) with no letter or value: he chose the shape he saw among certain shapes taken elsewhere in the letter. The effect of each answer had been fixed and hashed (SHA-256) before the answers, then applied as it stood. Six words move from probable to certain (“qu’ele”, “tant”, “esté”, “ofrir”, “seruice”, “denier”); two stay probable but read “laissee” and “traitee” (the last sign is e, not s: an unusual agreement); “recherchee”, “font” and “despende” stay probable (no clear choice); the first sign of ‹cissuada› is confirmed as c. No word became unresolved.
- Still unresolved: 31 words, including whole stretches in 3.10–3.12 (after “de Monsieur de”), 3.29–3.30 and 3.43–3.44 (‹dencor›, ‹baones›). ‹cissuada› would read “dissuada” if its first sign were d; it is c in the key, and a restoration never overrides a certain sign.
