## Contents

Ferrante has reported his intention to reach terms with Ottavio Farnese over the enterprise of Parma. Charles forbids that plan, gives his reasons, and leaves Ferrante a narrow, conditional authority. "Capitular" here means to conclude terms, not to surrender, and the capitulation in question is Ferrante's, not an offer by Ottavio.

- Ottavio is worth little: no means or money, in disgrace with the Pope, and likely to settle with his grandfather in the end.
- Backing him would mean declaring for the Emperor and open war with the Church, since Paul III holds Parma in the Church's name. It would also seem to rank Ottavio's right above the Empire's and weaken the imperial claim.
- If the Pope dies the quarrel passes to his successor, and the imperial side would owe both the Church and Ottavio.
- It could push Paul to give Parma to Orazio and to bring in the French.
- A private settlement between Ferrante and Ottavio is approved. Capitulation with Ottavio over Parma is forbidden: "no deueys en ninguna manera entrar por el presente en capitulacion".
- Case 1: Camillo Orsini plays a trick in a vacancy or in the Pope's lifetime, and Parma is evidently out of the Church's hands. Ferrante may do as he thinks best, provided the action is justified and has a safe way out.
- Case 2: by a deal between Pope and King of [?France], or otherwise, Orazio or another enters Parma in the name of someone other than [?the Church] or Ottavio. Ferrante is to think how to prevent it and to try to make himself master of the city, with dexterity and dissimulation.
- Otherwise, the letter ends, nothing is to be started against Parma while it is in the Pope's hands: "no se [?inoue] ni emprenda ninguna cosa contra ella sino en los dos casos arriba dichos".
- c. 62r: the soldiers kept over the winter are to be held for next summer, for use as now against Dragut, or for whatever may then arise. The clear text around it gives only pay and billets.

## Historical context

Pier Luigi Farnese was murdered at Piacenza in September 1547, and Ferrante, governor of Milan, took the city for the Emperor. Paul III kept Parma for the Church. Ottavio, married to Charles's daughter Margaret, tried to take it in October 1549 against the papal governor, Camillo Orsini. Paul III died on 10 November 1549. These dates and roles are general knowledge; Treccani, Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani, "Gonzaga, Ferrante", gives Gonzaga's part and his proposal of a Siena exchange in November 1549, and earlier instructions for a surprise of Parma "dopo la morte di Paolo III". So the idea of seizing Parma is not new.

This letter, dated four days after the Pope's death, still treats it as a future case ("muriendo el Papa", "en vida del Papa"). The news had not arrived. The anchor is in the next letter: the clear text of c. 64r (20 Nov) says Ferrante's letters of the 11th came "antenoche" with Mendoza's, on the papal election, so the news reached Brussels about 18 Nov. That is ordinary courier lag, not a finding.

Other sources that fit: Calendar of State Papers, Venice, V, 9 Nov 1549 (Ottavio asked help of Don Ferrante, who promised it); Calendar of State Papers, Spain, IX, Renard, 1 Nov 1549 (the Pope was to hand Parma to Orazio). Clear c. 60r answers Ferrante's letters of 25 Oct and 3 Nov and says the designs of the earlier letter have ceased, which fits the failed October attempt.

## What the clear text hides

The clear text of c. 60r thanks Ferrante for his zeal, and c. 62r deals with pay and quarters. The Parma policy is all in the cipher: the prohibition, the low opinion of Ottavio, and the conditional authority in two defined cases. Whether this policy is known from other copies is open: see Prior art in `NOTES.md`.
