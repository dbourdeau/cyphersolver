**f. 139r.** The [galleys] of the Venetians are detained in England, and this has made them very [uneasy]. Even those
who [govern] say openly that, because of it, they are in no state to act for France. The French party in Venice says
it cannot reach an understanding with His Majesty, because it has no one to trust. [The Pope?] (`hod`) was to have
been the mediator and the guarantor of the agreement between the two sides, but how could they trust him when those of
the French party draw everything to their own ends? The members of the [Council] are very [cautious] and dare not speak
out. Sánchez's view is that the [galleys] held in England, together with the other pressures, will bring the Venetians
round more than their own will would. The French ambassador recently went to the Signoria to complain that they were
speaking ill of his king. He also protested that his king was so wise and so [powerful] that ...

**f. 139v.** The Venetians answered that they could not [stop] what people said. The ambassador went on: Christian
princes were turning against the French, and the French would bring in the Turk to ruin Christendom. If anyone said
such a thing, [the king] would have him hanged. Sánchez recalls what he had written about the quartering of His
Majesty's army in [...]; Don Juan [Manuel] wrote him the same. The Venetians say they had a good answer from [`mif`]
about it. Sánchez writes to the Duke of [Sessa] on matters touching His Majesty. Geronimo Adorno sent a man [`sub`]
here, and Geronimo Centurion came with a letter of credence for Sánchez.

**f. 140r.** The French hold places in Picardy. It would do less harm to the French if the places of that frontier
were strong and provided. [Geronimo Adorno's plan]: with [about ... fifty] thousand ducats, xv thousand infantry and
other troops, Prospero [Colonna] and the Marquis of Pescara could carry the enterprise against the King of France
easily, with some fleet, since the places there are neither strong nor provided. That way the army could be kept up,
and [one of the King's treasurers?] would be needed to raise the money. Geronimo [Adorno] had written all this to His
Majesty and was waiting for the answer. He had sent [Centurion] here to [discuss] it with the English ambassador.
Sánchez answered Geronimo Centurion that Adorno seemed to him very wise and very good.

**f. 140v.** They were to wait for His Majesty's answer before speaking to the ambassador, and Geronimo Adorno had
[agreed] this with Don Juan Manuel. Sánchez then proposed to the English ambassador that the king of England could send
these 150,000 ducats to Adorno. The ambassador liked the idea and said he would write about it in two ways. One was to
say that the money should be sent to Adorno. The other was to propose [new ...] to his king. In England, though, this
might be taken badly, because some there wish Adorno ill. If His Majesty approves, Sánchez thinks it should be said on
His Majesty's behalf that the money be sent to Adorno. The ambassador agreed and will write so.

**f. 141r.** [The Baron of ...] is here as French ambassador. Another French envoy came four days ago from Ferrara, about
the [absolution] and sentence given by [the Pope?]. The Signoria has letters from France, but they say nothing of
[moment], except that His Majesty's men and those of [`hod`] have [risen?]. News of the Turk: still good. Rhodes is in
danger ("pero el turco ha hovido muy gran danyo", in clear). Three days ago [...] of the plague. The clear date is
"en venecia a xxij de otubre de dxxij".
