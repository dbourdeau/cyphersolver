In a postscript to my last, of the 20th, I told Your Lordships that the Most Christian King, in assigning the garrisons, had ordered Monsieur de Bourbon sent into Burgundy with 8 hundred lances and 10 thousand Landsknechte; and that if the Swiss did not stir from home, it would have been no wonder if, with the expense unexpectedly on his back and the fortresses at risk, a passage were made [?to your side] of the mountains. From the words of the Most Christian King, with whom I have just spoken at length, one sees that this has vexed him and broken some plan. What he will do now he does not seem to have fully settled, because on one side fear of the Swiss presses him, should they come into Burgundy, and on the other his anger against [?26] and [?47], of whom [?he spoke to me] just now very angrily, saying: "They excuse themselves by saying [?they were] forced by fear of the English army; and I being their sovereign lord, they had no regard for putting my kingdom in danger, and it was no thanks to them that I did not lose it."

He told me they had sent a man [?expressly] to Monsignore to [...] excuse themselves and to draw out something of the king's mind; and that Monsignore had answered them that no one could know the mind of the Most Christian King better than they themselves, who knew whether they had wronged him or not; that they should examine their consciences, and if they found they had not [?offended] him, they could hope for good; but if they found they had wronged him, they should expect punishment.

Nevertheless regard for the truce with Spain and the winter season could damp the anger and turn minds to resting this winter; and this noise and show may also be made to ensure that in future no prince gives shelter to their enemies.

The troops assigned to go into garrison have not gone yet, and will not move until England has left, which cannot be long in coming.
