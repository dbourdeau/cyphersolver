# English translation (Brice Dudout, with Claude Opus 5.5, 4 Nov 2026)

Translated from `reading.md`. Same marks: {word} = probable, [word] = restored (group lost in the binding or an evident
missing syllable), [...] = unresolved. The opening "Monseigneur" and the closing formula and signature are in clear on the
manuscripts. "The minister" is Nicolas-René Berryer, secretary of state for the Navy and the colonies.

## 1391 — Montréal, 13 August 1760 (ANOM COL C11A 105, ff. 147-152)

My Lord,
In separate letters I have dealt with what has happened in the various parts of the colony since the last accounts I had the
honour to give you. I have kept for this cipher letter particulars of which it would be dangerous for the enemy to learn at this
critical moment.
You will easily have judged, my Lord, the danger run by the three ships, the stores and the troops at Restigouche under the orders
of M. Danjac. {We} must indeed fear everything from the extreme superiority of the naval forces [which] hold that post and [...]
the ships blockaded. M. Danjac will, I think, do his best to follow to the letter the orders I have given according to the needs
of each case, but his zeal may [give way] under the perseverance and obstinacy of the enemy, who will want at any price to seize
these ships, provisions and munitions. Time will tell us what [will come of it].
The only hope left me, and which I wish still more [...], [is] that M. Danjac, having received my despatches for you, my Lord, [and
the] urgent orders I gave him on that occasion, will have decided to send off the frigate [the] Machault; that Sieur Lagiraudays
[will have] seized a favourable moment to slip past the enemy ships by night {and} will have escaped the frigates that went in
pursuit of him. The duplicates of these same despatches will reach you, my Lord, by the schooner l'Extravagante, which has been in
the Baie des Chaleurs since last autumn.
M. de Villemont, to whom I had granted leave to carry the first copies to you, was detached by M. Danjac to report the situation
to me. This officer, who is full of zeal, will finish {his} campaign here, and I am quite sure he will seize every occasion to
distinguish himself.
We are without a navy or anything like one. The English are masters of our waters; they can go wherever they like on the river.
Their fleet has now passed {Lake} Saint-François [the group for "se le" is written twice]; the passage of the [...] at the
outlet of that lake cannot be obstructed; we have absolutely nothing in the colony for that. The King's flute la Marie and our
feluccas [...] on this side of Saint-François [...]; so the English [will be able] to go by water to whatever place General
Amherst has appointed for their junction.
The manoeuvre I ordered M. D[umas] to make {has held back} this fleet; it has [a] ship opposite it [...] this officer; and now that
it is [de]cided that it does not want [...] [T]rois-Rivières, I have left M. de Longueuil in that town with a modest garrison.
However, twenty-two English [ships] have passed [...] Cartier; it is said that [...] others carrying the land[ing force] and the
rest of the troops of [Lou]is[bourg]; they are said to be already at the [...] [...]. Everything shows me that the English are
bent on making a diversion of [...], however weak they may be; [their] main object being to [...] towards the heart of the
[colony], I am drawing M. [...] closer; I keep my forces [u]nited and ready to seize the favourable moment to give battle.
The English have begun their landing on the north shore; they behave there as on the south: they make {seductive} speeches to the
Canadians; they make a marked show of paying them for the refreshments they need in salt, brandy and other essential articles of
which the colony is totally bereft; they give them everything cheaper than they ever had it in {time} of peace; they gently seize
the arms they have at home; they keep telling them that they alone can help them in their misery; but they warn them at the same
time that the houses of the farms whose men are absent will be burnt. That is an evil, my Lord, which I cannot remedy; the
English, masters of the waters, are always able to [forestall] our detachments, if they [crush] our small forces [piece]meal;
all the rest would cost them nothing more.
Had I had provisions, it would have been very easy for me to gather all the Canadians of the government of Quebec; their zeal for
the King's service would have made them scorn the risks and the certainty they would have had of losing their farms.
Our frontier on Lake Champlain is far from [...]; although it is our best post, the small navy we have there [can]not only not
compare with the enemy's, but [we] even lack the bare necessities [to] repair it.
I am in no less unfortunate a situation on Lake Ontario: the English navy dominates there; our two corvettes can neither face nor
resist their two frigates; one of our corvettes has even run aground and been damaged. We have a third on the stocks which might
have enabled us to dispute the mastery of the lake, but we have not one of the articles needed to finish it and make it fit to
sail.
That, my Lord, is a very exact summary of our sad position.
Hunger presses us; the Canadians have their harvest to bring in, but they cannot be taken away from [the] defence of the colony:
it is about to be attacked hard on every side. The English have about forty [thousand] men; what can I [set] against that?
Battalions of which the fullest is not half [...] of what it should be, militia and Indian nations. These last [notice or] tire of
our extreme want; nothing [remains to them] but my care and their attachment to [us]. The smallest of the enemy's armies will be
infinitely superior [to] all our forces united, far from our having a single [man] to spare beyond the number of our
fight[ers]; most [...] militiamen and Indians do not [understand] the design. We have only ten [thou]sand pounds of powder in the
inte[ri]or of {the} colony; it will very soon be used up if we have to use our artillery at all.
We are no better off for provisions, although the Canadians hasten to supply all they have. General Amherst, who knows our extreme
distress, seizes the urgent moment of our harvests to make his mightiest efforts.
The circular letter that M. Bigot had written jointly with me to the militia captains about the delay in paying the bills of
exchange would have had all the effect we wished; but you will see, my Lord, from the enclosed copy of General Murray's circular
letter to most of these captains, that he left nothing unsaid to discr[edit] these [bill]s of exchange and our treasury notes
entirely. To overcome all [these] obstacles the contractor has paid in his bills of exchange on France for the produce of the
inhabitants who had the le[ast] [...] doubt about the [King's] money; to put them more at ease, [I] had bills of exchange offered
to them on my own salary; the Intendant and the Chevalier de Lévis have followed my example with {success} [...].
Such are the critical, more than critical, circumstances to which the colony is reduced. I shall make every effort my zeal [for
the] King's service can suggest to me to preserve it. I beg you, my Lord, to be [good enough to] assure His Majesty that if [I]
succumb, it will be only in [the] last extremity. The Chevalier de L[évis] is animated by the same zeal [...] me; I should have the
greatest hopes from our close union and perfect understanding if we were less overwhelmed.
Finally, if the English complete the conquest of the colony, they will owe it only to the exhaustion of our forces and of our last
resources; for if I had only the provisions and munitions that are in danger of being taken at Restigouche, that, together with
the precautions I would take besides, would put me in a position to secure a retreat where I would hold out with the troops when
it was no longer possible for me to stop the enemy's progress.
I am with very deep respect, my Lord, your very humble and very obedient servant. Vaudreuil [in clear]
