## No. 21. Ompteda to Münster, Berlin, 24 July 1811

I received Your Excellency's much esteemed letter of 27 March at Dresden only on 22 June, by a safe opportunity through
Count Hardenberg. Fortunately Count Hardenberg, out of caution and uncertain whether I still had a cipher, had the
despatches deciphered beforehand, since otherwise I should have been unable to learn their contents: I have no French
cipher, only the German cipher of 1790, which also underlies the despatch Your Excellency let me have last year, and
which I must use now as well.

Having made the necessary arrangements to remove anything surprising from my journey to Berlin, I left Dresden on the
5th of July. On arriving in Berlin I at once learnt that the Duke of Mecklenburg-Strelitz was on a visit to His Majesty
the King of Prussia at Potsdam. I hurried there at once to discharge the commission given me towards him, about which my
most obedient postscript gives the details.

This occasion seemed to me extremely welcome for conveying, through the Duke, whom the King of Prussia treats with great
respect, directly to His Majesty, and wholly in line with the intention of His Royal Highness the Prince Regent, the views
that form the subject of Your Excellency's despatch of 27 March. The commission I had to carry out with the Duke gave me
the most fitting opportunity. The Duke listened to me with the greatest attention and replied: "But speak about it with the
State Chancellor von Hardenberg." I answered that this was just what I wished, since several reasons kept me from opening
myself to the minister Count von Goltz. The Duke agreed with me completely, but referred me again to Herr von Hardenberg
and promised to speak to the King in the meantime. Next morning about ten I went to Sanssouci (the OCR reads "Sandfouei"),
to pay my respects to His Serene Highness there. I met the Duke just as he was about to step into his carriage to drive
with the King to Charlottenburg. As several persons were present, I approached the Duke, and under the pretence of
embracing me His Highness whispered in my ear: "I owe you many obligations; I have spoken with the King." As the Duke only
returned from Charlottenburg in the evening and meant to go back to Strelitz next morning, I had no opportunity to speak
to him further.

After my return from Potsdam I did not fail to call on the State Chancellor von Hardenberg, and two days later I was
invited to dine with him. I found an opportunity to let him know that I wished to speak with him in confidence, and the
readiness with which he accepted this proposal made me suppose that he was already prepared for it. He quickly arranged a
secret meeting with me at a country house near Potsdam, to which Baron von Hardenberg is accustomed to go every Saturday
evening for rest, and where he believed we could be the less observed (note: the estate Glienicke, now the property of
Prince Karl of Prussia).

This meeting could only have taken place on the 21st of this month. It was, however, again frustrated by an unforeseen
obstacle, and I received from Baron von Hardenberg another written invitation to dinner here in Berlin, asking me to come
an hour before the other guests. But even at this meeting he took every possible precaution so that no one could notice a
special conversation between him and me.

In the meantime I had still had occasion to sound out the minister Count von Goltz from a distance, but soon found myself
confirmed in the conviction that it would be not only useless but also imprudent to open oneself to him.

I began the conference with Baron von Hardenberg by telling him that the trust I place in his personal sentiments moved me
to make known to him, without circumlocution and openly, the views of the Prince Regent and the wishes of His Royal
Highness, which aim for the most part only at the security and independence of the larger states of the Continent. His Royal
Highness wished to be convinced that in some measures taken by the governments of the mainland against England they had
only yielded to the pressure of circumstances, but that it was impossible that the sovereigns, or their cabinets, could give
ear to the insidious representations spread by the French government about and against the English government. The
strongest proof His Royal Highness could give of this was that he still showed himself willing to enter into explanations
with the governments of the mainland about their true interest. That Bonaparte's lust for power knows no bounds, and that
all yielding to his system of oppression has been fruitless, needs no proof. But His Royal Highness wished that the only
still considerable powers of the Continent, Austria, Russia and Prussia, should be most vividly penetrated by the danger that
hangs over their heads, and that they should seize the one means of rescue still in their power: a sincere, if secret,
understanding about their common interest, among themselves as well as with the British government, of which, however, there
is unfortunately as yet no trace. At the same time I explained to Baron von Hardenberg in what sense His Royal Highness
believed such an understanding should be initiated.

Baron von Hardenberg at once entered into these matters with me with the greatest personal confidence, and did full justice
to the views and wishes of His Royal Highness, but expressed great anxiety about the means of communication I might have to
make use of his confidential statements.

After I had given him the most reassuring assurances about my caution, he spoke to me in full confidence as follows: "Be
firmly assured that neither I nor the King are friends of the French, and that we cannot be. Napoleon does not trust us, he
hates us, he oppresses us; forced by our unfortunate situation, with exhausted forces and surrounded by foreign troops that
allow us no free movement, we must feign a friendship that is not in the heart. We must keep playing this painful part with
the greatest consistency, so as not to compromise ourselves with France needlessly. Isolated we can undertake nothing without
plunging ourselves wholly into ruin, and an understanding with the other courts is quite impossible to obtain. With the weak
character of the Emperor of Russia nothing can be counted on with certainty from that side. We have even taken pains to urge
Russia, for the present, to keep quiet, not to provoke France, and to await the moment when better forces have been gathered
to begin a war with more weight. We ourselves are still far from ready. If we are given two more years, I may hope with
confidence that we shall play a more considerable part. But as war, given the state of things, may nevertheless break out from
one moment to the next, we have above all had to look for a closer understanding with Austria. We stand with Austria on an
equal line; both of us are met in France with the same distrust and the same ill will; we are oppressed in the same way, so we
have the same interest and secretly the same sentiments. I have therefore" (Baron von Hardenberg made this admission to me with
great anxiety and caution) "I have therefore turned directly and personally to Count Metternich, to open a secret understanding
with him. I put the above reasons to him to convince him of the necessity of a common course of conduct for both powers. I even
left the choice of that course to Count Metternich, and declared that Prussia would conform to any system Austria might propose,
if only the two powers could act together. To this I have received from Count Metternich the most obliging and friendly replies,
but this minister has always declined any definite closer concert, because Austria has the weightiest reasons to keep up good
relations with France for now, because the shattered state of Austria's finances does not at present allow her to undertake
anything considerable (which, however, Baron von Hardenberg would not wholly concede), and because for the moment a near outbreak
of war in the North is not to be feared. Otherwise he would be very ready to maintain an exact communication with me about the
common interest."

The State Chancellor recommended this communication to me as the greatest secret, but of his own accord allowed me to give Count
Hardenberg at Vienna notice of it, if I had a wholly safe opportunity, under the indispensable condition that Count Hardenberg
should never let Count Metternich notice that he knew anything of this secret communication, since that would be the way to lose
altogether the confidence of Count Metternich, already so anxious.

One had to let matters rest with this attempt, the more so as, by all the news arriving from Petersburg as well as from Paris,
it did not appear for the time being that the peace in the North would be broken.

I then remarked to Baron von Hardenberg that neither the protests coming in from both sides nor any arrangements about a greater
freedom of trade between Russia and England, or about the affair of the Duke of Oldenburg, can give a sure guarantee for the
preservation of peace so long as the warlike preparations are not seriously stopped on both sides, so long as the troops on both
sides are not withdrawn, and so long as peaceful sentiments are not shown by deeds on both sides. Rather, the continuing increases
and preparations, among the French as among the Confederation troops, clearly show that Bonaparte may well have postponed the
outbreak of hostilities, but certainly has not given up the war itself.

Here too Baron von Hardenberg agreed with me completely and said to me at last: "I give you my word that we shall never fight
alongside the French; we shall never help forge the fetters they mean to put on us. We would rather fall with honour; never shall
we make common cause with the French. More I cannot tell you now."

Baron von Hardenberg said this to me with a kind of enthusiasm, but at the same time adjured me most solemnly to regard this
disclosure as the highest proof of personal confidence, and by imprudence to compromise neither him, nor the King, nor the country.

After thanking the State Chancellor for this proof of his trust, and giving the most binding assurances of my caution, I asked
him, since to avoid all attention I would return to Dresden, to remain in a secret communication with me. He then promised that as
soon as matters began to turn more serious he would at once give me a hint, since he himself had much at heart to put himself then
at once in communication with the English government, and this way, as the most secret, would then be the dearest to him.

Finally Baron von Hardenberg added: "Of all that I have told you here in confidence, Count Goltz and the foreign department know
nothing." I replied that I had just as little thought I had to open myself in any way to Count Goltz, since in my opinion it was
best to let the Minister of Foreign Affairs ignore such secret negotiations, so as not to embarrass him towards other embassies
with which he must live in confidence. Herr von Hardenberg seemed to like this view very much, though he could not hide his distrust
of Count Goltz.

Although the subject itself gave cause enough to consider the pressing need for the still existing governments to hold together,
any further pressing for a closer understanding to be established already now would have been fruitless and would only have
frightened him off. It also seemed to me that the intention of His Royal Highness had for the moment been achieved at least in
part, namely by the assurance obtained from Baron von Hardenberg that Prussia will courageously resist total French subjugation.

Your Excellency will now want to know my opinion whether that assurance can be trusted, and I feel bound to say that I have no
cause to put a well-founded distrust in Baron von Hardenberg's statements; rather, many circumstances come together that give those
secret intentions more credibility and that can almost only be explained from this one point of view.

To what extent these intentions are to be carried out, perhaps no definite plan has yet been drawn up, as much depends on time and
circumstances that cannot well be determined long in advance. But many measures taken openly and secretly betray to the attentive
observer the means that are being employed in preparation to carry out that aim.

In an earlier, fuller report of the month of March I indicated to Your Excellency the various means that State Chancellor von
Hardenberg has long been using to gather the forces of the state more and more for unforeseen cases. In these measures they go on
with great strictness and perseverance, even to the great burden of the country and the great hardship of the subjects, who do not
see through the intentions underlying them. Care has been taken unceasingly to train a larger number of men in arms, most of whom
are dismissed again to their homes, but are always ready and able to take up the musket again. Every regiment has a quantity of such
supernumerary men, whose true number is carefully hidden. New troop detachments are formed under all kinds of names, which must be
regarded as the stem of future regiments. In the active army there is a disproportionate number of cavalry and artillery against
the infantry, since the former cannot be formed so quickly. Above all, for some time the chief endeavour seems to have been, by
detaching troops and guns to Pomerania and to the coast in an unsuspicious way, to concentrate the army more in those parts and so
withdraw it from the danger of being disarmed by the French armies, as the Hessian troops once were. The garrison here is growing
ever weaker, so that a large part of the duty must be done by the citizens' guard. From the garrisons here and at Potsdam frequent
night manoeuvres are carried out, which recall only too vividly the measures by which Major Schill sought to hide his departure
from the garrison here. The regiments of these garrisons also keep a supply of live cartridges ready at all times. Several officers
have been dismissed under various pretexts to observe the condition and movements of the states of the Confederation of the Rhine,
chiefly in a military respect, and attention is directed above all to neighbouring Saxony, where the Saxon troops stand in a
concentrated and threatening position opposite the Prussian frontier.

All these and several other circumstances, whose detail would lead me too far here, certainly suggest that the secret intention of
the King of Prussia and of the minister who has his whole confidence is, at the outbreak of a war, by a bold and unexpected
resolution, to withdraw at least the army and the fortresses from subjection to France; for it is not conceivable that so great and
so secret an activity, with such great exertions and sacrifices, would be employed if it were merely a matter of making common cause
with France, forced against one's inclination and against one's recognised interest. There must therefore be a secret plan underlying
all those measures, on which Baron von Hardenberg's statements to me give the most natural explanation, so that the credibility of the
latter is in turn supported by those measures.

I must further remark that the Russian embassy here, as far as I could perceive by careful observation, seems wholly unacquainted with
the secret intentions of Baron von Hardenberg, giving a quite different interpretation to several of the measures mentioned above, and
seeming, at least outwardly, convinced that Prussia will have to yield to outside pressure. It would not surprise me, however, if Count
Rumyantsev's public agents were not in Baron von Hardenberg's confidence, and unfortunately I did not meet Herr von Alopeus (note: the
elder brother, now a private man in Berlin), who really has the State Chancellor's confidence, since he is at Carlsbad. The Russian
embassy also assures me that it has from its court absolutely no commission to negotiate with the court here in relation to the present
political relations with France.

Lastly Baron von Hardenberg asked me about the following circumstance: it had officially been reported to the court here that the
so-called Crown Prince of Sweden had secretly made overtures to the British government to enter a closer connection with it, but that
these proposals had been rejected on the British side. Baron von Hardenberg thought it not advisable to push this man wholly away, as
he has in his hands the most irrefutable proofs that Bernadotte is Bonaparte's worst enemy. I replied that this whole matter was wholly
unknown to me, and that I could not even presume to form a judgment on it. For the rest I must remark that during the whole conversation
Baron von Hardenberg showed the greatest esteem and the greatest confidence in the wisdom and firmness with which His Royal Highness the
Prince Regent steers the helm of state.

As nothing more can be accomplished here for the moment, I shall return to Dresden as soon as I have found an opportunity to have this
despatch leave from here, which I shall if possible send by a double route. I shall next try to arrange a meeting with Count Hardenberg of
Vienna in one of the Bohemian spas, to inform him of the result of my journey here, since we lack a cipher for correspondence, and he
himself wishes this meeting.

As I have definite reason to suppose that a secret communication, tolerated even by the Prussian government, exists between Admiral
Saumarez in the Baltic and the Prussian postmaster Dümoulin at Kolberg, Your Excellency might perhaps be able to send me your further
orders by that road. At State Chancellor Baron von Hardenberg's, in the house of the Prince of Orange, namely with Colonel Robert Fagel, and
also at the Austrian embassy here, my address is always known, and in each of these houses they will undertake the forwarding of letters to me.

In my circumstances, so greatly restricted by the times, since my income is reduced almost wholly to the pension I receive by the grace of
His Royal Majesty and on which I live with my family, I hope, through Your Excellency's kindness, to obtain the grant of my former daily
allowances for the time of my stay in Berlin, which is attended with all sorts of extraordinary costs. In the meantime, given the fear now
prevailing among merchants and bankers, I have so far not been able to find the possibility of drawing in the previous way, and have therefore
had to seek until now to help myself by other expedients.

## No. 22. Ompteda to Münster, Berlin, 24 July 1811. P.S.

According to my most obedient report, my journey to Potsdam had chiefly the aim of discharging, towards His Serene Highness the Duke of
Mecklenburg-Strelitz, the commission Your Excellency had given me for him in the name of His Royal Highness the Prince Regent. I sought a
fitting occasion for it, for which His Highness himself chose the house of his son Prince Carl, who serves in the Guards at Potsdam. The Duke
received the communications made to him with the liveliest emotion and the feelings of warmest thanks. His first and most eager wish was to
express his heartfelt obligation to His Royal Highness himself in writing. But the difficulty of sending such a letter, and the misgiving that
by some accident its contents might be betrayed and the so benevolent intention of His Royal Highness the Prince Regent thereby frustrated,
kept the Duke from following the wishes of his heart, and I received from His Highness the commission to be, towards His Royal Highness, the
interpreter of his sentiments, full of gratitude. His Highness seemed willing, if required, to accept the offer so generously made to him, and
was embarrassed only about the means of carrying it out, on which he asked my advice. I thought I might remark that, so long as Kolberg alone
remained in the hands of the King of Prussia, it seemed to me the most convenient place from which to put oneself in communication with the
commander of the English fleet in the Baltic, and from there to reach the fleet. Meanwhile the circumstances of the moment, which cannot be
foreseen, would have to determine the closer measures.

I do not think I say too much if I add the remark that the communications I made to the Duke contributed a great deal to cheer His Highness at a
very painful moment, since they were just about to go, in the company of His Majesty the King of Prussia, from Potsdam to Charlottenburg, to
see for the first time there the mausoleum of the late Queen.

A later letter of Prince Carl to me also shows how deeply the ducal family was moved by the kind offer of His Royal Highness the Prince Regent.

As a safe opportunity offers itself, which does not allow me to finish this report, I shall send the continuation of it by another opportunity,
as well as the duplicate of the whole report by yet another way.
