Heading in clear on the copy: The Minister of External Relations to Citizen Létombe, Consul at Philadelphia, acting
provisionally as Consul General of the French Republic in the United States.

The Consuls at New York and Boston, Citizen, have sent me the speech made by <<Mr>> Adams at the opening of the
extraordinary [session]. I await with much impatience your own despatches, which will no doubt bring me the decision
of the federal government. Despite the tone of Mr Adams's speech, and that of the draft address of the House of
Representatives, I hope that the President of the United States does not seriously mean to go to extremes. As for
the Directory's sentiments, they are set out in my despatch of 29 prairial, and I have [nothing] to add to it. The
instructions that Citizen Adet left you before his departure being exactly in line with that despatch, I can only
approve the language you will have held to the American government on the strength of those instructions.

The circumstances are serious enough, Citizen, that you will [have] neglected nothing to let me know as quickly as
possible, in detail, [about] everything that will have happened in Congress: I count on your zeal in this.

Citizen Hauterive, formerly Consul of the Republic at New York, whom Citizen Adet authorised to act in concert with you,
and whose talents are known, will have been of great [use] to you in keeping up [useful] [relations] with our friends
in the House of Representatives without compromising them.

The despatches of Citizens Rozier and [Mozard] contain political details that it is dangerous to entrust to the
hazards of the sea and often to the bad faith of the [bearers]. Those despatches, written in clear, would compromise
us gravely if they were intercepted. What has already happened must serve us as a [lesson]. I [ask] you to make them,
and the other Consuls, feel that their correspondence [should], as far as possible, be confined to [consular]
matters, matters of political economy, of the sciences and arts, and others of that kind which cannot give rise to
any dispute between government and government.

When opportunities [for sending] to France arise in their districts, they [should] inform the agents in charge of
political affairs of them, so that [they] [can] make use of them. This course [would be] in keeping with prudence and
with the good of the service in cases where they have to send, on internal politics, information [that] must be sent
without [delay]: they must at least [have] recourse to the cipher.

You will see from my correspondence with you that I have opened a series of despatches for what does not concern the
matters you have to deal with as Consul [General]. I suppose you will have taken care [to do] the same for everything
that concerns politics.

Citizen Ade<<t arrived in Paris a fortnight ago: he>> has had his audience of the Executive Directory, on the tenth of
this month.
