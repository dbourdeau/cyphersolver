# English rendering: Vaudreuil to the minister, Montréal, 8 Apr 1759 (ANOM COL C11A 104 ff.54-66)

In the letters I had the honour of writing to you in cipher last autumn I tried to give you as exact an account as I could of the sad situation to which this Colony was reduced. What has followed has only
too well proved that it was even more critical than I had had the honour of announcing to you: from day to day its extreme want of everything and the most dreadful misery of the settlers have grown; soon there will be no more hope of
finding resources: the breed of horned cattle, of domestic animals and of horses is almost entirely extinct; there remains, Monseigneur, to several of the better-off inhabitants only the animals of a single plough; the greater
part of the various inhabitants have none at all. These animals have served to make up for the bread cut from the troops; the people have only four ounces of bread a day, with no other matter to sustain themselves.
The English are not unaware of it; they hasten to seize so fine an opportunity to invade this Colony. Foodstuffs, munitions of war and every kind of thing are as plentiful with them as we are short of them; the attacks
with which they threaten us appear most certain. They are no longer in the same forces they employed in the previous campaigns: they are doing more than doubling them; they expect some resistance on our part; they make no secret of it; nor do they make
a secret that nothing will stand against their obstinacy and their determination.

I have not lost time in setting all the forces of this Colony in motion.

I have had a very exact census made of the three governments. That of Québec will furnish me six thousand three hundred and fifty-six men; that of Montréal five thousand four hundred and fifty-five; that of Trois-Rivières one thousand
three hundred and thirteen. Even so, nothing will be left but the infirm and those too old to serve; and from the number of militia fit for war one must also deduct the men indispensably needed for the transports and the various needs of the service.

We have besides the eight battalions of the land troops and the forty companies of the colonial troops, which are not complete.

I must, Monseigneur, provide 1st for the defence of our [?] of Carillon, which will probably be attacked by twenty-five to thirty thousand men. You saw, Monseigneur, from my letters of last autumn that my activity in
relieving that quarter had obliged the English to give up the attack; that it had even hastened their retreat. The Marquis de Montcalm would have made it extremely difficult for them, but I wrote to him in vain, the troops of the marine, the Canadians and the
Indians told him the wish they had to go in pursuit of the enemy, nothing was able to make him yield to my views.

2nd, for the defence of the Belle Rivière against an army of about ten thousand men. I had foreseen the fate of Fort Duquesne; M. de Ligneris, having been obliged to reduce his troop in proportion to the subsistence,
took the wisest course by evacuating, blowing up that fort at the approach of six to seven thousand English, and withdrawing to the post at Machault; he holds there; I have the forces and various reliefs that are near him to support him, but the thing looks very difficult.

3rd, for the defence of Niagara, which is to be besieged by ten thousand men. You have seen that this post and those of the Belle Rivière must help one another and attach themselves above all to the defence of Niagara
as the principal and most essential key of the upper country.

By this reprisal I match the one of our enemies who want to join their army from the Ohio to the one they are raising to send to Niagara. M. Pouchot, captain in the Béarn regiment, is going to take over the command;
I have given him my instructions for every case; he will attend particularly to opposing the enemy's landing.

4th, for the defence of La Présentation, to keep our communication on Lake Ontario and to set bounds to the very ambitious designs of our enemies, who announce ten thousand men for this quarter and who intend by this way to
penetrate as far as Montréal with an even stronger army.

This winter I have had two corvettes built, of ten twelve-pounders each, which will serve for the transports to Niagara and manoeuvre according to circumstances to harass the enemy. I have besides set up a flying camp
to watch the English and to be informed of their movements [and of their] men on that lake; they want to re-establish Oswego and will employ ten thousand men there.

All things considered, I see the same plan reborn which was conceived by the Duke of Cumberland: General Amherst, who was his aide-de-camp, doubtless means to repair General Braddock's mistake.

Québec is also threatened by forces incomparably superior to all those of this Colony; the fortifications of this place need many repairs and even outworks; for all these works four thousand men would be needed, where to find them and how to feed them? Québec, this position,
I have confined myself to having the materials got ready during the winter that it was possible to collect without causing any extraordinary consumption of provisions; M. de Pontleroy will stay at Québec to follow the most pressing works
as our situation allows.

The [?] [?] of this capital greatly occupies the persons who do not enter into my views; from this follow reasonings and remarks that insinuate themselves and gradually become public; but the matters I have in view
have at this moment the most pressing claim.

Indeed, Monseigneur, it is quite certain that our frontiers and the posts I mean to defend will be attacked; it is even to be feared that most of them will succumb to the too great superiority of the enemy; but it is
my policy to do my best to keep them: abandoning them would sound badly in every respect. 1st, all our Indians, seeing no more French, would find themselves obliged to join the English; 2nd, these, no longer occupied
far away, would no longer be subjected to hard and painful marches, to finding expedients to ease and speed the transport of the [?] [?] [?] [?] of their army; they could unite and penetrate quickly as far as they pleased; 3rd, finally, if we had
peace, they would find themselves at little cost in possession of the posts we should have evacuated, and consequently of the immense extent of country that depends on them.

It is not the same with Québec; I greatly fear that the English have a wish to measure themselves against it and to conquer it; but 1st, it is not at all certain that they have to their liking everything they need for so important an expedition; 2nd, I [?] naturally believe myself assured, the King having been informed by you, Monseigneur, both of the insufficiency
of our forces and of that of our means, that His Majesty, knowing of such an armament, will have been pleased to provide against this undertaking; 3rd, supposing that I were in the case of defending Québec
with the weak forces and small means that, at present, our enemies could not arrive there without my being informed long before by the lookouts I have set as far as the mouth of the river; now whatever diligence a fleet may make that sails with weight and measure, it cannot reach Québec before the outcome of the battle the English must give us at Carillon is decided; whatever may be, Monseigneur, the event of that battle I shall always keep the liberty of going myself to Québec with the greater part of our troops, all the militia and the Indian nations that I can assemble; Québec, [and] the three governments, I shall leave at Carillon only what is indispensable and the armed xebecs; being at Québec I shall give the enemy battle and repeat it until I have forced him to withdraw or until, overwhelmed by his excessive superiority, he has entirely reduced me; my obstinacy in opposing the landing will be all the more to the point as I should not have enough; for powder, bombs, cannonballs [sign ?] to sustain the siege for six days. If I have the success I desire, I shall at once march all these [forces] to Carillon to stop the enemy there or prevent him from spending the winter there, if he were in possession of it. After all these considerations you see, Monseigneur, that the least change in my dispositions would have the most unfortunate consequences: if the English seized Carillon and held it, they would take only too much advantage of it; they would make their powerful efforts to penetrate into the [?] government; the position in which I am is very thorny, whatever reflections my dispositions may be; I cannot, Monseigneur, guarantee you the events; I shall do my best constantly, I shall watch over everything; and it will be only through the insufficiency of my forces, the exhaustion of the Colony's resources and the extreme superiority of the enemy that he will owe his advantages, if he has any.

Can I, Monseigneur, have more powerful reasons for wishing the arrival of [your relief as early as] possible, in the good spring season?

1st, the battalions I had the honour of asking you for;
2nd, munitions [?] [sign ?];
3rd, provisions, essentially: this item, although placed last, occupies [and] worries the settlers and the troops in general.

I make of it [sign ?], Monseigneur, my most laborious industry and my continual care; I have drawn from the Canadians even their own food;
I have taken by authority all the [?] [?] [?] [?] [?] of the [?] [?] [?] [?] [?]; I appealed in the King's name, with the most pressing urgency, to the tithes of the parish priests; the Bishop was good enough to contribute by his exhortations to these gentlemen.
In spite of that I can so little flatter myself that I shall complete the provisions that are indispensably necessary to take the field that M. Bigot has been forced to have half of these provisions brought down to Québec to keep the people alive. We are totally short of goods. The Indian nations of the upper country in general suffer in their most urgent needs; the English are using every means to seduce them. Can I promise myself that the nations of the south will come to the Presqu'île and that all those of the north will come to this town? I dare not count on it; but if they arrive there, could I keep them, being
bare of everything and especially of subsistence? All of them, it is probable, would withdraw, pressed by their needs; they would have recourse to the English, who, welcoming them, would draw the same services from them as we.
I am studying the English movements and I am preparing everything to march our troops, militia and Indians as soon as I know them on the way; if I brought forward their departure, their provisions once consumed, there would remain to me no
[least] way left.

It is all the more to be wished that you have provided, Monseigneur, for sending to the Colony a supplement to the provisions asked for by M. Bigot and by the contractor, since the greater part of our inhabitants have no grain to sow their lands.
In whatever state I may be reduced, I shall never lack zeal or confidence in the defence of this Colony. I beg you, Monseigneur,
to be so good as to assure His Majesty that there is no [?] [?] that I shall not overcome to prevent the English from making any progress or to make them pay very dear for it.

*I am with very deep respect, Monseigneur, your very humble and very obedient servant, Vaudreuil.*
