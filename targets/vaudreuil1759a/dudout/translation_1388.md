## 1388 — Montréal, 8 April 1759 (ANOM COL C11A 104, ff. 54-66)

My Lord,
In the letters I had the honour to write to you in cipher last autumn, I set out to give you as exact an account as I could
of the sad state to which this colony was reduced. What followed has proved to me only too well that it was even more
critical than I had had the honour to tell you. Day by day its extreme want of everything and the most dreadful misery of
the colonists have grown. Soon {there will be} no hope left of finding resources: horned cattle, domestic animals and horses
are almost entirely gone. Several of the better-off inhabitants, my Lord, have only the animals of a single plough left; most
of the others have none at all. These animals have served to make up for the bread cut from the troops; the people have
only four ounces of bread a day and nothing else to live on.
The English are well aware of it; they {hasten} to seize so fine an opportunity to {invade} this colony. Food, munitions of
war and everything else are as plentiful with them as we are short of them. The attacks they threaten us with seem most
certain. It is no longer a question of the same forces they used in the previous campaigns: they are more than doubling them.
They expect some resistance from us, they make no secret of it; nor do they make a secret that nothing will withstand their
obstinacy and their fury.
I have lost no time in setting all the forces of this colony in motion. I have had a very exact census made of the three
governments: that of Quebec will give me six {thousand} three hundred and fifty-six men; that of Montréal five thousand four
hundred and fifty-five; that of Trois-Rivières one thousand three hundred and thirteen. And only the sick and those too old to
serve will remain. From the number of militia fit for war one must even deduct the men indispensable for transport and the
other needs of the service. We have besides the eight battalions of the land troops and the forty companies of the Marine
troops, which are not complete.
I must, my Lord, provide:
1. for the {defen}ce of our frontier at Carillon, which will probably be attacked by twenty-five to thirty thousand men. You
saw, my Lord, from my letters of last autumn, that my energy in relieving that side had obliged the English to give up the
attack, {and} had even {hastened} their retreat. The Marquis de Montcalm could have made it extremely {difficult} for them, but
although I wrote to him, and the Marine troops, the Canadians and the Indians showed him their wish to pursue the enemy,
nothing could bring him round to my views.
2. for the {defen}ce of the Belle-Rivière [Ohio] against an army of about ten thousand men. I had foreseen the fate of Fort
Duquesne. M. de Lignery, having had to reduce his force in proportion to his provisions, took the wisest course in evacuating
and blowing up that fort as six to seven thousand English approached, and withdrawing to {Fort} Machault. He holds out there. I
have the forces and other help near him to support him, but the thing seems very difficult.
3. for the {defen}ce of Niagara, which is to be besieged by ten thousand men. You have seen that this {post} and those of the
Belle-Rivière are to help one another, and to give preference to the {defen}ce of Niagara as the main and most essential one,
the key of the river of the upper country. By this arrangement I [...] that of our enemies, who want to join their army of the
Ohio to the one they are to send to Niagara. M. Pouchot, captain in the Béarn regiment, is to take up the command there again. I
have given him my instructions for every case; he will above all try to oppose the enemy's landing.
4. for the {defen}ce of La Présentation, to keep our communication on Lake Ontario, and to {set} limits to the very ambitious
designs of our enemies, who announce ten thousand men for that side and want by that way to push on to Montréal with an even
stronger army.
This winter I had two corvettes built, of ten twelve-pounders each, which will serve for the transports to Niagara and will
manoeuvre as circumstances allow to {harass} the enemy. I have moreover set up a flying camp to watch the English and to be
told of their movements on that lake. They want to re-establish Oswego and will employ ten thousand men on it. All things
considered, I see the same plan reborn that the Duke of Cumberland conceived. General {Hamerts} [Amherst], who was his
aide-de-camp, doubtless wants to make good General Braddock's failure.
Quebec too is threatened by forces incomparably greater than all those of this colony. The fortifications of that town need many
repairs, and even outworks. For all these works four thousand men would be needed: where can they be found, and how fed, in
this position? I have confined myself to having such materials prepared during the winter as could be gathered without causing
any extraordinary {consumption} of food. M. de Pontleroy will stay in Quebec to carry on the most urgent works as far as our
situation allows.
The safety of this capital much occupies those who do not share my views; from this come arguments and talk which creep in and
gradually become public. But the objects I have in view are at this {moment} the most pressing. Indeed, my Lord, it is quite
certain that our frontiers and the {posts} I mean to {defen}d will be attacked; it is even to be feared that most will give way
to the enemy's too great superiority. But it is my policy to do my best to keep them; to abandon them would sound ill in every
respect:
1. all our Indians, seeing no more Frenchmen, would find themselves obliged to join the English;
2. the English, no longer busy far away, would no longer be bound to hard and painful marches, or to finding means of easing and
speeding the transport of the [...] of their army; they could unite and push forward rapidly as far as they liked;
3. finally, if we had peace, they would find themselves at little cost in {possession} of the {posts} we had evacuated, and so of
the immense extent of country that depends on them.
It is not the same with Quebec. I {believe} indeed the English have an inordinate desire to conquer it. But 1. it is not at all
certain that they have at will all they need for so important an expedition; 2. I must naturally {believe} myself assured
[that] the King, having been informed by you, my Lord, of the insufficiency both of our forces and of our means, His Majesty,
knowing of such an armament, will have been pleased to provide for opposing that enterprise; 3. supposing I had to {defen}d Quebec
with the weak forces and small means [we have] at present, our enemies could not reach it without my being informed long
before by the look-outs I have posted as far as the mouth of the river. Now, however diligent a fleet that sails with care may
be, it cannot reach Quebec before the fate of the battle the English are to give us at Carillon is decided.
Whatever the outcome of that battle, my Lord, I shall always keep the freedom to go to Quebec myself with most of our troops and
all the militia and Indian nations I can gather in the three governments. I shall leave at Carillon only what is indispensable,
and the armed xebecs. Once at Quebec I shall give the enemy battle, and do so again until I have forced him to withdraw, or until,
overwhelmed by his excessive superiority, he has entirely reduced me. Given my determination to oppose the landing, [it] will be
all the more in place since I should not have enough powder, bombs, shot etc. to sustain a siege for six days. If I have the
success I hope for, I shall at once march all these [forces] to Carillon to stop the enemy there, or prevent him from wintering
there if he had taken {possession} of it.
From all these considerations you see, my Lord, that the least change in my arrangements would have the most unfortunate
consequences. If the English took Carillon and held it, they would make only too much of that advantage; they would make their
mightiest efforts to break into the colony.
My position is a thorny one. However {well considered} my arrangements, I cannot, my Lord, guarantee you the outcome. I shall
always do my best, I shall watch over everything, and it will be only to the insufficiency of my forces, the exhaustion of the
colony's resources and the enemy's extreme superiority that he will owe his successes, if he has any.
Could I, my Lord, have stronger reasons to wish for the arrival, very early in the spring, 1. of the battalions I have had the
honour to ask of you; 2. of munitions [...] etc.; 3. of food? This last object above all, though placed last, occupies [and]
worries the colonists and the troops in general. I make it, my Lord, my most laborious industry and my constant care. I have
taken from the Canadians even their own food; I have taken by {auth}ority all the meal of the mills; I appealed in the King's name,
with the most urgent entreaties, to the tithes of the parish priests; the Bishop was good enough to help by his exhortations to
those gentlemen. In spite of this I can so little hope to make up the provisions indispensable to take the field that M. Bigot has
been forced to send {half} of these provisions down to Quebec to feed the people. We are {tota}lly short of goods. The Indian
nations of the upper country in general suffer in their most urgent needs. The English do everything to win them over. Can I
expect that the southern nations will come to Presqu'île and all the northern ones to this town? I dare not count on it. And if
they came, could I keep them, being destitute of everything, and above all of {provisions}? They would probably {withdraw};
pressed by their needs, they would turn to the English, who, welcoming them, would get the same services from them as we do.
I study the movements of the English and {prepare} everything to march our troops, militia and Indians {as soon} as I know they
are on the way. If I sent them off too early, once their food was eaten I should have no means left at all. It is all the more to
be wished that you have provided, my Lord, for sending the colony a supplement to the provisions asked for by M. Bigot and by
the contractor, since most of our inhabitants have no grain to sow their fields.
Whatever state I may be reduced to, I shall never lack zeal or confidence for the defence of this colony. I beg you, my Lord, to
be good enough to assure His Majesty of it, and that there is no obstacle I will not overcome to prevent the English from making
any progress, or to make them pay very dearly for it.
I am with very deep respect, my Lord, your very humble and very obedient servant. Vaudreuil [in clear]
