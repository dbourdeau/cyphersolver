## What it is

Catalogue entry 1285 is the duplicate of despatch No. 35 from Thomas Robinson, 2nd Baron Grantham, British ambassador in
Spain, to Thomas Thynne, 3rd Viscount Weymouth, Secretary of State: a stray leaf at Houghton Library, Harvard, with 62
code numbers and nothing written between the lines. It was vetted as open on 13 November 2026.

The received original is at The National Archives, Kew, **SP 94/205 ff. 268r-268v**. A clerk of the Secretary of State's
office wrote the English between the lines of numbers when it arrived (the docket on the next leaf reads "R 6 July"). So
the passage **was read in 1778**, and entry 1285 can be closed as read at the time. What this folder adds:

1. the original located, and its numbers and the clerk's English transcribed with their places on the page;
2. the comparison with the Harvard duplicate (the same 62 numbers);
3. an account of the 62 numbers, including the 19 that have nothing written over them;
4. a preliminary partial table of the code from fifteen other deciphered despatches of 1778 in the same volume, with a
   test written before it was run, one part of which missed its mark.

It is not an independent solution and the code is not solved.

## The text (the clerk's decipherment of 1778)

> I have no reason to think there is any change in the Sentiments which I communicated in my last Letter. I imagine that
> Mons.r de Florida Blanca will propose Santander for the establishment of the Packet boats, in which case I apprehend
> that a Vice Consul or Agent should be appointed to reside there.

54 words, no holes (holes are counted by words; there are none because every word is the clerk's). Before the code the
despatch says in clear "I have nothing particular to acquaint your Lordship with by this Post"; after it, news of the
Queen Dowager of Portugal and the Patriarch's cardinal's cap. Files: `transcriptions/n35_cipher.txt` (the numbers),
`transcriptions/n35_clerk_decipherment.txt` (the English as it stands on the page), `transcriptions/n35_office_copy.tsv`
(each number with the words directly above it and the pen strokes), `reading/n35_reading.txt`.

Context. In June 1778 Spain stood neutral between Britain and France and was offering to mediate. No. 35 carries a
smaller matter: a British packet service by sea to northern Spain. No. 36 (25 June, next in the volume) explains the
last clause: there was no vice-consul at Santander because Grantham "thought it highly improper that any Person of the
House of Gardoqui should keep that Employment". Antonio Meijide Pardo, *Correos marítimos entre Falmouth y La Coruña
(1689-1815)* (La Coruña: Instituto "José Cornide" de Estudios Coruñeses, 1966), pp. 39-40, gives the same substance from
the Spanish side (Archivo General de Simancas, Secretaría de Estado, leg. 7004, a document of 12 September 1778):
Grantham backed two English vessels for Santander and asked for an agent to reside there "como se practica en Lisboa";
a royal order of 13 September 1778 approved a Falmouth to Santander service. He does not print or cite No. 35.

## How the decipherment sits on the page

The clerk's English is running text in the spaces between the lines of numbers. It is not written word over number. In
the despatches in a secretary's small hand (nine or ten numbers to a line) each English line roughly matches the numbers
under it. In those in Grantham's own large hand (five to seven numbers to a line), No. 35 among them, the English has
more words than the line has numbers: it runs ahead, ends before the numbers do, and the clerk filled the spaces under
the remaining lines with pen strokes. In the Private letter of 10 April 109 words stand over 93 numbers and the last
three lines are bare; the words cannot all belong to the numbers they stand over.

So the first 4 and the last 15 numbers of No. 35, which have nothing written over them, are **not unread text**. Number
and word can be paired only by taking both in order, helped by numbers that recur in other despatches.

## The 62 numbers (`reading/n35_account.tsv`)

| Status | Count | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| firm | 11 | the table built from the other despatches gives this word |
| one-crib | 9 | one or two places in the other despatches agree |
| placed by order | 31 | the word falls there only by its order between firmer neighbours; a guess |
| taken as null | 11 | the first 4 and the last 7; also a guess |

The sentence is placed as ending at the 55th number (2035 "there"); "Consul" (700) is the 47th, the last on the recto,
where the English above has already ended. Of the 19 numbers with nothing over them: 1 firm, 1 one-crib, 6 placed by
order, 11 taken as nulls. No number is left that must hold text the clerk did not write down; that statement rests on
the alignment, not on a key.

## The code and the table (`key/table_preliminary.tsv`)

- Numbers from 5 to about 4,000; two-part (number order does not follow the alphabet: 1852 of, 1854 the, 1862 taken),
  so nothing can be filled in by interpolation. Many alternative numbers for common words.
- Floridablanca has a number of his own (3709), which the clerk writes out as "M. de Florida Blanca". Names not in the
  code are spelled with several numbers (St. Andero or Santander, Gardoqui); the spelling numbers are not resolved.
- Passages open and close with unmeaning numbers. Which numbers serve as nulls is not established.
- The table: 1,209 distinct numbers seen in the fifteen despatches (2,314 in all); **263 "firm"** entries, 761 "weak",
  185 with none. `key/crib_pairs.tsv` gives every number of the fifteen with despatch, folio, line and the word matched.
- **Reliability.** The reader's estimate was that about a quarter of the firm entries may be wrong. The verifier's is
  **about a third**: on despatches left out of the count, 70% of firm content-word entries and 82% of firm
  function-word entries fall in order in the clerk's text, against 6.5% and 39.5% for shuffled tables, which puts the
  share that are right near two thirds. Entries for common words can match by accident. Three firm entries are known not
  to fit No. 35 (717 "the", 423 "of", 1178 "should"). Rebuilding the table from this folder gives the same best alignment
  but not the same sixteen runs (one differs, cause not found), and that moves six entries across the firm/weak line
  (3709 among them): the grade is fragile at its edge, and the frozen table is the one the test used.
- The code is not identified with a named British code. That it is one of the printed codes of the office is an
  inference; none was seen.

## The test (`tests/`)

The plan was written and the table frozen before any score on No. 35 was computed; the reader had already seen No. 35's
numbers and English and had formed the "runs ahead" view (the plan says so). One run.

| Test | Mark set beforehand | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A. The table's words for No. 35's numbers, matched in order against the clerk's 54 words | at least 60% of covered numbers and above every one of 200 shuffled tables | **Passed**: 16 covered, 11 matched (69%); shuffled tables 3.6 on average, 6 at most |
| B. The table's word against the words directly over the number | 60% if the page were word over number; expected to fail | **Failed, as expected**: 13 covered, 0 matched |
| C. Firm entries among the last 15 numbers matched in the last 15 words | at least 2 | **Missed**: 2 covered ("there", "if"), 1 matched |

The verifier re-ran the test from the frozen files and got the same figures. Test C had only two numbers to work with;
its miss means the pre-stated rule does not show that the last lines carry text. That they do rests on 2035 "there"
(twice elsewhere), 700 "Consul" (twice elsewhere) and, less firmly, 3460 "should" and 1092 "Agent".

## What would settle it

The code book itself. The National Archives catalogue lists printed codes K and L of 1759 at SP 106/38-39, and a Paris
embassy hand-over of 1772 names "K.L.M.N.O.P." (SP 78/285/84); these are not in State Papers Online and were not seen.
One page of the right table would replace this one and show which numbers are nulls.
