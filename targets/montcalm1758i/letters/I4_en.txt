I answer, in my letter common to you and M. de Vaudreuil, those you did me the honour of writing to me in cipher on the situation of the colony of Canada, however painful it may be. His Majesty trusts to your zeal and attachment to his service that, in concert with M. de Vaudreuil, you will use every means to defend Canada again this campaign, or at least to keep its most essential part, so as to be able afterwards to recover the whole more easily.
I have written a private letter to M. de Vaudreuil, in which I recommend him to consult you on all operations and to act in concert with you. He will lend himself to it all the more willingly since I have learnt with pleasure that there is no longer any question between you and him of the small matters of altercation there had been before. In short, Monsieur, the point is to unite to prevent the invasion of Canada and save this colony for the King. That is all one can say to a general and a citizen as zealous as you.
M. de Vaudreuil will show you a letter that I am writing to him jointly with M. Bigot about the shipments that can be made to Canada this year; they will be smaller than what was asked for, but that is all that circumstances have allowed at a moment when forces are being gathered in an attempt to relieve every quarter by some decisive operation. M. de Bougainville, who has carried out exactly the commission you gave him, will tell you himself how impossible it is to procure you greater help at present, so as to make it surer and more effective later. But the confidence His Majesty has in
the resources of your zeal, which made you win, with fewer than four thousand men, a complete victory over a considerable army on the day of Carillon, makes him hope that with troops so brave, and with what you will add to them of the colony's troops and the militia of the country, you will manage to halt the enemy's progress and make most of their undertakings useless.
