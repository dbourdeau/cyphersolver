## Summary

Pelissier, the League's envoy at Philip II's court, sets out for Jeannin how things are being argued in Spain. First comes the suspicion that the King wants France kept in a balance of mutual weakness, so that he can dispose of the French as he likes and take his share of a divided crown where it suits him. People back this up with his past conduct: His Majesty has "several times been lulled" about the state of affairs, and the business drags on "in a length that promises no end". Pelissier then gives the case made by those who would recognise Henri of Navarre (code group [315]). God has called him "as by miracle" to the succession ahead of so many others who stood before him, so he must be recognised though a heretic. They hope God will touch his heart and grant him the grace of reunion. Having lived from his youth "in perpetual pain and labour, poor and miserable", and now at the turn of his age, he will want peace and try to keep his subjects. If he ever moved against the Catholics they would unite against him and be released from their oath of fidelity. Pelissier warns that these arguments, though refutable, work on war-weary people. Such people fear ruin from continued war more than the establishment of the [315], and they hear these ideas from relatives and friends of the same nation. The Catholic princes might not prevent a conclusion so harmful to religion "not only in [441] (probably France) but in all other regions of Christendom". He asks that His Majesty, recognising that he alone can cure this malady, take up so pious an action. To the name of "Catholic" he would then add "most Christian" and "liberator of Christendom". The sentence runs on to f. 50r.
