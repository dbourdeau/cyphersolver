## Summary

Pelissier, the League's agent at Philip II's court, is setting out for Jeannin the military plan he is urging on
the Spanish king. Spain should raise a force in France. It should be mostly French and "in great number, horse and
foot", to weaken the Béarnais (code 315), as long as the men are paid. To it should be joined "a corps of seven or
eight thousand Germans". If judged right, some Spaniards and Walloons under good captains would give the commander
enough strength not to refuse battle. A victory would ruin the enemy, who could get no reinforcements, at a moment
when everyone is tired of the war. A defeat could be absorbed, because the surviving army, made up of nations
subject to Philip, could gather the remnants and halt the enemy's advance. Spain should continue its present aid
and extend it to other provinces. If that is too expensive, the king should weigh that the present piecemeal aid
ruins the provinces rather than relieving them. With the two sides evenly matched, they only take small towns that
the enemy retakes. It would be better to keep an army together that carries the war into the provinces. This would
revive Catholic morale and lead the League to "a lasting settlement ... to the ruin of their opponents".

Pelissier then turns to the king question. If Philip showed some particular preference "in the nomination of the
king", everyone would fall in line. Some would do so out of goodwill and some out of obligation for his help. The
reluctant would be driven by fear, and by "the advantage that the name and presence of a king brings to the
state". The page ends with political advice. A great monarch must know "the customs, conditions and affections in
which the nations subject to him were born, bred and raised", and also those of the nations he wants to use. By
governing them accordingly he makes them more pliable and his rule more bearable. The last row begins an argument
about the difficulty of changing "so many thousands of men".
