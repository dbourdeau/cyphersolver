## Summary

Pelissier, the League's envoy at Philip II's court, reports what he told the king. The French have always done willingly
what was asked of them rather than under force; forced, they would rather die to a man. Examples go back centuries and
include the last king (Henri III), against whom people rose for exactly this reason. Mildness, friendship and benefits will
lead them anywhere, so if Philip follows that course he will win honour and reputation with every nation. That would ruin
and confound the "wicked" (the royalists); the French would look for ways to employ their means in his service; he would
draw them out of their miseries and make his memory immortal. The king heard him out without interrupting. He said he would
first ask some questions, starting with whether Pelissier spoke on Mayenne's orders or on his own initiative. Pelissier
replied that he had had no word from Monseigneur since the armies faced each other near Caudebec (spring 1592), when Mayenne
had judged it necessary to raise two armies. His friends, knowing he was here, kept him informed of popular feeling and the
enemy's tricks. Since many reports agreed, he thought it his duty to tell the king the plain truth. He asked that the
grievances he had raised be remedied, or at least that he have the satisfaction of having done his duty. Past failures fed the
enemy's slanders and the people's loss of hope of relief through Spanish aid. Philip had spared no expense, but the illness was
growing, so stronger remedies were needed. People in the provinces know better than others what is good or bad; the sentence
runs over onto f. 47v.
