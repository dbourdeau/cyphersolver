# R289 — Pallotto to Barberini, Vienna, 10 August 1629: English translation

Translation of `R289_reading.md` (22 Oct 2026, Claude Opus 5.5). The text follows the Roman decipherment printed by
Kiewning (1897), Nr. 156, corrected to the figures of this sheet in three places; the clear words on the sheet agree with
it. The despatch continues on R290.

---

In accordance with the answer given to my note, of which I sent a copy with the last post, I learned that leading
ministers, and among them Prince Eggenberg, were saying to anyone who gave them occasion that the settlement of these
troubles depended on Our Lord, in whose hands it lay to make the King of France withdraw from Italy, just as he could
also have prevented his coming; but that he had not only not done it of his own accord, but not even when urged by the
instances of the Emperor, who had written him an express letter about it.

I therefore resolved to speak to His Majesty, and told him that, even if as nuncio of Our Lord I could believe I had
discharged my duty by having set out to His Majesty by word of mouth and in writing His Holiness's wish, and by sending
him the answer His Majesty had been pleased to have given me, of whatever kind it might be, I did not consider that I
was discharging the obligation of a good servant of His Majesty unless I put before him what occurred to me about that
answer; for, persuading myself that His Majesty has wished to satisfy His Holiness's instances, I could not doubt that it
was foreign to his intention. That to His Holiness's regret at not obtaining what he has sought for the public good of
Christendom there was added that of seeing His Majesty, instead of appreciating what he has actually done and the more
he has wished to do for the same end and for the benefit of the House of Austria, blame him for the contrary, and put it
in writing that, remaining in the public chancery and having to pass through the hands of the greatest princes and
personages in the world, would bear witness very different from what is due to the supreme vigilance and zeal for the
peace of Christendom, and to the fatherly and cordial affection towards His Imperial Majesty and the House of Austria,
with which Our Lord had acted continually from the beginning of these affairs.

And here I explained and repeated to His Majesty what I had told him several times: that besides the efforts made first
through the ordinary nuncios and then by sending extraordinary nuncios to Spain, to Italy and here, His Holiness had
wished to send him to the King of France as well, and had not done so because of the representations of the Count of
Monterrey. That His Majesty's letter was delivered to His Holiness no earlier than 27 March, and thus twenty-one days
after the King of France had seized the pass of Susa, and after the capitulations concluded with Savoy and Don Gonzalo,
and after the siege of Casale had been lifted. That Our Lord had on that occasion pointed out to Prince Savelli that,
things being in that state, any negotiation would be useless, because the answer would be that the business was
settled, and, an answer from Spain being awaited, no other negotiation could be set on foot meanwhile; but, more
important, that if representations were made in accordance with His Majesty's letter, touching the French on the point
that the arms of the Emperor and of the Catholic King would descend in force upon Italy, it would only serve to irritate
the King of France and push him, to the prejudice of the public peace, and precisely of the states of the Catholic King
and of the House of Austria, to what might be feared against neighbouring states from a king in person with the powerful
and numerous army he had close at hand, and in Provence under the Duke of Guise, and with the arms of the allies, without
soldiers, without money and without provisions, indeed in a time of extreme famine. That to Our Lord's representations
was chiefly owed the fact that the Most Christian King had not turned his mind and forces to new acquisitions in Italy,
although perhaps urged on by others, and that he had returned to France and recalled Cardinal Richelieu there.

His Majesty showed himself so new to these points that I was astonished. After saying that he had written the letter
before the French entered, and hearing that I was not concerned with the time the letter was written but was saying
when it was delivered to Our Lord, which was not before 27 March, His Majesty spoke like one who suspected that his
ambassador had been put off from his audience and the opportunity to deliver it earlier. To which I added, begging His
Majesty not to persuade himself of any such thing, which I would rather believe had not been written by the Lord
Ambassador, but rather that he had delivered it at his own convenience; and that, if he had not waited for the ordinary
audience, an extraordinary audience would not have been refused him whenever he had asked for one. In conclusion His
Majesty declared that he welcomed the representation I had made to him (for, so as not to commit Our Lord in any event,
I always declared that I was not speaking as nuncio), and that it was his intention that every possible satisfaction be
given to His Holiness, and he told me to speak of it with Prince Eggenberg.

I went to His Excellency the same day, and with him I enlarged further and weighed more closely the words "sequitur
etc." and "non adsequitur etc.", the first of which, especially after what preceded, seemed to touch not only the
efficacy but also the sincerity of Our Lord's representations, and the second to qualify His Holiness's request as less
justified and almost unreasonable. Truly I was confounded when I realised that to the Lord Prince too the things
mentioned above came as news, though I had told them to him again and again; but in the end he was persuaded by the
evidence of what I told him, and expressed to me with great emphasis the supreme reverence and respect that His Majesty
bears the Apostolic See and Our Lord, and that it is as far from his intention to do or say anything by which he might be
offended as it is his most particular wish to give every greater and livelier demonstration of deference and respect
towards that Holy See. He concluded that he would send me Questenberg, who had drafted the answer, so that he might hear
