## Reading

The Spanish court, continues still so lockt up, that no judgment can be formed when they will declare. They have fifty
ships of the line, ready; Their finances are in excellent order, & nothing seems wanting but resolution.

The Marquis your Brigadeer is to return, with 2000 French [troops?]. I do not like the plan. Our alliance has given me
a nearer view of the manner in which things are conducted here, & of their disposition, & I am convinced it is not
safe to let them come too near us, or introduce their [troops?] into our country, or continent on which I am by no
means satisfyd they do not wish for a footing. We must not rely too much on their being restrained by the treaty.
European politicians are much at ease on that head.

Never will our affairs go well, while Benjamin is here. For god sake let some sensible & respectable person be sent
instead & put an end to all this disgraceful & dangerous work, of which he is & ever will be the Father. In my
judgment none of those hitherto employd woud be proper. That woud have the air of party & injure our cause. But
surely a new man of dignity & ability may be found, who will be far above those meannesses which render the
representatives the [contempt?] of the represented. He must win the cause. In one word he is the servant of this court, not
of his country. They have him.

Our Enemies are distressd, but they are determined. The late Successes have augmented their resources, but their
expense is enormous. The weakness with which things are conducted here, & the wickedness which Benjamin, Deane, &
junto, have been carrying on, gives them hopes. They judge of the corruptibility of all by these men; & still hope
this will be a means of their success.

I have reflected very much upon the propriety of presenting the piece against the old Doctor, & I think it will
entirely depend upon the part taken by John Adams. If he speaks out, & does not act the base & insidious part he has
done here, which I fear he will, they may be presented with effect. There is but one person whom he does not seem to
envy, hate & speak ill of & that is Samuel, if he therefore make a point of his speaking out, I think he woud have
great weight. notwithstanding his conduct has convinced me that of all [viceroys?] he is the most base & most unjust,
& yet I, never quarrelld with him & I hope [you?] will not. He is too contemptible for that. Be upon your gaurd
against him. The successor of Gerard has often declard his opinion here of the old Doctor's unfitness to be minister,
here. Perhaps he will do so with you. It must have weight. Take the favorable moment of making the attack. All success
depends upon. Vis consili expers mole ruit sua. Both the new minister, & Secretary, are of worth & deserve your
cultivating.

## Summary

Lee reports that Spain has fifty ships of the line ready but has not declared; that "the Marquis your Brigadeer" is to
return with 2,000 French men (the word after "French" is doubtful); and that he does not trust the French on
American soil, suspecting they "wish for a footing". He writes that affairs will never go well "while Benjamin is
here" and asks for a new man to be sent. Who "he" is in "he is the servant of this court, not of his country" is not
fixed by the code: the sentence before is about the new man. He says that presenting his piece against "the old
Doctor" depends on "the part taken by John Adams", and that Gérard's successor has spoken of the old Doctor's
unfitness to be minister. Whom "he" means in the passage about the most base and unjust man is also not fixed by the
code. These are Lee's private views to his brother.
