# English translation (partial)

Square brackets: words supplied from the context or the clear text, or gaps ([...]) where the cipher is not read. Translated from reading.md; where the Italian has unresolved words the English follows the sense of the surrounding clear text and says so.

[f. 49v] ... but would to God that this were the only ground of his hope [Navarre's hope of seeing Paris fall through the “practice of commerce”]; but there are so many others besides, such as [...] the instruction I sent to Your Most Illustrious Lordship with my letter of the twenty-sixth of November, and I shall speak of it in its place. May God help us! [f. 50r] As for the passports and letters patent issued on that occasion, [...] precisely what I foretold has happened: although those letters had been partly amended by this Council before being published, they had not been put in such a form that one of them, fallen into Navarre's hands, would not irritate him. [...] and he has had orders given to hold back all that can be, and to hang without appeal those who carry them, saying that he did not mean to give Mayenne the whip to whip him with. Cardinal de Gondi, besides what he writes to me, has written to the Provost of the Merchants — who, at my instance, was inviting him back for these Christmas feasts, but on condition of the oath, to calm the people — that he would not swear. So he has not come. Meanwhile the service of the church [...]. [f. 50v] [The Dean has admitted to others what he denies to me: that he spoke to Navarre,] “the handsome, the [...] and the most favoured of all”, of whom, in the letter that was not left with me, a eulogy of purity and innocence is made [...] to St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist. One of the good canons complaining of it, and coming to words with another over whether or not to adhere to the heretic, the latter let slip that he would kiss even the devil rather than consent to the heretic to save himself; and no demonstration has been made of it. So I wrote to Cardinal de Gondi to order things as best he thought [for the Te Deum for the election of Gregory XIV], and have brought back an answer [...]. [f. 51r] [The preachers] are all the more stirred up; indeed, in the said letter great complaint is made [of this opposition]: that Senlis [Guillaume Rose] and his followers are these seditious men and the cause of the controversy. [...] the concert [with Navarre] is about to vanish; [Madame de Guise showed me a letter] from La Châtre; [he] had left Orléans on the twenty-seventh of last month for the Berry; [...] left for Nemours. The Jesuit Provincial, seeing little hope of bearing fruit here, [goes to meet Mayenne]. [f. 51v] I did not go with them, so as not to hasten the loss of this city, since by staying it falls to me to keep [it] to its duty, if nothing else. [Mayenne] has sent the Count of Brissac here to explore by various means the true state of Paris: [...] most unhappy and [most perilous], exposed to sedition, to escalade or some other stratagem, or to the will to surrender to another prince; and if he does not relieve it, [...] let the enemy in. [...] He came back on the night of the fourth and carries a letter of mine [...]. We ask for a thousand paid Spanish and Italian foot, victuals for three months for them, [...] where we have within the provision of food [...] and about a hundred [and fifty] horse, [...] and to order the neighbouring garrisons to keep in touch with these. [f. 52r] [...] the escalade of Saint-Denis [...]: if it came into our power, we could [hold] the enemy until the arrival of the Duke of Parma. If Mayenne then relieves [Paris], I do not leave; if he does not, being able [to leave] with the Duchess of Guise, I shall have satisfied my conscience and the world. Cardinal de Gondi [seeks the way] to separate the princes of the blood and the Catholic nobility from Navarre; [I put the flea in his ear: by making] the separation [and throwing themselves] at the Pope's feet, [they] would easily place themselves under his protection [to obey the one] whom an assembly [...] of the Holy See [would make king]. [f. 52v] [It is time to raise] the siege [...]. Brissac: if Mayenne is not prompt and diligent, [he may find himself with a fistful of flies]; between the princes, the Catholic nobility and Navarre; not only of this city but of all the other cities of the Union; [...] the Catholic nobility, already ruined by the vain hopes and promises of that man [Navarre]; [the greatest of services will be that] the Pope [declare] incurring the censures all who follow Navarre, ecclesiastics or laymen, unless they [...] and swear within a certain time, [f. 53r] according to the formula I sent to Your Most Illustrious Lordship or another suitable one; and this monitory or declaratory [...] would come in time, accompanied by a good warning [to the princes] of the Union about the errors [...] to the prejudice of the good progress of the cause; [...] for otherwise, through the obstinacy of some, the blindness of others and private interests, everything is running to ruin, as Your Most Illustrious Lordship will see from the enclosed copies of writings fabricated and sent [...] to Melun, sent back by a [...] to the Provincial, and which I had sent to Mayenne, so that [Your Lordship] may judge in your prudence whether what they contain is seditious [...] and of all too evident truth. [f. 53v] [...] Corbeil, [...] Lagny, Saint-Cloud; [...] the tightening of commerce; [...] neither eat nor drink [...]. Four [plots] have been discovered: at Orléans, one at Nantes, the second; the third at Chalon in Burgundy [...]; [they have] written to Mayenne [that], as he is not strong enough to defend them, they will provide for themselves; [they ask] the King of Spain to receive them into his particular protection; [...] for the service and glory of God. [And of all these plots no punishment is seen through the diligence] of Mayenne, [except that] of Orléans. [f. 54r] [As for that of] Chalon, [said to have been made with the participation] of the castellan of that citadel, [perhaps nothing more will come of it]; [...] in the arsenal. [We wait to see what comes of the negotiation of] Brissac. [...] of importance. [Impertinent resolutions are feared, born] from this third [...] of the Council [...] of President Le Maistre [...]: [the first] cannot speak or discern; [the second ...] married his niece [...]; [the third is the soul] of Cardinal de Gondi; [the fourth is] the precipice. [The two are closely bound] with the deliberations [...] the votes. [...] in council; [...] to Mayenne. [The most ridiculous battle in the world:] the City orders, the [...] Parlement prevents, the [...] Council opposes; [everything is done and undone,] one against the other, the deliberations [...]. [f. 54v] [Mayenne, moreover, is so inconstant that he wants and does not want the same thing at the same moment.] Between the Parlement and the Sixteen, between the magistrate and those of the police, among the members of the Parlement themselves, one against the other, and with little obedience, all [turn against] this weak head; [...] the author of the second [writing]; [and as this] head [speaks different languages according to the men] who surround him [...]: thus, when the head aches, the other limbs languish (“dum caput dolet, cetera membra languent”). [But no more of this for now: the writing will treat more fully] of the defects of the Union. [Meanwhile, may Your Most Illustrious Lordship think of the remedy, which] would be to have the Pope asked to take under the protection, government and tutelage of the Holy See this city with the whole Île-de-France, [until an Assembly of the Estates and the canonical election of a Catholic and Most Christian King], when opportune, [to bring some order] to these confusions, and to turn the war away from these parts by the authority of His Holiness, excommunicating all [who molest the Catholics] [...] the State, with which they colour ...
