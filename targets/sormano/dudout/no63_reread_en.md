[... better] to settle, part of the difficulties raised by the said lord we shall not hide.

His Excellency says he does not see that, on the side of Your Majesty and your confederates, the provisions are being made
that would be needed to win the enterprise, all the more now that we are, as he said, in the good season, and to put the army
in the field [...] to be here, not to decide what must be done, and above all in Lombardy, an enterprise which, in his view,
must be carried out without losing time or an hour, with every care and diligence, so as to be able to attend to the other
more promptly, and so as not to give the enemy time to bring Spanish infantry, which are in the Genoese, into Milan and to
make other provisions, by which, if they are made, the said enterprise would become more difficult, longer and more
dangerous. And, he added, as things stand at Genoa, if nothing is provided on that side, time will bring it about that by
the road from Spain and from towards Naples, Milan will have succour of men and of other things.

Continuing, he said that, for such an enterprise, they ought to put their people in order; they had disbanded and were still
disbanding their infantry, and the duke of [?]Orvieto, little pleased with this act, quite against the present common need,
instead of planning his departure into the field, was going towards Brescia, intending to meet Monsieur de Saint-Pol, to
take counsel together on how to proceed on these and the other difficulties raised.

His Excellency added the no small fear in which <R> now seem to have fallen, namely that the Spaniards, having now recovered
L'Aquila and La Matrice and having quieted the affairs of the Abruzzo, plan to enter Tuscany to the harm of Florence; if that
happened, which God forbid, [...] they would recognise their danger [...] they have done in not having so supported and
succoured the affairs of the Abruzzo, as they were asked, urged and pressed by us and by the other agents of Your Majesty,
and all reason required that they at least do it for their own particular interest. For if they had done so, besides having
barred the enemy in the kingdom and hindered his collection of money, and consequently keeping the war in the house of his
enemy, they would have secured Florence, and in the said kingdom, on the side of Rome, because of the open league, a great
gate would have been held, which would have helped not a little [...] to draw the Pope into the league, or at least to keep
him thus suspended and neutral as until now.

After these and many other things, which are passed over so as not to trouble Your Majesty, His Excellency concluded,
having alleged these difficulties, that when in fact he saw such an army ready and at hand as so great an enterprise,
in his view not a little long and difficult, requires, and saw beside the army the provisions needed to maintain it as long
as necessary, then he would show how much and what desire to serve Your Majesty he holds; but not seeing the army, nor the
assurance of the provisions, above all of money, it weighs on him the more because it seems to him that the confederates of
Your Majesty, and chiefly <Venetians> and [...] Monsieur de Lautrec, have kept the agreements and their promises badly.
Among these considerations, he said that the <Venetians>, who, as Your Majesty wrote to His Excellency and to me, Sormano,
many days ago, should have approached him for this captaincy, had so far not only not approached him but not made any sign
or move showing that they would be content for him to accept it. If, about his answer on this captaincy, he did not dare go
further than what he had already answered me, Sormano, this should not be taken ill, since, not having the means to offend
<I> in deed, given the importance and danger of his state, he had either to be careful not to offend him in words; Your
Majesty, holding him for the good servant he is and wishes to be, should hold him excused.

Although these and other difficulties were fully answered by us, and with good and lively reasons we tried to refute them
and resolve them, nevertheless His Excellency stayed in his already settled purpose. And having nearly ended his talk, he
recalled two things for Your Majesty's service. One: that for the enterprise of Naples Your Majesty might perhaps use the
duke of Calabria, to whom, by the reasons [...] of Your Majesty and of the crown of France, Naples seems to belong, so he
said, adding that for such an enterprise, to make the difficult easy, no one could be appointed more suitable for Your
Majesty, more to the purpose, nor more acceptable to the nobility and peoples of the kingdom than that duke; and that if
Your Majesty decides and commands, His Excellency will procure to draw him out of the hands of <I>, whence he promises that
neither for the liberation of the lords your sons nor for any other cause will he put himself in the power of <I>. The other:
that for the captaincy of which we speak [...] the marquis of Mantua, and that Your Majesty, if it so pleased, could have him
approached for such [...].

His Excellency further recalled that it would be well that, <R> having fallen, as said, into very great fear of the Spaniards,
Your Majesty, in order to keep [...] office, order that Monsieur de Saint-Pol, if need [...] comes, be given all that can be
given him; that provision be made for the <Venetians> to do the same on their side; and that besides this it seems to him
that Your Majesty, both by your letters to <R> and in words to their ambassador at court, should keep them well content
[...] and comforted, and His Excellency [...] the same office. On his side, he said, he would not fail; nor have we, with the
ambassador of the Florentines who is kept here, failed or shall fail in anything that we can do. As to the restitution or
deposit of Ravenna and Cervia, considering the nature and quality of the <Venetians> and of this lord, he shows that the
matter must be not a little difficult, and here he concludes that the authority and credit of Marshal Trivulzio will help
and serve greatly in this negotiation and enterprise. As to the accord between the said lord and <the Pope>, His Excellency,
who shows he much desires it, persisting in what we wrote to Your Majesty in our previous letter, with all insistence begs
you to intervene, promising that when it comes to things that are honest and that the said duke can do and perform, he
will not fail, for the honour and satisfaction of Your Majesty.

Sire, although for the reasons given we have no or very little hope that this lord duke will wish to listen to the
captaincy, nevertheless, not wishing to omit in this cause any kind of effort and office, if Marshal Trivulzio, going to
Venice, passes through here, as we have asked him by our letters for Your Majesty's service, we think to work so that His
Lordship makes the last attempt with the said lord; and meanwhile for three or four days I, Joachim, shall wait for him here,
and if in that time I do not see him come, I shall go to Mantua to do with the lord marquis the office that it pleased Your
Majesty to command me; and this journey of mine towards His Lordship seems all the more necessary because the said lord has
written here to me, Sormano, and to others that by letters of Your Majesty he was advised that you had sent me, Joachim, to
him with a commission. Sire, since this lord duke, unless he changes, which given his nature we shall hardly believe, shows
he does not wish to listen [... continues on f. 123r].
