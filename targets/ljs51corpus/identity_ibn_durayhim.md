# Is the compiler of LJS 51 Ibn al-Durayhim?

Question: is the first-person compiler of Penn LJS 51 Taj al-Din 'Ali b. Muhammad Ibn al-Durayhim al-Mawsili
(b. Mosul, 15 Sha'ban 712; d. Qus, Safar 762)?

**Verdict: supported.** Three independent kinds of evidence agree: (1) the compiler names his own cipher book with the
title, and the first two chapter names, of Ibn al-Durayhim's treatise; (2) the compiler's patron al-'Ala'i is, from
a detail on f. 62r checked against Safadi, 'Ala' al-Din 'Ali b. Yahya Ibn Fadl Allah, head of the Cairo chancery,
and that passage falls after Safar 760, when Ibn al-Durayhim was in Egypt; (3) the compiler's movements, the people
around him and a second book he cites all match Safadi's account of Ibn al-Durayhim, written by a man who knew him.
Nothing found contradicts the identification. Two points are unconfirmed (the vizierate of Syria, Alexandria), and the
Miftah's chapter list is known here only from a secondary article (see section 4).

Worked 28 Nov 2026 from `chains_all.json` and the `translation_*.md` files in this folder, and from the biographical
sources as texts (OpenITI copies of the Shamela editions). No image was read for this note: the manuscript readings
are the chain records' readings.

### What the biographies say (paraphrase)

- **Family and name.** Son of al-Sahib Muwaffaq al-Din; Mosul family; nephew of Baha' al-Din al-Husayn Ibn Abi l-Khayr.
  His father died when he was small, and others took most of the estate. Safadi calls him "al-khawaja", the
  title of a great merchant (*A'yan* 3:519-521).
- **Career.** First came to Egypt as a merchant in 732 or 733, went back home, then went to and fro between Syria and
  Egypt. He had a special standing with al-Malik al-Kamil Sha'ban and with Khassakiyya emirs and turbaned officials.
  al-Muzaffar Hajji turned against him and sent him to Syria. He arrived in Damascus after Ramadan 748. He was owed
  over 200,000 dirhams in the Diwan al-khass for goods sold, went to Egypt several times to recover it, and got
  nothing. A letter from the dawadar of the viceroy of Syria had him expelled from Damascus in Jumada 749: his house
  was raided and his books taken. He went to Aleppo, came back to Damascus in 750 meaning to make the hajj, could not,
  and went back to Aleppo. Later in Damascus he held a teaching post at the Umayyad Mosque, then *sahabat diwan* of the
  mosque (the clerks there resented him and he lost it), then *istifa'* of the Diwan al-asra. At the end of 759 or the
  start of 760 he went to Egypt and stayed two years or more. Sultan al-Nasir Hasan sent him, against his will, as envoy
  to the king of Abyssinia, and he died on the way, at Qus, in Safar 762 (*A'yan* 3:520-523; *Wafi* 22:45-46; *Durar*
  4:126-127). Safadi adds that he was never raised to any rank in office, "a thing the learned are used to"
  (*A'yan* 3:524).
- **Ciphers.** He "wrote on al-mutarjam" (*Wafi* 22:45) and was outstanding in arithmetic, magic squares, letter lore,
  "hall al-mutarjam", riddles and puzzles (*A'yan* 3:522; *Durar* 4:127). Safadi met him several times and wrote him
  three verses telling a would-be cipher solver to study with Taj al-Din. The third verse pairs the two
  cryptologists' coin-names: «فلابن دنينير تصانيف ما لها نظير ولكن فاقها ابن الدريهم» (*A'yan* 3:522; *Wafi* 22:45).
  In his rhymed prose Safadi plays the same dinar/dirham pun again: had Ibn al-Dunaynir lived in his time he would
  own himself no match for Ibn al-Durayhim (*A'yan* 3:523-524). Ibn al-Durayhim wrote Safadi several riddles (*ahaji*)
  and answered Safadi's. Safadi collected them in his *Najm al-dayaji* (*A'yan* 3:523).
- **Works list** (copied by Safadi from Ibn al-Durayhim's own hand, *A'yan* 3:524-526) includes: *al-Mubham fi hall
  al-mutarjam*; *Mukhtasar al-Mubham fi hall al-mutarjam*; **مفتاح النوز في حل الرموز** (as in the OpenITI text,
  surely *Miftah al-kunuz fi hall al-rumuz*; the printed page should be checked for كنوز); *Ghayat al-i'jaz fi
  l-ahaji wa-l-alghaz*; and a run of "answers" and letters by place: *al-Ajwiba al-Qahiriyya*, *al-Ajwiba
  al-Halabiyya*, *al-Ajwiba al-Hamawiyya*, *al-Ajwiba al-Mawsiliyya*, *al-Tarassulat al-Shamiyya*, **al-Ajwiba
  al-Shamiyya** (unfinished). No title in the list is a book of solved cipher correspondence as such, unless it is one
  of the *Ajwiba* (see 2c). The noonpost article adds *Idah al-Mubham fi hall al-mutarjam* and a verse rule-book on
  cipher.
- **The Miftah.** The surviving treatise is titled *Miftah al-kunuz fi idah al-marmuz* (Wikipedia; Mrayati et al.). The
  noonpost summary gives its chapter 2, "the kinds of encipherment", as eight methods. The first is **al-maqlub**
  (reordering, i.e. transposition) and the second is **al-ibdal** (substitution by an agreed rule). The others are
  adding or dropping letters, devices, number values, letters as words, letters as categories, and invented shapes.
  This list is secondhand; it must be checked in Mrayati et al. vol. 3.
- **GTTAD 2026.** The article names Ibn al-Durayhim only as one of the classical treatise authors (p. 1014 and the
  conclusion). Its Table 1 lists him (as "Ibn Dürreyhim", d. 762) with the office "envoy (Abyssinia)" from *A'yan*
  3:521-528. It does not know LJS 51 or any book of solved correspondence. It does not quote the 38v.10 verse:
  NOTES.md l.78's "(Table 1 of the article)" means only that he is in Table 1.

## 2. The evidence in LJS 51, point by point

### (a) f. 102v: "my book Miftah al-kunuz fi hall al-marmuz", chapter of al-maqlub. **Strong for.**

Record 102v.11 (technique note, 102v l.6-10): Taj al-Din [son of?] Taj al-Din, nazir of Qatya, wrote the compiler a
cipher "one of the kinds of cipher mentioned in my book" «مفتاح الكنوز في حل المرموز» "from the chapter of
al-maqlub". He wrote it expecting the compiler to take it for "the chapter of al-ibdal", and the compiler solved it at
once. Three matches with Ibn al-Durayhim:
- The title. The modern title is *fi idah al-marmuz*. Safadi's list, from the author's own hand, has *fi hall
  al-rumuz*, and LJS 51 has *fi hall al-marmuz*. So LJS 51 agrees with the author's own form of the title against the
  later manuscript title. "Miftah al-kunuz" plus a cipher subject is not known for any other author.
- The chapters. al-maqlub and al-ibdal are the first two methods of the Miftah's chapter on kinds of encipherment
  (noonpost summary; to be checked in Mrayati vol. 3).
- The scene. A visitor tries to catch the author out with a method from the author's own book.

### (b) f. 38v.10: the verse praising Taj al-Din Ibn al-Durayhim. **For (weak to moderate); not against.**

Record 38v.10: the introduction (38v l.8-9) reads «وكتب لي الشيخ بدر الدين الجولاني المذكور من نظمه». So al-Jawlani
enciphered his own verse and sent it **to the compiler**, who solved it "at once, straight from the pen" (39r l.6). The
verse answers "one who asks me about a lord and son of a lord" and names "Taj al-Din, scion of al-Durayhim", gifted by
God with solving ciphers (translation_0078_0101.md). Sending a cipher that praises its own recipient is a courtly
compliment, and the recipient solves it and copies it into his book. The third person is the sender's, not the
compiler's. "Son of a lord" fits a man Safadi calls "son of al-Sahib Muwaffaq al-Din". If the compiler were someone
else, the verse would be a praise of a third party, sent to him for no stated reason.

### (c) ff. 49v-50r: Ibn Dunaynir or Ibn Durayhim. **Strong for.**

Record 49v.2: the compiler's own enciphered verse (heading فكتبت) to the master. He had thought himself unique in
cipher, and beside the master he is cotton; the last hemistich names the solver "Ibn Dunaynir". On 50r the master
proposes «من ابن دنينير». The compiler argues that «ابن دريهم» is better on two counts of style (tanazul; avoiding a
ta'sis in one verse only). The record's reading of 50r is not yet checked on the master. The pairing of Dunaynir with
Durayhim is the same coin pun Safadi used twice about Ibn al-Durayhim (above). A poet putting his own name into his
verse, and arguing for it, makes sense. A third party preferring an unrelated man's name in a verse about his own
modesty does not. Also f. 48r.8 / 49r l.8-13: the compiler says he thinks himself skilled in solving and «ولي تصانيف
فيه» ("I have written works on it"). Safadi lists at least three cipher works by Ibn al-Durayhim.

### (d) f. 61v-62r: the boast naming Ibn 'Adlan, Ibn Dunaynir, Ibn al-Rumi, al-Kindi. **Neutral to weakly for.**

Record 61r.3: had Ibn 'Adlan, Ibn Dunaynir, Ibn al-Rumi or al-Kindi seen the master's solution, they would have dropped
what they wrote in their books (cf. 43v: Ibn 'Adlan and al-Kindi). These are the classical cipher authors that
Ibn al-Durayhim's own treatise builds on (Wikipedia: he uses al-Kindi's and Ibn 'Adlan's methods; GTTAD p. 1014 lists
al-Kindi, Ibn 'Adlan, Ibn Dunaynir, Ibn al-Durayhim together). A cryptologist's reading list, but any learned
amateur could name them. The boast praises the patron, not the compiler. "Ibn al-Rumi" as a cipher author is not
identified (not checked).

### (e) The patron al-'Ala'i, the correspondents, places and offices. **Strong for, taken together.**

- **al-'Ala'i = 'Ala' al-Din Ibn Fadl Allah.** Record 62r.3 (62r l.6) dates the master's cipher «بعد وفاة اخيه الامير
  صلاح الدين». Safadi, *A'yan* 5:490-491 (entry Musa b. Yahya b. Fadl Allah; marker V05P490): the emir Salah al-Din
  Musa was the full brother of "al-qadi 'Ala' al-Din, head of the noble chancery diwans". He lived in his brother's
  house in Egypt and died in Safar 760, and 'Ala' al-Din grieved deeply for him. With 39r.10 (a verse praising "Ibn
  Fadl Allah" as the giver of bounty) and the chancery titles (al-maqarr al-sharif al-makhdumi al-'Ala'i), this makes
  the patron 'Ala' al-Din 'Ali b. Yahya Ibn Fadl Allah al-'Umari, kātib al-sirr in Cairo. The NOTES hypothesis is now
  backed by a source. 62r is then after Safar 760. Ibn al-Durayhim was in Egypt from late 759/early 760 for two
  years or more (*A'yan* 3:523).
- **Shihab al-Din Ahmad al-Mutarjim al-Baghdadi.** Safadi last saw him in Damascus in 745; he then went to Cairo
  (*Wafi*, NOTES.md). In LJS 51 he brings ciphers to the master and the compiler, and once sends one "from Siryaqus"
  (95r), near Cairo. This fits a Cairo setting, in either of Ibn al-Durayhim's Cairo periods (to 748, or 760-762).
- **"My return to Syria", allowances, and money owed.** 13v.10: a verse petition for customary allowances "on my
  return to Syria" (translation_0006_0053.md). 82r.9: the compiler held a receipt (*wusul*) on the Damascus treasury
  for a sum that was never paid, and sought a sultanic order to the governor of Gaza to settle it. Compare Safadi:
  more than 200,000 dirhams owed him in the Diwan al-khass, repeated trips to Egypt to recover it, without result
  (*A'yan* 3:522). The record is not the same claim (a Damascus receipt, not the Khass), but it is the same kind of
  trouble, and he goes back and forth between Cairo and Syria, as Safadi says.
- **Aleppo and Ibn al-Wardi.** 65r.5: the compiler wrote a cipher "of my verse in Aleppo". 71r.10/72r: he told a conceit
  to Zayn al-Din 'Umar Ibn al-Wardi in Aleppo, who then claimed the verses (translation_0126_0149.md). Ibn al-Durayhim
  lived in Aleppo from Jumada 749 to 750 and again after 750 (*A'yan* 3:522-523). Ibn al-Wardi died in Aleppo in
  749/1349 (general knowledge, not re-checked in this pass). The brief's "now dead" for Ibn al-Wardi was not found in
  the record text read here.
- **Damascus circle.** Correspondents in Damascus include Shams al-Din, khatib of al-Salihiyya (80r.9), Nasir al-Din
  [Ibn] Sallar (80v.9), and Burhan al-Din al-Qudsi al-Hanafi, imam of the malik al-umara' in Syria (77r.10). Amin
  al-Din 'Abd al-Wahhab al-Mizzi al-Hanafi was "today qadi of Hama" (71r.10, 75r.1). The work list has *al-Ajwiba
  al-Hamawiyya*, *al-Ajwiba al-Halabiyya*, *al-Tarassulat al-Shamiyya* and *al-Ajwiba al-Shamiyya*.
- **al-Ajwiba al-Shamiyya cited in LJS 51.** 80r.9 and 80v.9: a Damascus piece's "rendering is recorded in the Kitab
  al-Ajwiba al-Shamiyya", with "a qasida recorded in al-Ajwiba al-Shamiyya and its answer". Safadi's list of Ibn
  al-Durayhim's works has *al-Ajwiba al-Shamiyya* (unfinished). LJS 51 does not say "my book" here. It is still a
  rare title, and an "Answers" book on exchanges in Damascus is exactly the content described. **Strong for.**
- **The vizier Muwaffaq al-Din.** 69v.11/70r: the compiler sent a qasida "to the vizier Muwaffaq al-Din from Syria".
  Safadi, *A'yan* 2:677-678 (entry 'Abd Allah b. Sa'id al-Dawla): Muwaffaq al-Din became vizier of Egypt after Ibn
  Zunbur's arrest and held it until he died on 22 Rabi' II 755 (also Maqrizi, *Suluk*). Ibn al-Durayhim was in
  Damascus then, so the qasida fits c. 754-755. Ibn al-Durayhim's own father was also "al-Sahib Muwaffaq al-Din", but
  he died in the son's childhood, so he is not the man.
- **The vizierate of Syria.** 45v.13 and 46r.11 (45v l.10-12: «بعد ان طلبت لاباشر وزارة الشام فابيت ... واستعفيت»): the
  compiler was asked to take up the vizierate of Syria and refused it. He asked the master to have him excused, and it
  was settled before the sultan; "now the mukus are abolished" is also in the passage (record reading medium). **Not
  found in the biographies.** Safadi says Ibn al-Durayhim never rose in office. A refused post would not count as
  rising, and the posts he did hold in Damascus were financial (mosque diwan, *istifa'* of the Diwan al-asra), the
  line from which "wizarat al-Sham" (the Damascus finance post) was filled. Plausible, but not confirmed.
- **Alexandria.** 86r.9 records what a pupil of Ibn 'Ata' recited "at the port of Alexandria in the year thirty-odd
  and seven hundred". 99r.10 has verses the compiler improvised "when we entered Alexandria", addressed to al-Sahib Taqi
  al-Din Ibn Murajil, who was nazir of Damascus (Maqrizi, *Suluk*). The biographies do not mention Alexandria. They do
  put him in Egypt from 732/733 as a merchant, which fits a 730s Alexandria memory. **Neutral.**
- **Mosul.** LJS 51 quotes Ibn al-Majd al-Mawsili the preacher (61r), Sharaf al-Din Ibn al-Halawi al-Mawsili (76r) and
  a cipher from 'Abd al-Qadir b. Hasan [?] al-Mawsili, merchant (105v). Ibn al-Durayhim was a Mosul-born merchant.
  **Weak for.**
- **al-mamluk.** The compiler calls himself al-mamluk ("your servant") to the patron (93r, 75r). That is the usual
  chancery form of self-reference to a superior, not a sign of slave status. **Neutral.**
- **Shihab al-Din son of the late Zayn al-Din al-Safadi, the muwaqqi'** (101v-102r), **Taj al-Din nazir of Qatya**
  (102v), **Taj al-Din Ibn al-Malih** (100r): not identified (not checked). A chancery muwaqqi' fits the Ibn Fadl
  Allah setting.

## 3. Weighing

**For (strongest three)**
1. f. 102v: the compiler names "my book *Miftah al-kunuz fi hall al-marmuz*" with chapters al-maqlub and al-ibdal. Ibn
   al-Durayhim's own works list (Safadi, *A'yan* 3:525) has *Miftah [al-kunuz] fi hall al-rumuz*, the surviving treatise
   is *Miftah al-kunuz fi idah al-marmuz*, and its kinds of encipherment begin with al-maqlub and al-ibdal.
2. The patron is 'Ala' al-Din Ibn Fadl Allah: his brother the emir Salah al-Din Musa died in Safar 760 (*A'yan*
   5:490-491; LJS 51 62r). That date falls in Ibn al-Durayhim's last stay in Egypt (late 759/760-762), next to Cairo
   correspondents (al-Mutarjim, Siryaqus).
3. The Dunaynir/Durayhim argument (49v-50r) and the "Taj al-Din, scion of al-Durayhim" verse sent to the compiler
   (38v.10), read with Safadi's verses using the same pun about Ibn al-Durayhim. Add the *Ajwiba al-Shamiyya* (80r-80v)
   and the Aleppo/Damascus/Cairo movements, with unpaid treasury money, as in Safadi.

**Against or open (strongest three)**
1. The compiler never names himself in the passages read. The identification depends on the 102v self-reference and on
   the 50r argument, whose reading is "not yet checked on the master". Both should be read on the TIFF.
2. The refused vizierate of Syria (45v-46r) and the Alexandria visit (99r) are not in Safadi or Ibn Hajar, and Safadi
   says Ibn al-Durayhim never rose in office. This is silence, not contradiction, but it is the only place where the
   book claims more than the biographies support.
3. The chapter list of the *Miftah* is known here only from a popular secondary summary (noonpost), and Safadi's title
   is known from one OpenITI text with a typo (النوز). The book in LJS 51 has "fi hall al-marmuz", while the surviving
   treatise has "fi idah al-marmuz". The difference fits a revised or retitled work by the same author, but it should
   be checked against Mrayati et al. vol. 3 and the printed *A'yan*.

On the third-person praise: in 38v.10 the praise is al-Jawlani's verse, sent to the compiler, not his own words. In
49v-50r the name is the compiler's own self-naming in a verse, argued for in the first person. Neither is evidence
against.
