# William Lee to James Monroe, Paris, 2 February 1813 (Copy of Dispatch C): reading

### NARA RG 59, Despatches from US Consuls in Bordeaux, T164 roll 3, frames 0106-0107; the clerk's decipherment of 1813 on frames 0104-0105. Cipher: 41 passages, 1,097 numerals. Files: transcription_1813-02-02.txt, decryption_1813-02-02.txt, key.md. Printed from the clerk's copy in The Papers of James Monroe, vol. 6, MONR-01-06-02-0283.

The letter was deciphered at the State Department in 1813 on separate sheets, and the printed edition gives that
copy with the clerk's notes of error. Our reading is made from the figures, not from his copy. It agrees with him
wherever he read a passage, except where marked, and it reads seven passages that he could not.

## Marks

Lee's clear text is plain. Words he wrote in cipher are in italics. Bold italics mark cipher that the clerk who read
these letters at the State Department did not read. A dagger † after a passage means the clerk failed or left a gap
there. The sign ≠ means his wording differs from what the cipher gives. A double dagger ‡ means he never came to it.
The sign § means that our reading rests on a slip we suppose Lee made with his key, or on a doubtful division of
the figures: it is a proposal. A letter in square brackets inside a word is one that Lee's figures give wrongly or
leave out, and we supply it. A letter in angle brackets is one he wrote in excess, and we drop it. A word in square
brackets after a question mark is supplied or guessed from the sense. Three dots in square brackets mark figures
not read. A question mark in round brackets marks a name or word we read but doubt. The figures and the letters
they give, without any repair, are in the decryption file, passage by passage. The stops and capitals of the
passages in cipher are ours.

#### Examples: misfo[r]tunes (a letter supplied), o<f>f (a letter dropped), [?to] (a word guessed), [...] (figures not read), Bassagne(?) (a doubtful name). Notes on each passage: NOTES.md section 12.

## Reading

Copy of Dispatch C.

Paris Feby 2.d 1813.

Sir.

I have just learnt from Mrs B. that *Mr Petry* called yesterday & told her *that the Treaty* was to be *laid before
the Emperor* this morning, *who had not y[e]t seen it, and that he thought the whole of it would [b]e sanctioned*
***except that part relatin[g] to indem[n]i[t]ies***†§. He asked her if she thought *indemniti[e]s would be insisted
on by us, as in [t]hat* case there would be some *difficulty in coming to an arrangement*. She answered that it was
always the *opinion o[f] [he]r husband that wi<i>thout [i]ndemnities the P[r]esident wo[u]ld not sanction the
Treaty. The [D]uke Dalberg* with whom I conversed on this Subject last evening told me that the great difficulty
existing in the *mind of the Emperor wa[s] [th]e inde[m]nities*. He stated that *France was [n]ot now in a
situation* to Subscribe to them.

My own opinion is that had Mr Barlow lived he would not only have ***indem[ni]ties through***†§ but would have been able on his
arrival at Paris to have made *almost any T[r]eaty with France* — ***thei[r] game now is to spin out the
[ne]goti[a]tion[s] still farther***†§ — If they *su[c]ceed* in the *e[f]forts the nation is now making, they will
try to dictate terms to you* — If on the other hand *they do n[o]t succe[e]d*, you will have it *in your power [t]o
obtain justice* — Thus you will observe that the death of the Minister *proves favorab[l]e* ***[?to] their
policy***≠§; indeed I doubt if they *regret t[h]at eve[n]t* in any *point of view*. Such has been the impression
made here but that *unfortu[n]ate* and *injudicious* ***letter***†§ of ***his to Latro[b]e***†.

The *refusal* now to *admit the pr[i]nciple of i[n]demnity*≠ appears very extraordinary when I have every reason to
believe from *Mr [B]arlow’s* ***correspondence with the Dukes of Bassano and Dalberg***†§ together with his
*declarations* and *correspondence with me* on this Subject, that it was a *point [fu]lly [a]djusted be[t]ween
them*, and had it not been so he never would have undertaken that unfortunate journey to Wilna —

It is said they intend to offer you *commercial advantages* — which they *consider as equivalent to indemnities*,
but the *instability of all such arrangements* in the present *distra[c]ted state of Europe* and during our
*contest with Great Br[i]tain* will not perhaps be *satisfacto[r]y to the President*. It is possible that their
adhering so strictly *to the licence sys[t]e[m] is* with a view to *induce you to accede t[o] thes[e]
arrangements*≠, by thus proving that you can *have no commerce* here without great *sacrifices*.

I am not permitted for reasons which I have made known to you in a private letter to send you *a copy of [th]e
Treaty*, nor of *the ministers last* ***correspondence***≠§ with the *Duke of Bassano*.

I am Sir with the highest
respect. your obedient humble
Servant.

Wm Lee

[foot of p. 1] Honorable James Monroe, Secretary of State.

## Summary

Lee reports, from Ruth Barlow and from the Duke of Dalberg, that the treaty Joel Barlow had negotiated was to be laid
before Napoleon. The Emperor would sanction it, except the part on the indemnities the United States asked for, which
France is in no situation to grant. Lee expects the French to spin out the negotiations, and thinks Barlow's death
suits them. He finds the refusal of indemnities strange, since Barlow's correspondence with the Dukes of Bassano and
Dalberg had settled the point. France will offer commercial advantages instead. He cannot send a copy of the treaty,
nor of the minister's last correspondence with Bassano.
