# William Lee to the Secretary of State (James Monroe), Paris, 6 February 1813 (Second Copy): reading

### NARA RG 59, Despatches from US Consuls in Bordeaux, T164 roll 3, frames 0108-0110. Cipher: 74 passages, 1,456 numerals. Files: transcription_1813-02-06.txt, decryption_1813-02-06.txt, key.md. Not printed.

The letter was never deciphered in full. The clerk at the State Department wrote the letters of the key over the
figures and his reading under them in pencil on the first three pages, and stopped on the third. The last
thirty-two passages were never read until now.

## Marks

Lee's clear text is plain. Words he wrote in cipher are in italics. Bold italics mark cipher that the clerk who read
these letters at the State Department did not read. A dagger † after a passage means the clerk failed or left a gap
there. The sign ≠ means his wording differs from what the cipher gives. A double dagger ‡ means he never came to it.
The sign § means that our reading rests on a slip we suppose Lee made with his key, or on a doubtful division of
the figures: it is a proposal. A letter in square brackets inside a word is one that Lee's figures give wrongly or
leave out, and we supply it. A letter in angle brackets is one he wrote in excess, and we drop it. A word in square
brackets after a question mark is supplied or guessed from the sense. Three dots in square brackets mark figures
not read. A question mark in round brackets marks a name or word we read but doubt. The figures and the letters
they give, without any repair, are in the decryption file, passage by passage. The stops and capitals of the
passages in cipher are ours.

#### Examples: misfo[r]tunes (a letter supplied), o<f>f (a letter dropped), [?to] (a word guessed), [...] (figures not read), Bassagne(?) (a doubtful name). Notes on each passage: NOTES.md section 12.

## Reading

Second Copy

Hotel of the Legation Paris<br>
Feby 6th. 1813.

Sir,

For the *pur[p]oses of [c]ommerc[e]* ***I established***†§ with C. D. S. S. L and C. W. ***a [c]ipher, a copy***†§ of which I have taken means to put *in your hand*s.

The events in the north are highly *distressing*. — I have not seen of late any English papers, but it is scarcely
possible for them to *exaggerate [t]he misfo[r]tunes of the grand army*. — It has been *totally destroyed*. The best
*officers* have either been *taken or have died off by* the *severity o<f>f the climate*. — *History* does not
furnish ***[a]n account of such a disastr<c>ous campaign***†.

The effects of this *misfortune* upon the *nation is no[t] to be descr[i]bed* — a General *discontent prevails*
which *threatens [t]he destru[c]tion* of the *Government*. — Nothing but the general impression among the leading
characters, that it is *[n]ec[e]ssary to [s]upport the Emperor to preserve* the *country can save him*, and even with
the pains taken to disseminate this *patriot sen[t]iment*≠ it is *doubtful if the[y] succeed*.

Austria, who it is *considered is less exh[a]usted than any of the nat[i]ons on the continent,* ***it is feared***†§
will *profit of this state [o]f things to strike [a] decisive b[l]ow*. The *Arch Duke Char[l]es* and *his party* wish
it — The *[p]ar[t]y of the Prince Swartzenb[o]urg*. The Emperor from his attachment to his daughter is in favor
***[?of an armed neutrality]***†§ and through him it is said *ne[g]otiatio[n]s* ***for a g[e]neral [p]eace are operat[i]ng***†.

The Prussian Monarch has *left his capital fearing*≠ the ***app[r]oach of the Russian[s]***†. His dominions are
*[c]ompletely exhauste[d]* and *Poland* is in a *distracted state* from the part they have taken, having *lost all
confidence in Napoleon*.

The *King of Naples* has quit *the remna[n]t o[f] the grand army* in *disgust* having it is said committed great
errors, and the *Vicero[y] has* taken *the command* and has we are told at this moment ***[hi]s headquarters at
Posen***†§. Reports say that the Russians *have entere[d] Berl[i]n*.

In the midst of these *disasters* and of the *great* ***depression of the nation***†§ a conscription of three
hundred and fifty thousand men has been called for, and ***recourse***‡ has been had ***to forced donations***‡ of
***men and money***‡ — Fifty thousand horse are to be raised by each town furnishing its quota, in proportion to its
population and wealth, thus Paris furnishes five hundred Lyons one hundred & twenty Bordeaux Eighty and so on.

By this means a considerable army will soon be collected ***but it is the last effort of the nation***‡ — We must
therefore look for something very decisive in the course of the Summer months.

The papers I have sent you will give you the account of the formation of the Regency, and furnish you with most of
the addresses to the throne from different parts of the Empire These addresses may ***have an eff[e]ct abroad [?but
on]***‡§ The nation they have ***none, for the people know***‡ How they have ***been produced***‡. The Concordat
recently concluded with the Pope ***would have made at any other moment***‡ a good impression, ***but at this time
i[t] is not thought of, while***‡§ The ***[d]isco[nte]nts in some of the departments***‡§ are ***magnifi[e]d***‡ and
the ***exhausted stat[e] of the fina[n]ces***‡ lead to a ***dread***‡ of an ***emprunt forcé***‡.

These, Sir, perhaps you will say, are the ***dark shades***‡ of the ***pict[u]re***‡ yet I do not see how I can
***give it relief***‡.

***Terms of peace***‡ it is said have been ***offered to England if she will abandon Russia***‡ — ***A Mr de
Bassagne(?)***‡§ has been ***sent to London***‡ with ***proposals***‡ to that effect.

The ***Duk[e] Dalberg told me***‡ an ***answer***‡ to the ***propositions for peace***‡ was ***shortly expected***‡ —
When I questioned him to learn if it was from ***proposals mad[e] to Russia o[r] England***‡ he waved the answer — He
told me: Austria would remain firm on the principle of ***an armed neutrality, negotiating as mediatrix***‡. But if
not, ***successful in thes[e] negoti[a]tio[n]s***‡ She would then join Napoleon, with one hundred thousand men —
Perhaps to be indemnified ***at the expense of Poland***‡.

Part of the new levies are to be distributed about in the Garrisons, and military positions and the veterans are to
be drawn from thence, thus one hundred & twenty thousand old troops will be joined to part of the new ones, which
when added to the remnant of the Grand army, will make it is said an army of Five hundred thousand men, added to
which all the immense ordinance, prepared for the Marine, and all the Engineers with the marine soldiers, and
Cannoniers of that Department have it is reported, been ordered to march to the North.

With this force it is thought Napoleon will retrieve the losses occasioned by the severity of the climate in Russia
and still be master of Europe — ***on this r[e]sts th[e] hopes of his court***‡ — ***his crown and [h]is fame***‡§.

I am Sir, with great respect<br>
Your very humble Servant<br>
Wm Lee

The Secretary of State

## Summary

Lee reports the ruin of the Grand Army in Russia and its effect on France. There is general discontent; a new
conscription of three hundred and fifty thousand men has been called, with forced gifts of men and money, which he
calls the last effort of the nation. The addresses to the throne impress no one at home, the departments are restless,
and people dread a forced loan. Austria is less exhausted than the other powers and may strike; her Emperor favours an
armed neutrality and talks of peace. The King of Prussia has left his capital as the Russians come on, the King of
Naples has quit the army, and the Viceroy commands at Posen. Terms of peace are said to have been offered to England
if she will abandon Russia. Dalberg tells Lee that Austria will stay neutral and offer to mediate, or else join
Napoleon with a hundred thousand men in return for a share of Poland. On the coming campaign rest the hopes of his
court, his crown and his fame.
