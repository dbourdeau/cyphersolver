# William Lee to James Monroe, Paris, 1 February 1813 (private): reading

### NARA RG 59, Despatches from US Consuls in Bordeaux, T164 roll 3, frames 0099-0102, docket on 0103. Cipher: 8 passages, 110 numerals. Files: transcription_1813-02-01.txt, decryption_1813-02-01.txt, key.md. No decipherment of 1813 known; not printed.

The letter is in Lee's hand, dated from the Hotel of the Legation in Paris and marked private. It runs to seven
pages, nearly all in clear, with eight short passages in his cipher. No decipherment by the State Department clerk
was found on the film. The whole letter is given here, with the docket. It
is not the letter of the same date, wholly in clear, in which Lee regrets that he has not the cipher.

## Marks

Lee's clear text is plain. Words he wrote in cipher are in italics. Bold italics mark cipher that the clerk who read
these letters at the State Department did not read. A dagger † after a passage means the clerk failed or left a gap
there. The sign ≠ means his wording differs from what the cipher gives. A double dagger ‡ means he never came to it.
The sign § means that our reading rests on a slip we suppose Lee made with his key, or on a doubtful division of
the figures: it is a proposal. A letter in square brackets inside a word is one that Lee's figures give wrongly or
leave out, and we supply it. A letter in angle brackets is one he wrote in excess, and we drop it. A word in square
brackets after a question mark is supplied or guessed from the sense. Three dots in square brackets mark figures
not read. A question mark in round brackets marks a name or word we read but doubt. The figures and the letters
they give, without any repair, are in the decryption file, passage by passage. The stops and capitals of the
passages in cipher are ours.

#### Examples: misfo[r]tunes (a letter supplied), o<f>f (a letter dropped), [?to] (a word guessed), [...] (figures not read), Bassagne(?) (a doubtful name). Notes on each passage: NOTES.md section 12.

## Reading

private.

Hotel of the Legation Paris
February 1st 1813.

Sir

I have my consular affairs to attend to, which give me(?) Some occupation, my Commercial affairs which are of
some Moment to me, the private affairs of my deceased friend to arrange, as no one else is acquainted with them,
and the affairs of the Legation to put in Some order. I shall therefore run you this letter off àla hate, mixing
private affairs with public ones, leaving you to select such as shall appear interesting to the President,
calculating on your indulgence and the friendship you have always honored me with on which be assured no one could
set a higher value.

I Shall begin with Mr Warden. This Gentleman has assumed the title of Consul General I am told, at the Exterior
Relations, by virtue of a Commission which he received as such from Genl Armstrong. This was necessary to his
purpose, in order to take the precedence of myself and other Consuls whose Commissions are older than his, and who
according to the usages of this Government, had an antecedent right to him to take charge of our affairs — But
Sir, all usages, all right has been done away by the intrigues of the *Irish Brigade* and others on whom they have
an immediate influence to favor the views of their champion, who by established rules can have no political or
Diplomatic Character, while a Secretary of Legation, acting Secretary of Legation, private Secretary, or in short
any one attached to the Legation is present.

In Stating this to the Duke of Bassano, he shugged his Shoulders and Said: Que voulez vous? "there was no one
present, and we were forced to Consent that Mr Warden Should a[c]t," But, Sir, the absence of the Secretaries was
not the Motive for the Duke's charging Mr Warden with the Communications to be made to & from this Government. He
knows that gentleman is totally unacquainted with the special & current affairs of the Legation, and that sometime
would be necessary for him to get installed — The darling object therefore of procrastination could be better
answered through him, than through those who are acquainted with every Stage of the Negotiation, and who though
they could not to meddle with the special business of the Minister, might nevertheless, viewing the urgency of the
case, press this Government to make such Communications to you on the Subject as might embarrass them. This is my
firm belief, I may be wrong, but the Correspondence of Mr Warden, the last dispatches of the late Minister, and
what Mr Serurier may state to you, will enable you to judge if I am correct or not in this opinion.

The measures which Mr Warden has adopted to gratify his ambition, the imperious manner with which he has treated
those respectable americans, who have Conversed with him on this Subject, his refusing to sign the address
presented to Mrs Barlow and to wear, with all the americans, Mourning on this mournful occasion, when he ought to
have reverenced his own Government by respecting the office if not the man, together with the irritating letter he
has had the impudence to write to the poor disconsolate widow, has drawn upon him the Severe animadversions of all
the Americans here who love their Country & respect the memory of the Deceased.

The Moment the Consul for Paris heard of the death of the Minister, he Should have presented himself at this Hotel,
and have put the Seals of the Consulate on the Cabinet of the deceased until the Secretary of Legation Should
arrive to take charge of the publick papers, and Mrs(?) Barlow or the legal heir Should be in a situation to take
charge of the private ones. This was the extent of his powers, and this Mrs Barlow says she would not have refused
him permission to do; her own security and peace of mind demanded it.

Instead of pursuing this line of conduct, which alone could have placed during the confusion of the family, the
papers of the Legation in perfect security, he assumes instantly functions which do not belong to him, to the
injury of the Government of the U States, and the prejudice of those legally authorised to exercise them by
getting, it is said, the *Marshal Serrurier* (who is a perfect old woman, and in no other case would have any
influence whatever) to persuade the Duke of Bassano, on the 16th January, the very day after he heard of the death
of the Minister, to acknowledge him "as the only proper organ of our Government at Paris, to supply the vacancy
left by the death of the Minister." He then demands of Mrs Barlow the Seals, Cipher & papers of the Legation, and
asks "by what authority she opens & mutilates the dispatches of our Government," threatening at the same time to
take Care," that in future no dispatches Shall fall into her hands to prevent the Secrets of the Government being
known to improper persons." — This you must acknowledge, Sir is very singular language for the Consul of the U
States to use on this occasion, towards those who have reason to suppose, they enjoy the Confidence of the
President.

Will you have us Sir, transport the papers and affairs of the Legation from a privileged house and lodge them in a
house that is not privileged? will you have us place the Correspondence of the late Minister in the hands of
Strangers? (and in this instance Strangers to our Country) for neither Mr Warden or his Secretary have been long
enough in the U States to have been regularly and legally — Naturalized — Shall we take it from those with whom it
has been deposited, who enjoyed the Confidence of the deceased & deposit it with those who did not enjoy his
Confidence, and who ought not to be charged with his secrets; with one who enjoys no Consideration and who, though
at the head of our Commerce in this Country, [?is] as totally ignorant of its interests as he is of the mere forms
of his office. The Secretary of Legation if he consented such an Arrangement ought never again to shew his face in
his Country.

I cannot Sir, sufficiently Caution you against the recommendations which this man's friends will send you from
this Country in his favor, and will induce his friends in ours to address you. This sort of testimony is so easily
procured by those who are Strangers to delicacy that they ought to have no weight. He has taken infinite pains to
injure the Minister by representing that unfortunate & injudicious letter to *Latrobe* in the worst colors, and what
is more, he and his friends have been drinking all winter toasts to the *success of Clinton* and their friend
*Emmet*. He represents that the *United Irishmen* in the U States form *a distinct party*, that their influence is
so great *the President dares not offend*§. Sentiments unbecoming him as an officer of our Government, particularly
one who has so recently sought an asylum among us.

In my letter of the 30th ulto. I mentioned Mr Thos Barlow having announced himself to the Duke of Bassano as
Secretary of Legation, by Stating to him he held a Commission as such from the President, and by calling to his
recollection his uncle's letter on the subject, and his having presented him to His Excellency in that quality at
Wilna.

To this letter we have not as yet received an answer; but Mr Petry called this morning to say that Mr Barlow's
application to the Duke had come too late, Mr Warden having been chosen for the purposes therein mentioned. He
observed that had Mr Barlow signified his wishes on his first arrival from Wilna he would have been received and
accredited. Mr Barlow, (who is not deficient in intellect and prudence) very properly answered, that the reason he
did not immediately on his return, take charge of the affairs of the Legation, was his finding Mr Warden had been
designated by the Duke for that purpose; that if Mr Warden's having been named by the Duke was the only reason for
not accrediting him at this moment, the same reason existed on the 19th January, the day of his arrival as that
Gentleman had publicly notified his acceptation by the Minister of foreign affairs on the 15th of January.

We know from this visit of Mr Petry what it is to be the answer of the Duke; when it arrives, we shall answer it
calmly & respectfully, and here the business must rest until we hear from you. —

As this letter has been spun to a greater length than I intended, and as I have not time to Condense it, I propose
touching on other matters in a Separate one.

If I have been too severe against Mr Warden in this, you must attribute it to my zeal for the public Service and
for the memory of the "best man I ever knew and the dearest friend I ever had."

I have Sir, with the highest respect & attachment, the honor to remain

Your devoted
humble Servant
Wm Lee

[foot of p. 1] Honble James Monroe, Secy of State

## Summary

Lee, in Paris after the death of Joel Barlow, writes privately to Monroe against David Bailie Warden, the consul at
Paris, who has taken charge of American affairs. In cipher he names the Irish Brigade and Marshal Serrurier as the
means by which Warden got himself recognised. Warden has blackened Barlow's letter to Latrobe, drunk toasts to the
success of Clinton and to Emmet, and claims that the United Irishmen form a distinct party in the United States, so
strong that the President dares not offend them.
