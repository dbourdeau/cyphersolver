**[view 0380, f. 307]** (Heading:) Answer to the letter from Vienna. At the camp before Stralsund, 20 November 1715 ("9bre"; first transcribed "8bre", see NOTES.md).
I have seen by yours of the 5th of this month … a line of reasoning that was put to you on the interest of the allies of the
North with regard to the King of Sweden. There is in truth much that is solid in it … on a closer look it appears quite plainly
that it is only in so far as the King of Sweden finds his account in it. But let that pass, provided that we too really find our
convenience in it. You answered him very well … or, should the occasion arise, you may naturally bring this talk up again, and I
will tell you that it is quite certain that

**[view 0381, f. 307v]** the King of Sweden will infallibly be dislodged altogether from Germany, where so far nothing is left
to him but Stralsund and Wismar. Before Stralsund the attack has been [pushed] so far that, unless the King of Sweden wishes to
await the last extremity, I mean a general assault, he will draw no other advantage from it than the satisfaction of having
remained master of it a few days longer <400>. Since the island of Poel was taken without any loss on the [side] of the allies
of the North, Wismar is so closely pressed by land and by sea that, if only those who keep that place blockaded do not slacken
their attention, it cannot

**[view 0381, f. 308]** avoid falling this very winter, should it be needed to besiege it in form. From this situation of the
King of Sweden's affairs you can well see that this <183> can no longer prevent his being, in a short time, entirely dislodged
from the Empire or from the duchy of Zweibrücken, so that [he] can hardly trouble the allies of the North. The harm that comes
of it can only be regarded as a fruit of His Swedish Majesty's own will, since neither the good disposition of the allies of the
North for the restoration of peace, nor the exhortations of the Emperor and of his <c?eill> friends for that same peace and for
the restoration of tranquillity and rest in the Empire, nor the sad

**[view 0382, f. 308v]** situation <251> of his affairs compared with those of his enemies, the deplorable state of his people,
the exhaustion of his own forces, in men as in money as in the other needs for continuing a war, with only <aiparene> … against so
many enemies, each of whom is now able by himself to sustain the war against him, above all now that he will soon be brought so
low that a foreign power will hardly wish to embark with him, unless it were the Turk, whose help is so distant that he cannot
set his affairs right; finally,

**[view 0382, f. 309]** experience itself has not been able to persuade this [prince] that by going on with the war he would
make his condition ever worse. Could one see his state otherwise than as a fruit of his own will? It is also quite certain that
the King (Augustus II) is well disposed to peace, but to a general peace, by no means a separate one, and such as the
[conjuncture] can reasonably demand, and such also as it must be to secure <270> <aue> [the] foundation, the rest that is its
aim. I will tell you further that the King is by no means [minded] to lend his hand to a total ruin of the Crown of Sweden, and
the King has gone so far in this sentiment that he has even

**[view 0383, f. 309v]** stipulated it in the treaties with his allies. It seems to me that this shows well enough his good
dispositions for peace, provided it is not of a nature to lay the foundation of a new war, but that one finds in it all the
security one can reasonably desire. Besides, the reasoning put to you seems to me to resemble in some manner the letter that
Monsieur the Count of Croissy wrote at the beginning of this campaign, in which he set out the firmness of the King of Sweden and
the drawbacks to which the allies were going to expose themselves by resolving to attack this [prince] in Stralsund and Rügen
<4008> <10009> <680>.

**[view 0383, f. 310]** It is true that in the reasoning put to you there is more moderation; but, depicting to us what we
would have to fear from this prince in Poland, and depicting him in such a way that we should fear him more than any of the other
powers making war on him, and giving us at the same time such an idea of this [prince]'s firmness in pursuing his designs as must
persuade us that we have much to fear from him, could one reasonably hope that such insinuations might have any other effect than
the letter of Monsieur de Croissy, which produced only a closer bond among the allies and an increase in the King of Sweden's
enemies, to

**[view 0384, f. 310v]** bring him to such a peace as the conjuncture can reasonably demand? And since the King of Sweden has
made it a rule to reject [it], should not these unhappy consequences that we are made to foresee lead us to embrace the surest
means of putting ourselves under cover from what we are meant to fear: the remnants that the King of Sweden could gather to go
back again to Poland and put himself at the head of our rebels? For, as the man who spoke to you so confidentially assures you,
he would find two thirds of them for him. And what

**[view 0384, f. 311]** means could be surer and more effective than to destroy, while we can, these remnants before they can
serve to strengthen the King of Sweden in a firmness as pernicious to him as to us? All that is left of these remnants we are
meant to fear, and that was not destroyed on the island of Usedom, in the entrenchment taken from him here before Stralsund and
on the island of Rügen, is now with the King of Sweden's own person in the town of Stralsund,

**[view 0385, f. 311v]** which on the land side is entirely enclosed, and will be no less so by sea since the taking of Rügen,
if the Danes, as they assure us, have sent prams and frigates to stop up on both sides the way out of Stralsund by water. In this
situation, where the [remnants] of the King of Sweden's forces and his own person, whose return to Poland [we] [must] fear, are
shut up in Stralsund, ought we not to forestall the harm with which, it is claimed, we are threatened

**[view 0385, f. 312]** and try to destroy these remnants while it is in our power to do so? What is left in Wismar can no longer
escape and must succumb, as I said above. This situation in which the King of Sweden now finds himself leaves him no course to
take but to surrender the place to us and accept peace, or to await a general assault and expose himself to being massacred with
all his men or taken prisoner. This will make me take

**[view 0386, f. 312v]** the course of writing to General Dücker as soon as the greater part of our cannon and mortars have been
brought to the batteries; above all as I find myself so close to the salient angle of the counterscarp that at the latest I shall
be able to make myself master [of the place] in <357> or <358> times <365> <358> hours, and make all the cannon and mortars play
at once. And before coming to that, I will try whether, by the representations I shall make to the said general in my letter, he
might touch the heart of his King, so that, by taking the resolution

**[view 0386, f. 313]** to surrender the town and to come to a general peace, this prince may turn away the harm that he can no
longer avoid otherwise. Is not acting so to give a new and unquestionable mark of the King's good intention for peace? I will
write again today to Monsieur the Count Virmond, minister of the Emperor, to ask him to come to me without delay, so that, if the
King of Sweden, through the pitiful state he is in, through the compassion he owes his people and through my representations,
lets himself be bent to treat of peace, this may still be done under the

**[view 0387, f. 313v]** direction and by the mediation of the Emperor. I hope this will further convince <68> of the regard
that the King has on all occasions for the glory and interests of His Imperial Majesty. I cannot but be persuaded that the
Imperial Court will be grateful to him for it, and that <échangée/engagée> it will espouse on all occasions the interests … with
the same eagerness. There, my dear wife, is what to answer to those who hold such talk to you … without affectation. I am working,
not without hope of success, to bring

**[view 0387, f. 314]** things here to such a point that the operations of the war can no longer give us matter for occupation.
