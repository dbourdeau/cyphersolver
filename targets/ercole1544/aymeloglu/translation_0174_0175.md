# Translation of cc. 174r-175v, Ercole Gonzaga to Ferrante Gonzaga, Mantua, 9 January 1544

Same blocks and the same holes as `reading_0174_0175.md`.

EN[D01-D07]: As Your Excellency may have seen from the many reports I have given you from time to time, I have always taken great pains to learn the Pope's dealings with France, at my own expense and trouble and at manifest danger to those who have served me in it; and I did it all so as to inform His Majesty and his ministers, to whom it seems to me this diligence of mine has always been little welcome; indeed they have held that I am being duped by whoever gives me the reports.

EN[D07-D13]: I have been unable to make out why. Perhaps I did not say what pleased them, or perhaps I went too far into [?particulars], such that anyone who wanted to know them would almost need to have seen the very letters they write to one another, and that this cost me credit. And in truth I too have at times doubted whether the man who informs me is not being duped by whoever reports to him,

EN[D13-D17]: and for that reason I have many times held back from writing [...] things, and I have always wished for a chance to make sure of it. That chance now offers itself through Your Excellency, because my correspondent, meaning to tell me of a certain negotiation between the Pope and France, first gives me account of one that has passed between the Pope and the Emperor,

EN[D17-E02]: and tells me that the Pope, in order to show that if he declares himself French he will do it with a legitimate excuse, has begun to complain of the Emperor, and that some days ago he wrote to His Majesty protesting that in the Diet, as is commonly believed, he is the head and it lies with him to make and unmake it, and that he sees him united with Lutherans to the destruction of the Church, without regard for God or for his own honour, and that he does all this for private interest; and he complains that the promises made at Busseto are not kept to him, and bids him take care not to provoke the wrath of God, since against his own faithful he makes use of his greatest enemies, who are Lutherans and the king of England.

EN[E02-E10]: To that letter, he writes me, the Emperor answered in few words and good ones: that he is a Christian, and that to increase the faith of Christ he is ready to risk all his kingdoms and his own life too, as was seen from what he began to carry out; but that Christians have always harassed him, as they would in future if he tried to do it again; [so that] the quarrels between the king and himself must first be settled, and to come out of them with his honour [...] he will use every means he can have and do whatever suits him;

EN[E10-E13]: meaning that he cares little what His Holiness says about the Diet and England; and adding that he wonders at His Holiness, who he hears is sending Farnese to those parts, for it is effort thrown away and he will come back with little honour, because he wants no peace with the king and means to finish it in any case, without interposing in anything else.

EN[E14-E20]: Now if this is true, I think Your Excellency will easily know it and can tell me plainly whether I am being duped or not; I beg you as much as I can to let me know. I do not ask you to tell me His Majesty's secrets, or whether the report of these letters is true, but to write whether you think I should persevere in this office and whether you believe I am duped; for if this were of no service to His Majesty I would gladly give it up, since besides the expense there is the danger to many people who, if by ill luck it were discovered, would fare very badly.

EN[E20-F02]: [So] I beg you as much as I can to tell me your opinion, so that I know whether I should keep the dealings going. And so that you can better say, I shall continue what I began, telling you why he tells me what passed between the Pope and the Emperor: he writes that His Holiness has sent the copies of the letters, his own and the Emperor's, to the king, telling him that now he is clear about the Emperor's mind he should make ready for the new season and send an army into Italy, where he is sure he can gain more than at Perpignan or in Flanders, especially as he has the Turkish fleet, the fear of which counts for a great deal.

EN[F03-F09]: These letters, he says, were most welcome to the king, who answered that he will bear himself so as to make known to the whole world, very soon, how great his greatness and power are, and that he will send to raise troops at Mirandola. This man has always written me that the Pope's mind, inclination and heart are turned to the king [...], and that he deals doubly with the Emperor, and that Farnese has gone meaning to make the marriage alliance and to come to a secret agreement with the king,

EN[F09-F17]: whom the Pope believes he can persuade by argument to be content that he take Milan with his good grace, promising to give him the way in and his help in the affairs of Tuscany and of the Kingdom; and that if Farnese can settle this with the king he will then come to the Emperor and accept Milan for a payment of money, on whatever conditions he chooses to give it. The Pope will certainly try first to have it free, but if he cannot he will take it on any terms, hoping to free it later with the help of France. These, he tells me, are the Pope's designs, which I do not wholly believe,

EN[F17-F22]: but I do believe he has dealings as close with the king as perhaps with the Emperor, as you will see from the copy of a letter from Pier Luigi's wife written to her brother, which came into my hands by a very good route and will be with this, and also a few reports from France.

EN[F22-G07]: As for what will be said in Rome about your visit to England, my informant writes that the Pope has heard you were sent to explain the war. You will tell the king that if the Emperor had had money he would have continued it. He asks for money and troops, or at least money, since he will provide the troops himself, hoping for a quick victory with little loss. Your visit also concerns a marriage. His Holiness wrote all this to the French king, urging him to bring the war into Italy, where he can gain much. He half-promises to declare for him and take off the mask if the king gains a foothold. The Emperor hints at the need for action [...] things cannot go on this way.

EN[G07-G17]: Salviati writes that the French seized Cardinal de La Baume’s holdings in Savoy. The marquis was then instructed to sequester the revenues in the State of Milan of cardinals serving the French king. Salviati asked me, since I knew his mind, to see that he was not treated as a partisan. I wrote the marquis about the dealings I had through Your Excellency on Salviati’s behalf. He replied that the sequestration had already been ordered when my letter arrived, but he would do something to please me. To help him do so, I have written again with what you told me before leaving for England.

EN[G17-G26]: I thought I should tell you so that, if you hear this discussed, you can help Salviati as needed. I cannot say how much he owes Granvelle and Your Excellency for interceding with His Majesty. He is writing to thank Granvelle, and on his behalf I thank you. I ask you both to continue the good work. In my judgment he deserves it as much as any lord I know. I have told the count of Mirandola what you wrote about his affairs. He has sent word of the great losses he has suffered and the costs he has incurred because [...] [the Modena sheet ends here].
