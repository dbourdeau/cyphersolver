# Ferdinand I to Castaldo, Ebersdorf, 18 October 1552: English translation of the cipher passages

Translated here from `latin_18oct.txt`. `[...]` marks a clear passage between cipher passages. Codes: [Lippa],
[Transylvania], [the army], [the Moldavian], [the Sultan].

... as to the unruliness of those troops of ours, which riot at the prompting and under the leadership chiefly of their
captains, not only the foot but also the horse, and especially the heavy cavalry of Fabian von Schönaich, who, as you
complain again, seek by right or wrong a chance to slip away from there: this we learned with great annoyance, for we
easily see that it does nothing to keep those loyal subjects of ours in good will, loyalty and duty towards us, but is
rather wholly contrary to it [...] by flight or departure. [...]

[With the] money and cloth now on the way, [the troops] will keep quiet for a while and at once become gentler, steadier
and readier in our service. [...] [We shall] arrange and send there at the first opportunity another sum of money for the
pay of our said soldiers, to remove, as far as possible, all the causes of the difficulties and troubles of every kind
that seem to flow from the lack of money and the delay of pay. This will be far easier for us in future than until now,
since the common penny granted us by the estates and orders of the Holy Roman Empire is now being paid in and collected
everywhere under the conclusion of the recent treaty of Passau; indeed it has in large part already been counted out or
deposited in several places, and we have sent our commissaries to various places to receive it, not doubting that in a
few days we shall have here a large sum with which our soldiers can be paid. [...]

[...] the present [Moldavian voivode] [...] his friendship [...] his envoys whom he sent to you, [that] the [Moldavian]
[...] to remove the Wallachian from the scene as well [...] what will come of the peace negotiation that the same
[Moldavian] has in hand, though it matters greatly to that kingdom of ours to have both those provinces friendly.

We hope moreover that the other place, nearer [Lippa], may in this way be recovered into our hands, and that so much
trouble may be made by you there for the enemy who are at [Lippa] that they are forced to call for help on the rest of
the [army]. For we see no better way to bring help to Agria or to draw the enemy off from it, than if you were to feign
some move towards [Lippa]; just as on this side the illustrious Duke of Saxony, Maurice the Elector, leaves no stone
unturned to divert the Turks from Agria, and since he may at this time be inferior to them in numbers and strength, so
that he cannot drive them off by force without danger, he uses art and cunning for it and feigns an expedition against
some place of the Turks. For we hardly believe that the Turks, with the pride they have drawn from this year's
successes, will allow any of the places they lately took to be harassed, still less taken from them. And if they
decide to bring help on two sides, they cannot do it without raising the siege of Agria, because they will have to
divide their [army] into two or more columns and so split their forces. Yet we hope that they have already given up
that siege, or will soon, and that Agria will be freed with God's help, because a spy of ours reported to us, who had
been led all round the enemy camp before Agria by another Turk, also a spy of ours, that the plague rages among them so
that every day from three hundred to four hundred [and five hundred men die] [...] [as was confirmed by] other spies.

As to what you write, that you are being forced, willing or not, to leave the kingdom with some part of the troops for
the relief of Agria: to us this does not seem advisable, for many reasons, but chiefly because those subjects would take
such a departure of yours very differently, and it would bring nothing but many troubles on our affairs. We therefore
graciously charge you to remain there with those troops of ours, who, we do not doubt, will be obedient and ready as
soon as they learn that money and cloth for the relief of their poverty are on the way; and that you continue to apply
yourself to the defence of that kingdom of ours, whose preservation rests at present chiefly on your shoulders, and
act steadily and bravely as you have done so far. For we shall see to it that another sum of money is sent there as soon
as can be, and that nothing is neglected on our part that will serve to remove and put an end to the troubles and
difficulties in which you find yourselves there. [...] but also these evils which press on you now. [...]

And because we are persuaded that, heeding these warnings of ours, you will stay there in [Transylvania], we did not
think it worth while to write anything on this matter to our Voivode Andrew Báthory, who, as we fear, could not be held
there with so small a body of soldiers by any letters of ours [...] above all by the reply and decision of the [Sultan].

But to come down to some particulars, and so that you may know our mind and will the more exactly, we want and
graciously charge you to strive in handling this business with all diligence and zeal and by every means, way and
method, that [the Sultan] agree that the tribute formerly paid from that kingdom of ours be paid to him in our name;
[...] that the tribute be offered and paid in the name of the three nations, but so that they remain under our
allegiance. But if neither of these is granted, then the loyal subjects [are by no means to let themselves be brought
to] the payment of tribute; [for unless] the loyalty and obedience of the said kingdom remain safe [to us], that peace
to be made with the Turks will by no means be approved or ratified by us.
