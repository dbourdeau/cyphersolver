# Castaldo 1552: Ferdinand I to Giovanni Battista Castaldo, Vienna, 15 October 1552 (catalogue 1332)

## Document

Milan, Veneranda Biblioteca Ambrosiana, G 275 inf., unit 9, ff. 34r–35v (letter ff. 34r–36v, sealed); OPAC: “Lettera a Giovanni Battista Castaldo con testo cifrato, Vienna 15 ottobre 1552”.
15 October 1552 (“Viennae, die xv mensis Octobris anno Domini MDLII”). Vienna → Transylvania (Castaldo’s camp).
Sender: Ferdinand I of Habsburg, King of the Romans, of Hungary and of Bohemia (countersigned by vice-chancellor Jonas and secretary Sinckmoser). Recipient: Giovanni Battista Castaldo, marquis of Cassano, commander of the royal troops in Transylvania.
Digital copy: https://digitallibrary.unicatt.it/veneranda/0b02da828027c8d4 (images not included).

The King of the Romans writes to his general in Transylvania, Giovanni Battista Castaldo, while the Ottomans are besieging Eger (Agria). The political and military passages, a page and a half, are in cipher. The other letters of Ferdinand bound in the same volume carry a contemporary marginal decipherment; this one has none. We rebuilt the alphabet from the glossed letters of 13 and 18 October, checked the key on two glossed passages of the letter of 22 October held out, and then read the letter.

The catalogue lists four letters (13, 15, 18, 22 Oct 1552). Correction for the catalogue entry: the ciphered parts of the letters of
13 Oct (ff. 9r-15v), 18 Oct (ff. 37r-42v) and 22 Oct (ff. 43r-48v) carry a complete contemporary decipherment in the margins (and at
the top of some pages); only the letter of 15 Oct (ff. 34r-36v) has none. The letter of 8 Nov 1552 (ff. 17r-25v) is in clear.

## System and key

Homophonic substitution with symbols (≈ 45 signs: ∀, ш, ʌ, ⁖, ʒ, ꝩ, ou, o‑o, ıı…), two or three signs for frequent letters; nulls (ƀ, M4, q̈, d:, n̈, m̈, Є) and plain Latin decoy words (Sicut, Ex, Insuper, Postquam); a few code groups for names (ϱ³, ϱ⁶, ϱ⁷, ϱ⁸) and for the army (Ħ⁹); double consonants written single.

`key.tsv` = key K2; `signs.md` = sign codes used in `transcription.txt`.

## Validation

Method (protocol written and hashed before any computing): (1) transcription of the glossed cipher passages of the letters of 13 October (f. 10r) and 18 October (f. 37r) and automatic alignment with their contemporary marginal glosses (alignment with nulls and omissions, re-estimated until stable); table K1 frozen (SHA-256); (2) validation V on the letter of 22 October held out (f. 44v, ll. 10–24, transcribed and frozen before the margin was read): mechanical decoding with K1 agrees with the gloss for 91.8% of characters and 73.1% of words, against at most 31.2% and 3.0% for 200 control keys; the threshold set for words (75%) is narrowly missed, because of the cipher’s spelling (ilorum, ila, que for quae) and three signs absent from K1; (3) validation V2, decided by amendment before transcription, on another held-out passage (f. 45v, ll. 1–16) with the completed key K2: 93.2% of characters and 77.8% of words (controls: at most 28.2% and 3.2%); (4) transcription of the target (ff. 34r–35v, ≈ 82 lines), frozen, decoded with the key, then read for sense.

## Reading

First published decipherment, to our knowledge. ≈ 1,900 signs, 319 words. Published under Daniel Bourdeau’s rule: 95% of the words read in their sense. Here: 316 of 319 words read in their sense (99.1%), of which 273 certain (85.6%), 41 probable (12.9%), 2 restored from the sense (0.6%) and 3 unresolved (0.9%): the code groups ‹C7›, ‹C3› and ‹C6›, which no contemporary gloss explains. Justifications in the notes.

## Notes

- The moment. In summer 1552 Temesvár (late July) and Lippa (August) fell; from 11 September the army of Ahmed Pasha and Ali Pasha besieged Eger, defended by István Dobó. Ferdinand was counting on the army of Maurice of Saxony on the other side and wanted Castaldo, from Transylvania, to make at least a diversion towards Lippa. The letter is dated 15 October; the Ottomans lifted the siege on the 18th. The plain text before it (f. 34r) says the letter answers Castaldo’s of 1 October and follows shortly after another letter carried by Balthasar; the plain text after it (ff. 35v–36r) deals with András Báthory’s inquiry into the Aldana affair and with the trial over the death of Brother George (Martinuzzi).
- The code groups. <C8> (ϱ⁸) stands for Lippa: the contemporary decipherer of the letter of 22 October glosses the same group as “Turcas qui Lippe indies augeantur” (f. 45v, l. 10); we count it as probable in all three places. Ħ⁹ with nulls stands for exercitus (gloss “cum hoc exercitu”, f. 44v). <C7>, named with Lippa as a place where the Turks left a garrison, is very probably Temesvár, but no gloss says so: we do not restore it. <C6> follows “regno nostro”; the decipherer of 45v leaves it out (“Regno illo nostro”): perhaps “Transilvanie”, perhaps a null. <C3>, whom the Turks would have asked for help, is not attested (a pasha?).
- The probable words (41). (1) The hash-like sign is o with two bars and l with one; I did not separate them in transcription, and the sense decides: uel ×4, leuiter, auxilium, facile ×2, alia loca, nihil, aliis ×2, dificultatibus, replicatis, tale, clementi, aliquam, fideliter, plurimum, solertia, ualeres. (2) Code groups explained by a gloss in another letter: Lippam ×3, exercitu, exercitus. (3) Rare or corrected sign: demonstrationem (Ж with “ou” written over it), fortasis (ʒ added above), Quamuis (one sign under a blot), potuerunt (corrected sign), agressus (sign 6 = g, a hapax), pannorum (compound sign = nn; cf. the gloss on 10r “pannorum quantitatem”), adferre (sign ıo = rr, as in “ferre” on 44v), accedente (variant of ʒ = cc, as in “accedit” on 44v), industria, uident. (4) A sign I probably misread: presidio, habemus, singulari, rationibus. Restored (2): [milites] (the cipher gives milit-s, the s after the decoy “Sicuti”) and [diutius] (final s missing); neither contradicts a certain letter.
- Prior work. Carpentieri (2012) notes that the ciphered parts of the letters of 13, 18 and 22 October were deciphered in the margins by different hands, without saying so of the letter of 15 October, which has none; Domokos (2025) studies the language of the volume without deciphering. As of 29 October 2026, Daniel Bourdeau’s repository holds only the catalogue entry (no. 1332). The plain draft in the Vienna chancery, if it survives, was not looked for.
