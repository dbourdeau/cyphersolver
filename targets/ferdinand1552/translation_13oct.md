# Ferdinand I to Castaldo, Vienna, 13 October 1552: English translation of the cipher passages

Translated here from `latin_13oct.txt`. The cipher passages are interrupted by clear text, not translated here; `[...]`
marks where a clear passage stands between two cipher passages. Codes: [Transylvania], [the army].

... how badly our affairs go in the parts of our kingdom of [Transylvania]: namely how stubbornly and obstinately the two
companies of German foot behaved, which you wanted to send as far as Deva together with our heavy horse, and how
disobedient and fickle those subjects of ours show themselves, and seem in some way, not obscurely, to lean to the wrong
side, so that you doubt their will and loyalty and fear being driven at last to what the enemy never could, namely to
think of your own safety. These things struck our mind, and with just cause, not lightly; and all the more gladly did we
learn of your great diligence, care and loyalty in scraping together twenty thousand florins for the full and complete
pay of the aforesaid heavy cavalry, and another sum for those two companies of German foot, so that you could use their
service to drive the enemy off from his raids. [...]

As much as we were troubled by such insolence and obstinacy of our soldiers, so, in turn, your singular and outstanding
loyalty and diligence, which you have shown us without pause in this and every other matter until now and show still,
is welcome and pleasing to us above all; above all your great zeal in finding money, amid so many difficulties arising
time and again, to restore the state of our affairs, which is sometimes failing. [...]

For as to the lack of money, we have had another, not small, sum of money and a not much smaller quantity of cloth
brought there by Captain Stephen for the pay of our troops, [...] and [you should] send men as far as Cassovia [Košice]
to accompany such cloth and money into [Transylvania] and bring it in. [...]

... and their minds led into doubt, so that they cannot grasp in their minds the plans and designs of our [army], which
are not to be despised, with which the illustrious Duke of Saxony, Maurice the Elector, is now encamped at Iaurinum
[Győr]. [...] [by which he may] draw the enemy away from the siege of Agria [Eger] and either force him to break it off
altogether or at least turn elsewhere. [...] from the town of Frauenmarkt [...] As soon as the loyal subjects and our
soldiers there learn of it, they will no doubt be readier and more eager in our service; and you will not have to
think so anxiously about what you write that the enemy could never force you to, namely your own safety. [...]

[We trust that you,] as a brave man experienced in war, will put such thoughts out of your mind, and rather direct and
refer all your plans and designs, as you have done until now, to the benefit of ourselves, our kingdoms and dominions
and of the whole Christian commonwealth, whose cause is at stake here, a benefit not separate from your own praise and
advancement, by the way of your usual industry, constancy and diligence. [...]

... and how much the most serene [queens] complain that we have not yet performed what we are bound to by the force of
the treaty concluded with her Serenity, [...] as her Serenity supposes, we have forgotten. [...]

Moreover, as for our having more than once charged you in recent days, above all because we value your person most
highly, and then that you should keep those troops of ours in camp in some fortified and suitable place, our mind and
will was no other than that you should oppose yourself and them to the enemy with whatever forces you could, and yet not
throw yourself into a manifest or grave danger until stronger and greater help could be sent to you. We are still of
that mind: that you oppose the enemy bravely, as far as can be done without serious danger. For since we are now helping
those soldiers of ours with money and cloth, we trust that not only they but also our subjects will be obedient to your
word and more spirited; and in future too we shall take fatherly and gracious care to send money and to arrange the
other necessary things. [...] [two companies] of up to a thousand men each.
