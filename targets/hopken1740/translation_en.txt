# The whole despatch No. 2 of 15/26 January 1740, images 321-331.

[image 321]
(Marked "Duplicate"; archive date 15/26 Jan. 1740; chancery note, first word doubtful: read in the Council 10 April
1740.) Most Mighty, Most Gracious King! In our last despatch, which was of the 5/16 of this month to Your Royal Majesty
and the Council of the Realm and to the President, the high-born Count Carl Gyllenborg, of which we humbly enclose the
duplicate, Your Royal Majesty will graciously have had laid before him how the exchange of the defensive alliance was
put off from day to day. (No. 2.)

[image 322]
and what we supposed [to be] the cause, and more. On the 10/21 of this month the Ottoman Porte's dragoman came to
us, and after greeting us from the Grand Vizier and the Reis Efendi he reported that, on the Seraglio's repeated command
and in keeping with the said ministers' own wish, the treaty was not only signed but also delivered into his hands,
which he had now brought with him, and he showed it, saying that he had orders to have a suitable purse made to lay
[it in]. And as the Ottoman Porte regards Your Royal Majesty's interest and good as its own (which it also [expects]
that Your Royal Majesty will do with it), they did not hesitate to confide to us that several weighty considerations
lead it to wish and desire that this negotiation be kept quiet for some little time yet; at which he said, as if of
himself, that the Ottoman Porte would gladly be assured of the execution of the last peace treaty on Russia's side.
Therefore, he said, he had orders to propose to us whether we would not agree that both exemplars of this alliance
might for some little time yet be kept [in] his hands, and that we should all the same [write] and report to our court
that the alliance was concluded, so that Your Royal Majesty's high ratification

[image 323]
might be sent here. We answered that all that was agreeable to the Ottoman Porte was also agreeable to Your Royal
Majesty, but that we, as instruments for maintaining and [increasing] the friendship, could not omit to point out that,
as [we?], on the strength of his and the other Porte ministers' assurances of a prompt exchange, which according to
those promises and assurances ought already to have taken place, had several times written and reported what was
confirmed and agreed, it would now fall hard on us, and could have an [ill] effect, if we were now obliged to report
this proposed delay and so to alter and overturn our earlier reports; and the more so as we had already reported that
he, the dragoman himself, had asked us to declare that this was a concluded negotiation to all who might have any
knowledge of it. As for the [secrecy] he now recommended, there is no doubt that it shall be [observed] as desired, but
[the exchange] in no way promoted it; for one had [no] need to show the original exemplar if one wished to make public
a negotiation agreed and concluded on both sides. That, as it was [known] to the whole world that Your Royal Majesty's

[image 324]
bearing and armaments [towards] the [Russian frontier] had most probably hastened the peace and been the cause that
Russia agreed to and pressed for it, so we have no doubt either that knowledge of this defensive alliance would much
sooner contribute to a prompt execution of what is stipulated in the same peace treaty than in any way hinder it.
That, as a vessel now lay ready to sail for Marseilles, we could by the best means and in all secrecy send over the
Ottoman Porte's original exemplar; so that if this opportunity slipped from our hands we should be obliged another
time, when the exchange took place, to send it for want of another opportunity by a [courier], which would arouse far
greater attention and cause more umbrage. Besides, as we [saw] the treaty to be signed already on 22 December, and we
had pledged that its ratification should be here within four months, it became impossible to carry out what we had
firmly promised. He said that, as to the last reason, we need not be troubled about the term of ratification, on which
we should [not] have to be so exact, since they did not mean to reckon

[image 325]
with us as with others. And that he [professed] to be the more easily convinced of what we had said as he had himself
long been of the same mind. That he would take it all ad referendum, to make his report on it and give us an answer the
following day. For the rest he made many protestations of the Ottoman Porte's sincerity and good will and of its
particular esteem for Your Royal Majesty, of which this alliance was the [strongest] [proof], being the very first of
its nature and kind that the Ottoman Porte had ever entered into with another power. He said he ought also to do the
French ambassador the justice that he had contributed much to opening the eyes of the Porte's ministers and brought
them to grasp the usefulness of this alliance, which he had said was the strongest means of making France's guarantee
effective. As the dragoman had first been with the said ambassador before he came to us, we saw well enough that the
ambassador had asked him to speak so; but we were made surer of it the same evening, when we visited the ambassador.
He forestalled us before we could speak and asked whether the Porte's dragoman had not said something about him.
Whereupon we took occasion to thank him for the part he had had in this negotiation's

[image 326]
happy [conclusion], which pleased him much, so that he asked us [earnestly?] to write to our court and [report] that
France has at present [very] great credit with the Ottoman Porte, which credit, he assured us, would be used wholly for
the benefit and advantage of Your Royal Majesty and his realm. Hard as it has been for us that we have until now been
obliged to complain of the said ambassador's conduct, so glad are we that we can now humbly report to Your Royal
Majesty that he has changed his conduct and begun to be helpful to us; which change we have observed ever since the
last express arrived from Venice, so that we perceive that it must have brought him strict orders from his court,
which must have been an effect of Count Tessin's good work at that court, as the ambassador also gave us to understand
in his discourses, when he told us in what credit the said count stood at the French court. On the 11/22 of this month
our dragoman was told at the Ottoman Porte that our view about the exchange of the treaty had been approved, when the
Porte's dragoman had us asked to [write out] our exemplar afresh and to prepare the ornaments needed for it;

[image 327]
he would then come and exchange it as soon as we were ready. On the 12/23 we let him know that our [exemplar] was
ready, but he avoided coming [under] one pretext and another, until at last we [perceived] that something new was
again [afoot], of which we were [informed] by himself, who sent word that we could not avoid making to the
[Mektupçu] or Secretary of State, Ragib Efendi, a fitting present, not only because he had been one of the
commissioners named by the Sultan but also because he had worked much and contributed to the happy conclusion of this
our negotiation. Whereupon we sent the said Secretary of State (who is himself a brisk and disinterested man) a present
of [three?] purses or [1,500] [piastres], [whereupon] at once the same day, namely the 14/25 of this month, the dragoman
presented himself to us and delivered to us the Ottoman Porte's exemplar, humbly enclosed herewith, which we exchanged
[for] ours after various [...] reciprocal compliments and assurances of mutual friendship and confidence between Your
Royal Majesty and the Ottoman Porte. Among other things he reported that the Reis Efendi had sent word to us that one cause of this negotiation's
good outcome was the French [ambassador's ...] and the

[image 328]
secrecy with which we had negotiated, so that no one, attentive as several were to us, had been able to notice the
least thing or had the slightest scent of it, at which he declared his particular pleasure. Thus, Most Gracious King,
we have now, by the help of the [Most High], fulfilled and carried out Your Royal Majesty's gracious orders and
instruction for entering into a treaty of defensive alliance with the Ottoman Porte. And as our only thought and
striving is to fulfil, to the utmost of our power, our humble duty and obligation to Your Royal Majesty, we long
[greatly] to hear that Your Royal Majesty has regarded these our proceedings with [most gracious] approval, which is
and shall be the greatest reward we desire in the world. As to the further reasons that persuaded and moved the
Ottoman Porte to conclude this treaty of alliance with Your Royal Majesty, we can and must on our consciences most
humbly [assure] that the firmness with which Your Royal Majesty acted last year in regard to the armaments on the
Russian frontier, and the readiness Your Royal Majesty showed to assist the Ottoman Porte, chiefly moved it to all
gratitude and brought it to [seek] to bind itself with Your Royal Majesty in so close

[image 329]
a friendship, about which it had for some years believed that Your Royal Majesty for his part cared so little, and
that because of the long delays and drawing-out of time in getting an answer from Sweden to its repeated offers of
that treaty of friendship, that we have never until now been able to convince it that various mischances, and not at
all a want of good will and sincerity on Your Royal Majesty's side, were the cause of these delays. Nor can we [deny]
that the French ambassador's good offices were of some use now at the close of the negotiation, and we have also let
the said ambassador understand this, the more as he is in great credit with the present ministry and will reside here
for some time yet and so can be of further service to us. What use and advantage Your Royal Majesty has to expect from
this alliance, as a good and effective means of holding Russia back from committing all kinds of disturbances against
its neighbours, Your Royal Majesty can best consider and judge himself in his high wisdom. This we must most humbly
say: as Your Royal Majesty's above-mentioned conduct in the conjunctures [that then] existed was with the Ottoman
Porte the most moving reason for concluding this alliance, so it is and remains also the surest guarantor of its
observance on the Porte's side. The greatest benefit that

[image 330]
Your Royal Majesty can draw from it on this side will come when the Ottoman Porte finds itself forced by Russia's
conduct, or of its own counsel through the ministry's inclination, into some quarrel with Russia, in which event Your
Royal Majesty will be able to stipulate what [conditions] he may desire. It therefore seems to us, in our humble
opinion, necessary that Your Royal Majesty have his minister or ministers residing here furnished with a provisional
instruction and full powers, so that through a correspondence as slow as the long road makes unavoidable good
opportunities may not slip from our hands. The old hatred this nation bears Russia is more than doubled by this last
quarrel, so that the peace will [hardly] be lasting and runs the risk of being overturned at the first change of
ministry; so that Your Royal Majesty's ministers here can, in our humble opinion, do no more useful service than
always, in a fitting and proper way, to keep the eyes of the Porte's ministers open to Russia's all too great and
disproportionate power, and to the necessity of reducing that power within its old bounds. As to the subsidies, we
have for very many weighty reasons not thought it advisable to press hard for them now, least of all

[image 331]
with this very suspicious nation, which might believe that Your Royal Majesty had no other aim with the alliance than
to get the said subsidies. That it was also impossible to get them now will be seen clearly from the fact that so
self-interested a ministry as the present one was able to resist such an offer as that of keeping a quarter. But when
the case of some activity arises, it will not be hard to get all that can be desired and [found] necessary, especially
if France, which alone together with Your Royal Majesty has any credit at this court, always seconds Your Royal
Majesty's views and intentions with the Turkish ministry. With all a subject's zeal and reverence we remain until the
hour of death, Most Mighty, Most Gracious King, Your Royal Majesty's most humble and most dutiful servants and
subjects, C. F. v. Höpken, E. Carlson. Pera in Constantinople, 15/26 January 1740.
