GISTS (3 lines a page, recipe level only)
p.50: Theoretical opening on philosophical mercury as root and mother of all metals.
      Claims it serves both to cure human bodies and to transmute and cleanse metals.
      Cites the philosophers: "est in mercurio quidquid quaerunt sapientes"; not common mercury.
p.51: Says the mercury is to be drawn from the perfect metallic body (gold), "mercurius solis".
      Latin aside: mercury is the root of the art of chemistry, all metals come from it.
      Promises a medicine to renew and restore the human body against nearly all sickness.
p.52: Continues the medicine claim, excepting illness sent by God as punishment for sin.
      Gold as a tempered body that some physicians give raw in medicine, e.g. for chronic disease and leprosy.
      Argues raw gold does little; it must be made into something else.
p.53: Gold must be brought into a spiritual body that penetrates and cleanses the blood.
      Clean blood drives out the disease; the medicine suits man as he too is made of earth like the metals.
      A mineral cure is a specific remedy because it shares the metals' properties.
p.54: Latin: people know gold is hard to destroy but not how hard; "easier to make gold than destroy it".
      Exhorts the reader to value the philosophers' mercury as a gift of God.
      Warns that the work cannot be hurried and needs proper means and diligence.
p.55: Patience: "festinatio ex parte diaboli est"; haste is the worker's fault, not the art's.
      Promises to describe the work from start to finish.
      Introduces how the philosophers proceeded; the first operation is next.
p.56: The first operation is sublimation, which is destruction by putrefaction of a dead-seeming body.
      From it comes a nobler thing fit for the tincture, called philosophers' mercury, a spiritual earth.
      Defines sublimating as subtilising, making a thing finer.
p.57: Sublimation separates the dross and the unfixed from the fixed so they are then joined and fixed together.
      Quotes Hermes ("make the fixed volatile and the volatile fixed") and Geber on sublimation.
      Latin list of operations held to be contained within sublimation begins.
p.68: Latin list continues (distillation, ascension, descension, congelation, putrefaction, calcination,
      inceration, white and red tincture) in one furnace, one vessel, one way; then putrefaction to a spiritual body.
      Practical recipe begins: a recipe for preparing the solar mercury in a glass dish (passage partly unread).
p.69: Same recipe continues: cleaning the material and sublimation, via the "arcana philosophorum" said to be
      hidden in wine.
      Recipe-level only; no further detail given here.
      Page ends mid-sentence.
