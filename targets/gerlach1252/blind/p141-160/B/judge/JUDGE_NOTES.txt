UNIT RULES
- 1 unit = 1 decoded letter. '?' (doubtful marker), ';', '=', '.', spaces: not units. The letter before a '?' is judged as a letter.
- '#' and '_' = 1 unit each, always N (brief rule). '___' = 3 N.
- No { } clear-script text occurs in this decode, so nothing excluded.
- S = letter in a word that reads as 16th-c. German / Latin chemical term. Period spellings accepted (uor, uil, wirt, waser, tzehen, langck, wegck, nigck, mercury/mercurio, precipitiret, figiret, on = ohne, hir, sin/nict/wide with a letter omitted: omissions cost nothing because no wrong letter is present).
- E = one wrong letter in an otherwise readable word (substitution or one extra letter).
- N = letter or sign with no sense (unknown signs; 'gr' p.148a L08).
- judge.tsv markup: [x] = E letters, <x> = N units, plain letters = S. Script check: the letters in every row's reading match decode.txt exactly.

GISTS (recipe level only)
p.151: Continuation of a recipe for a fixed red precipitate (powder) of mercury; the sealed glass is kept in its oven over weeks, then set in sand with heat raised in stages, and tested for fixity.
p.152: The same recipe continued: the powder is multiplied by repeating the process with ever larger shares of 'mercurius solis', each round making it stronger for medicine and for tingeing metals.
p.153: Start of a recipe for turning common quicksilver into good gold with this 'precipitate of sol': purified quicksilver is combined with the red powder, sealed and kept until it becomes a crimson-red powder.
p.154: Continuation: the red powder is repeatedly combined with further common quicksilver, sealed and heated for weeks, each round again giving a red powder.
p.155: End of that recipe: further rounds as before, then a section on turning the powder into gold in a crucible with a flux (borax) in a wind furnace; a note that quicksilver new to the powder is not yet medicine.
p.157a: Start of a recipe for preparing salt of tartar (calcined wine-stone) by dissolving in warm distilled water and filtering through a felt into a flask, with a reference to a drawing ('wie hir gemolett ist').
p.148a: Continuation: discard the dregs, distil the water off in sand until the tartar salt is left in the flask, with a warning to use a new flask each time because the salt cracks the glass.
p.149a: Continuation: the salt is ground, redissolved in distilled water, filtered and dried down again, repeating the cycle.
p.150a: End: repeat ten or twelve times until the salt is snow-white; this salt is called 'arcanum philosophorum', and is then put in a clean wooden box and set aside.
