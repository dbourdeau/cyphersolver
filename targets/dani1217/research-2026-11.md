# Berlin, Ms. or. oct. 3629

The two title-page lines remain unread. The copy is catalogued as 1217. The copyist wrote 615 AH in the colophon, both in words and in numerals. The fourth lunar month of 615 AH falls in July 1218. The date of the cipher itself is uncertain.

## Checks against the opening

Number the upper signs from the right, omitting the three dots. Assume one sign per written letter, one sign shape per letter, and no omitted letters. The first seven base shapes are distinguishable even without assigning the nearby isolated dots. This gives the following repeat tests without requiring a transcription of the damaged middle.

| Proposed opening | Positions that must agree | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| بسم الله | 5, 6 | Different shapes |
| التيسير | 4, 6 | Different shapes |
| كتاب التيسير | 3, 5 | Different shapes |
| أبو عمرو (author’s kunya) | 3, 7 | Different shapes |
| العبد المسرف | 1, 6 and 2, 7 | Different shapes |
| أبو سعيد (copyist’s kunya) | None within the first seven | No positive identification. The following بن would require signs 2 and 8 to agree. Both have an upright over a foot, but sign 8 has a conspicuous mark below. |

These tests concern the opening only. They do not exclude the same words elsewhere, and rely on reading the line from the right. For the copyist hypothesis, سعيد and حميد also predict a repeated two-sign ending. Identifying that pair at the expected spacing would be more informative than accepting the seven distinct initial letters as a match.

## Checks against the ending

[See the lower left edge of figure 69.](https://content.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de/dc/PPN1879946874-00000461/full/full/0/default.jpg)

The numbers run backwards from the presumed end of the inscription. They assume right-to-left reading, that the three dots are a boundary mark, and that the two upright signs nearest that boundary are separate letters. Under that segmentation the final five shapes are distinct. A proposed ending can therefore be checked without relying on the faint middle of either line.

| Proposed ending | Required repetition counted from the end | Observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| رحمه الله, “may God have mercy on him” | 2 = 3 (ل) | The second sign is forked and the third is S-shaped. |
| غفر الله له, “may God forgive him” | 1 = 3 (ه) | The first and third signs differ. |
| رضي الله عنه, “may God be pleased with him” | 1 = 4 (ه) | The first and fourth signs differ. |
| الشافعي المقري, with both final letters treated as yāʾ | 1 = 7 (ي) | The first sign has two horizontal strokes on an upright. This shape does not recur among the visible candidates for position 7. The spelling المقرئ, with a distinct hamza-bearing letter, removes this prediction. |

The five-letter suffix المقري alone contains no repetition, so this test cannot identify it. The opening and ending checks do not yield an alphabet that decodes the intervening signs.

### Passage search

[check_passages.py](check_passages.py) searches sequences of complete words totaling 38–48 normalized letters. It allows each physical line 17–26 letters, including a break inside a word, and tries both reading directions and both line orders. Its three hypothesized repetitions are upper right 1 = lower left 3, upper right 3 = upper left 2, and upper right 9 = lower right 1. It then checks distinct sign shapes at the line ends. This tests simple substitution with the specified segmentation. It cannot rule out a spelling variant, a different inscription length, or a different cipher.

The scanned sources were the [Tanzil simple-clean Qurʾān](https://tanzil.net/download/) (6,236 verses) and the [OpenITI edition of al-Taysīr](https://github.com/OpenITI/0450AH/tree/master/data/0444AbuCamrDani/0444AbuCamrDani.TaysirFiQiraatSabc) (Shamela0005527-ara1). No window wholly inside a single Qurʾānic verse survived the strict check (81,070 windows). Allowing windows across verse breaks produced one strict match among 200,336 windows, from Q 7:73–74. It fails on characters withheld from the search. Its wāw requires the upper hourglass and the fourth sign from the lower right (an upright) to be identical. Its alif also requires upper right 7 (stacked loops) to equal the far-left upper sign. The exact length of 43 is coincidental. The al-Taysīr scan produced no strict match among 88,361 windows allowing paragraph crossings, and six manuscript-based title, signature and date variants yielded none among 102 windows.

The [Fihrist manuscript descriptions](https://github.com/fihristorg/fihrist-mss) supplied 210,816 windows from Arabic-script colophons, ownership notes and other transcribed material. Two passed the strict filter. Both failed the held-back checks (Arabic_MS_424.xml and ms_380287.xml).

As a check on false positives, the same search covered the OpenITI texts of [Bukhari](https://github.com/OpenITI/0275AH/tree/master/data/0256Bukhari/0256Bukhari.Sahih) (1,350,587 windows) and [Muslim](https://github.com/OpenITI/0275AH/tree/master/data/0261Muslim/0261Muslim.Sahih) (1,178,293). Nine passages satisfied the strict filter and both held-back conditions. Only paragraph 6144 of Muslim also assigned the same letter to the two 9-shaped forms with a lower stroke, at upper x335 and x440, assuming each counts as one sign. That reading assigned alif to both forms and to the lower far-left comb, a conspicuously different shape. The eight other passages gave different letters to the two upper forms. This illustrates how known Arabic passages can accidentally satisfy even the held-back conditions. The paragraph indices are zero-based in the script's split of the OpenITI text.

[check_shapes.py](check_shapes.py) starts at every normalized Arabic letter in a corpus, even inside words. It varies the upper length from 19 to 23 and lower from 20 to 24, in both directions and both line orders. It requires the three earlier repetitions and then scores six further hypotheses about the bodies of signs, ignoring detached marks. In the illustrated layout these are U2 = U8 (uprights with a foot), B3 = B4, B5 = B18, B8 = B22, U19 = B21, and U9 = U15 = U17. The script anchors the corresponding positions at the nearest physical edge when varying lengths. The six comparisons are exploratory, and different symbols may share a base shape.

No passage met even five of the six extra conditions in the Tanzil Qur'an, the OpenITI *Taysir*, or the Shamela0007754 text of the *Hirz*. The best Qur'anic result met four, in Q 28:48-49, beginning and ending inside words. It assigns alif both to U1, the curled S, and U9, the 9-shaped sign. Thus it fails a clear shape distinction that was not used in scoring. The best *Taysir* and *Hirz* results each met three. This result is conditional on the model for line lengths and sign grouping.

### Blind substitution and letter diversity

[blind_search.py](blind_search.py) trains an Arabic character trigram model on al-Taysir, Bukhari and Muslim. Ten consecutive, non-overlapping 43-letter segments from the manuscript's reading certificate on 109a serve as controls. The solver sees only the equality pattern of the letters, with the same six middle positions hidden as in the provisional Berlin layout. Two sets of 35 simulated-annealing restarts use different seeds. On the controls, the highest-scoring assignment recovered 0, 8, 9, 9, 11, 11, 14, 16, 27 and 31 of the 37 visible letters (sorted). In every case a wrong reading scored higher than the known text. This calibration concerns this trigram model and this sample.

Two explicit classifications of the Berlin signs use 30 and 26 different classes among the 37 visible positions. Both group the two capped hourglasses, curled S signs, three lower curls and two lower combs. The 26-class version also groups several signs whose detached marks differ. Across both line orders and both directions, the two runs agreed at 0-10 of 37 positions. No assignment supplies a reproducible reading.

The diversity itself constrains both classifications. With the six positions masked at the same offsets in upper-first right-to-left order, the maximum number of distinct normalized letters was **23** in all 328,049 continuous 43-letter windows of the Tanzil Quran (within chapters), **23** in 150,590 windows of the OpenITI Taysir (including headings), and **21** in 484 windows of the 109a certificate transcription. Under one sign per letter, even the 26-class Berlin interpretation needs three more letter types than the maximum observed in either long text. Additional mergers of signs, a different segmentation or orthography, variants for the same letter, and material with unusually high letter diversity all remain possible. The observed maximum is a corpus result, not a theoretical limit on Arabic.

A broader comparison found at most 24 distinct normalized letters in 2,354,168 continuous Bukhari windows and 1,928,617 Muslim windows. A third Berlin model groups the wavy U10/U18/B17 forms and U4/B14, reducing the number of classes to 23. Such diversity actually occurs in the comparison corpora. On this grouping the two blind runs agreed at 4-17 visible positions depending on order, and the resulting sequences did not give a continuous reading. The additional mergers are hypotheses about similar pen strokes.

A single traversal of the ordinary 28-letter alphabet in conventional or abjad order would have no internal repeats before the end. In the upper line alone U3 and U20 are both capped hourglasses, only 17 positions apart in the provisional layout. A plain alphabet traversal therefore also fails if those two signs have the same value.

### Readers documented in this copy

[Sellheim p. 24, including the Arabic certificate of folio 109a](https://content.staatsbibliothek-berlin.de/dc/PPN1879946874-00000052/full/full/0/default.jpg) identifies a thirteenth-century owner who read this copy, Jamal al-Din Yusuf ibn Abi Bakr ibn Yusuf. His own notes use al-Aqfahsi and call both his father and grandfather al-Hajj. The certificate uses al-Aqfasi. These spellings are evidence for variants worth testing.

* Under right-to-left simple substitution, an upper line opening with **يوسف بن أبي بكر** would have identical ya at positions 1 and 9. Those signs are a curled S and a 9 on a stem. Writing **ابن** instead requires the different signs at positions 6 and 9 to agree.
* **يوسف بن الحاج أبي بكر بن الحاج يوسف** evades the first-nine-letter test. But its third letter is sin, and the next sin occurs at letter 27. The upper capped hourglass repeats at the second position from the left, so no proposed upper line of 17–26 letters beginning this way accounts for both hourglasses. This checks the specific expanded formula only.
* A name beginning **جمال الدين** requires equal signs at positions 3 and 5. Both **محمد بن أحمد** and **محمد بن علي** require 1 and 3 to match. The later recorded owner's **إسماعيل** requires 1 and 4 to match. All conflict with the upper right opening.
* I transcribed the six printed Arabic lines of the 109a certificate and tested 304 windows under the four direction and order hypotheses. Six placements met the three initial repetitions; all six failed the line-end distinctness check. Substituting the original reading *Abi* for the editor's *Abu*, plus alternate spellings of *al-muqriʾ* and *ʿAbd Allah*, gave the same counts. Sellheim also mentions four verses on the title page. Figure 69 shows only the two cipher lines and the Arabic attribution, so those four verses could not be checked from this plate.

Al-Shatibi's *Hirz al-amani* is a pre-1218 versification of the very work in the Berlin copy. I tested three [OpenITI texts of the poem](https://github.com/OpenITI/0600AH/tree/master/data/0590IbnFirruhShatibi/0590IbnFirruhShatibi.HirzAmani) with page codes removed, allowing matches across verse breaks. There were 34,107 windows in JK000450, 33,422 in ShamAY0034095 and 37,252 in Shamela0007754. The continuous texts include headings and in the last edition prefatory material. No window passed the strict distinct-sign filter. These overlapping editions are not independent trials.

The additional script [check_joining.py](check_joining.py) lets a single Arabic letter take different signs according to whether it connects to the preceding or following letter in its word. With the 21+22 transcription and its six masked positions fixed, it examined every 43-letter window within Qurʾān chapters (328,049) and within al-Taysīr paragraphs (95,820), each in four line orders and directions. Zero windows reproduced all the assigned repetitions, even before requiring different signs to represent different forms. The result depends on the transcription, the spelling of the editions and the hypothesis of positional encoding.
