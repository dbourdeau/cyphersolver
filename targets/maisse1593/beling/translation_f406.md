# Henri IV to Maisse, Chartres, 8 February 1593 (BnF fr. 16093 f. 406): English translation

Andrew Beling, with AI agents, 12 Nov 2026, of beling/reading_f406.md. The clear part is WAINUT's translation (wainut/f406_translation.md); the part in cipher is a new translation of the corrected copy. [?] = doubtful.

## In clear

Monsieur de Maisse, I have always answered your despatches as I received them and noted their dates in my letters, so as not to leave you in doubt, and so that you could tell whether any of them had been lost.

Because of that uncertainty, I approve your practice of sending duplicates of the previous despatch with the most recent ones.

Since the last letters I wrote to you, I have received together yours of 26 December and 1 January; the latter reported the arrival with you of the ambassador[?] with his despatch.

If you found that despatch out of season in some respect, you will nevertheless have understood that this was not for want of his being sent back early enough to reach in time the place where he must go, had he not met extraordinary hindrances on his journey.

Had the remedies for these been in my power and had I not provided them, I would regret this inconvenience the more; but since I could do no more than I have done, one must still do what one can for the rest and wait, as in all other things, for the fruit that it will please God to bring from it.

I believe that in the said despatch you will have found an answer to all the points on which you thought an answer from me was needed concerning my affairs over there.

Or if you saw anything lacking, you will have supplied it, as I left it to your judgement; and I am confident, as regards the Pope, that you will have seen to it that it was not delayed on that account, as your letter gave me hope.

Once this step is taken, we shall wait for what the first news after the receipt of the said despatch brings us from that quarter.

Meanwhile, having thought it fitting to make some reply to the letters I received from the sieur de Brèves, I have by the same means chosen to write a word of thanks to the Grand Seigneur, and likewise to write to his chief ministers about the refusal to let the ambassador of Spain go to Constantinople, which they say in their letters, that you sent me earlier, was made out of regard for me.

If you have kept the copy of the cipher that I sent to the said sieur de Brèves, as I understood you would, you will see what I write to him; for I wish you to know all that comes and goes in that quarter, because of the connection between the two charges, and because of the value I set on the good advice you can give in it.

## In cipher, from "Le surplus" to the end

The rest of your letters concerns the affairs of Rome, the journey of the marquis de Pisany, and the war in Piedmont. I was glad to learn from your letters that the marquis has arrived in Italy, having as yet heard nothing from him myself. I await the outcome of his journey, and likewise of the new means you have found to help my affairs in Piedmont. Although I know that you do what you can there, with the other despatches I sent to the sieur de Lesdiguières by the men who came to me from him, I sent him a letter I wrote to you on that very matter, so that he can get it to you by whatever route he thinks best. I have nevertheless told his men what all your letters show of the care you take there, so that they could assure Lesdiguières that, without being asked, you do of your own accord everything he could wish. Still, so as to leave out nothing that depends on me, I have added this further charge, which he will use if he sees fit, to learn still better what he may expect.

The sieur de Gondi arrived at Nancy some days ago. I sent him the passport he asked of me for the rest of his journey. But since I must make a journey [?] to Tours that I cannot put off, and from there go down to Saumur, where my sister arrived some time ago, I shall only be able to see him on my return, about the end of this month or the start of March. Meanwhile I have told him to go and rest at Mantes, where the sieur de Schomberg has gone to wait for him. I have set a day for the nobility of the nearby provinces to join me at Mantes, from where I mean to advance with my army against the enemy's, wherever it may be.

One of the matters I have to settle at Tours is to bring my officers and other servants who have taken refuge there into contribution, as those around me have freely offered, to make a fund that can keep the garrisons around Paris for a whole year, if need be. Their upkeep is charged on what comes from trade, so as to cut that trade off and forbid it altogether; for everyone judges that if the city stays so closely pressed for six months it will be forced to come to reason, since it cannot hold provisions for so long. Even so, to be safe, it has been decided to make the fund for the whole year. Once that is well settled, it will remain for me to stop the enemy army from bringing in large convoys or taking the places I need for this purpose, and I fully hope to prevent both.

Although [?] I may not have as large a force as they, their assembly in Paris goes on. After it opened, the duc de Mayenne went off to receive the foreign army, which is no more than five or six thousand foot and eight hundred to a thousand horse, with no commander but Count Charles. Mayenne has left the assembly no freedom but to propose and discuss among themselves without concluding anything until his return, for which he has given them three weeks. Meanwhile there is every sign, as he saw well enough before he left, that they do not all agree to this, most of them leaning towards coming to a conference, following the offer sent to them in the name of the Catholics who serve me, despite the efforts of those who want no peace in this kingdom to turn them from it. With this letter you will have the declaration I have made against the convocation and the other acts of the duc de Mayenne. Praying God …
