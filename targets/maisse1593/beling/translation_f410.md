# Henri IV to Maisse, Mantes, 27/28 April 1593 (BnF fr. 16093 f. 410): English translation

Andrew Beling, with AI agents, 12 Nov 2026, of beling/reading_f410.md. The clear part is WAINUT's translation (wainut/f410_translation.md); the part in cipher is a new translation of the corrected copy. [?] = doubtful.

## In clear

Monsieur de Maisse, since my last letter of the 29th of last month, I have received yours of 20 February and of 7 and 23 March; the bearer of the last arrived sooner than the sieur de la Clielle[?], whom my cousin the duc de Nevers had kept with him for some days in Champagne.

You show me so clearly in your despatches the disposition and state of all things over there, even in what concerns my service, that I take much satisfaction from it, and I recognise more and more the usefulness that your good offices and the continual care you take there bring me.

So I beg you to be assured that I hold your merit towards me to be such that your absence on this occasion will make you lose nothing of what, by serving so well and worthily, you may rightly desire of my good grace and favour.

## In cipher, from "L'experience" to the end

Experience has shown that you were in no way mistaken in the judgement you long ago formed of the affairs of Rome. Yet this proof was needed to convince those who would otherwise have kept thinking a mistake had been made if it had not been tried; they will have more reason from now on to agree to another course and remedy for the affairs of this kingdom, when it is decided to take one. And although there is already justification enough for doing so, I have not wished to reject the advice given me not to let the Cardinal de Gondi or the marquis de Pisany come back yet. It is thought this may to some extent keep His Holiness from the extremes he is being pushed towards, once he sees by this patience that no pretext is being sought to break with him, as the enemy want to persuade him. In any case I shall be glad for them to go forward or back in this according to the advice the Grand Duke sends him, and not otherwise, at least unless they have other news from me; from his affection and prudence, I believe, they can only come out well advised and working for the good of my affairs. In this I think that what I have told the sieur de Gondi of the esteem I have for the Duke's counsels may do much to encourage him to embrace warmly everything that serves my establishment, as Gondi has promised me on his behalf.

I have received by La Clielle the original letters of the Grand Seigneur and his ministers, of which I had already had copies in translation and had answered. I shall wait before doing anything more until I know what the ambassador's despatch has produced, together with the change he will have found on arriving. As I have seen by your letters, in order to set the course of my affairs on that side, I believe the decision was good to have Lancosme seized, for the reasons you give me. But having decided it, it would have been better to put him aboard some good English ship than to send him as was done. It is much to be feared that by that route he will find a way to escape, and there is no doubt that the Spaniards will help him with money and whatever else they can; I see from their despatches, several important ones of which have newly fallen into my hands, that they lament him greatly, as an instrument that would enlarge [?] the merit of their faith and of the cause they uphold. What makes me fear his escape most is the reproach I may receive from the Grand Seigneur and his ministers, who will not be long in hearing the news, since he would not fail to make the most of it with the King of Spain, as a man able to do him great service, even on that side. But since it can no longer be undone, we must wait and see what comes of it.

I have learned from your letters of the orders already given for the war on the side of Hungary and Croatia, and of the alarm the coming of the Chaush caused those lords over the enterprise of Signa. If this goes ahead and opens the way for you to warm them to some good offices and services towards me, I am sure you will make good use of the occasion, and of any other that time may bring. Their ambassador does not stir from Chartres, which leaves me no way to thank him in person, as you suggest, for the good offices they have done with the Pope; I can do it by letter. La Clielle has told me of a plan you are working to bring off. Knowing that its success can only turn out well for my affairs, I shall be glad for you to pursue it, if you see the means to do so. He also told me of the help sent you, twenty thousand écus, both to pay for the presents for the Levant and to begin relieving the needs of the sieur de Lesdiguières. I have also seen from your note in cipher what had already been granted you for him by the month, and the assurance you were also given for "Gien-ne" [?], once only.

The sieur de Gondi gives me hope that they will go further in the affairs of Piedmont. He has not spoken of putting them under any name but Lesdiguières's. He did tell me that if Lesdiguières, while remaining the real chief and principal leader, were willing to let some Catholic lord bear the title, it would remove much of the scruple his religion causes in Italy, but that nothing should be done about this except as far as Lesdiguières himself thought fit. For otherwise he is recognised there as a man of such merit and valour, so good a chief and so excellent a captain, that those who wish my service well judge I would do great wrong to give him the slightest cause for discontent on this point. You may believe I was very glad to see their opinion agree with my intention; not that, had they tried to persuade me otherwise, I would for any reason have wronged so deserving a servant of mine in a matter touching his honour. But it has made me all the surer that they will not fail to support him, and I ask you to go on helping them in this with all the skill you can.

You will have seen from my previous letters that I approved the proposal of the princes and other lords who serve me to meet in conference with some men whom the Paris assembly would name. The common people took this up so warmly that all the intrigues of the Spaniards and their partisans could not stop it going ahead: deputies have been named on both sides, and they are to meet within two or three days for certain. It is thought that their side comes with little good intention, that fear of the blame a refusal would bring them with the public makes them come rather than any wish for some healthy fruit for this state, and that they will look for some plausible pretext to break off instead of attending to the common good. If so, those of our side who are there will take good care not to let them gain any advantage. And perhaps God will bring things to a point that some men's wills would not have reached of their own accord. Praying God, etc.
