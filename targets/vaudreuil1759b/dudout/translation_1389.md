## 1389 — Montréal, 10 April 1759 (ANOM COL C11A 104, ff. 68-72)

My Lord,
I have the honour to send you herewith a copy 1. of the letter the Marquis de Montcalm wrote me on 27 February last; 2. of a
memorandum giving on one side the Marquis de Montcalm's reflections and on the other my answers; 3. of the summary of the [...]
of my general operations for this campaign, which I handed to the Marquis de Montcalm. You will see from all these papers, my
Lord, what the Marquis de Montcalm's opinion was.
I have the honour to point out to you, for yourself alone, my Lord, that the Marquis de Montcalm's way of thinking has been public;
it was the talk of officer, townsman, soldier and militiaman long before it became known to me. Unfortunately he did not voice his
opinion, when he spoke it aloud and talked with all and sundry, with as much discretion as he did in his letter and his written
reflections. From this followed a flood of arguments that soon went round every parish. The loss of the colony has been regarded
as quite evident; the measures I take for its preservation have been criticised by certain officers of the land troops, who
added the most improper remarks about me before the people themselves; and this people, already only too weary of the war, short
of subsistence and of all its other most pressing needs, and feeling at the same time the goodness and vigour of its constitution
weakening; this people, I say, would be easy to persuade; the evil it is threatened with frightens it and would discourage it if
I did not reassure it, and if my firmness and the confidence it has in me did [not] revive its zeal and its ardour for the King's
service.
I have seen only too well that all this talk {did} not displease the Marquis de Montcalm. The progress it has made is unknown to
no one; but the officers who started it are only the bolder and freer in their talk. I have asked the Marquis de Montcalm to be
good enough to cut all this talk at the root, failing which I could not avoid having arrested those officers who take most part
in it and sending them to France to answer to you for [their] conduct. I would already have done, my Lord, all I said on this
point to the Marquis de Montcalm; but in our present circumstances I restrain myself and use gentle means to bring minds round
and to revive the timid; yet I shall have to take a severe course if the case requires it, to prevent a greater evil: that {is}
something I shall do only {as} a last resort.
I do justice to the officers of the land troops in general, as is their {due}; many of them have a circumspection equal {to}
their zeal. None of this prevents me, my Lord, from seizing every occasion to keep as much union as understanding between the
Marquis de Montcalm and myself. I shall pass over in silence all he has said and may say about me; far {from} showing him any
resentment, I give him marks of my confidence; in short I do everything to avoid the scandal of a break between him and me. I
consider only the good of the service itself and the glory of His Majesty's arms.
I await your first despatches, my Lord, with the greatest impatience. I hope you will have been good enough to enter into the
reasons I have set out to you alone. Meanwhile, so as to have nothing to reproach myself with, you see that I have told the
Marquis de Montcalm that I expect him to take command of the army of Carillon.
I am with very deep respect, my Lord, your very humble and very obedient servant. Vaudreuil [in clear]
