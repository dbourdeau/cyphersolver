# English rendering: Vaudreuil to the minister, Montréal, 10 Apr 1759 (ANOM COL C11A 104 ff.68-72)

Working translation of `reading.md`. `[...]` = supplied from the sense; `[?]` = groups read but not understood.

I have the honour to send you herewith copies of: 1st, the letter that the Marquis de Montcalm wrote to me on the 27th of last February; 2nd, a memorandum
containing on one side the reflections of the Marquis de Montcalm [?]; 3rd, the summary of the plan of my general operations for this campaign,
which I handed to the Marquis de Montcalm. You will see, Monseigneur, from all these papers what the Marquis de Montcalm's view has been. I have the honour
to point out to you, for yourself alone, Monseigneur, that the Marquis de Montcalm's way of thinking has been public: it has been the talk of the officer,
the townsman, the soldier and the militiaman long before it came to my knowledge.

Unfortunately he did not make his opinion known by announcing it by word of mouth and talking to all sorts of people indiscriminately with as much discretion
as he showed in his letter and written reflections. From this a train of arguments followed which quickly circulated through all the parishes; the loss of the
Colony has been regarded as very evident; the measures I take for its preservation have been censured by certain officers of the land troops, who have added
the most indecent remarks about me to the people themselves, a people already only too weary of the war, short of subsistence and of all their various most pressing needs, and who
see at the same time that the goodness and vigour of their constitution are weakening. This people, I say, would be easily persuaded; the evil with which they
are threatened frightens them and would discourage them if I did not reassure them, and if my firmness and the confidence they have in me did not revive their zeal and their ardour
for the King's service.

I have only too well recognised that all these arguments do not displease the Marquis de Montcalm; the progress they have made is unknown to no one, but the officers who are
their authors are only the bolder and the freer in their talk for it. I have asked the Marquis de Montcalm to be so good as to cut off these arguments at the root; otherwise
I could not spare myself from having arrested those officers who take the largest part in them, and from sending them to France to give you an account of [their] conduct.
I would, Monseigneur, have already carried out all that I have said on this point to the Marquis de Montcalm, but in the circumstances in which we are I do myself violence; I use
[?] [?] of gentleness to bring minds round and to revive those who are timid; but I shall have to take a course of rigour if the case demands it, to
forestall a greater evil. That I shall do only in the last extremity.

I do the officers of the land troops in general the justice that is due to them: many of them have a circumspection equal to their zeal. None of this prevents me, Monseigneur, from
seizing every occasion to maintain as much union as understanding between the Marquis de Montcalm and myself, and I shall pass over in silence all the remarks he has made and may make about me.
Far from showing him resentment for it, I give him proofs of my confidence; in short, I am doing everything I can to avoid the scandal of a rupture between him and me; I look only to
the good of the service itself and the glory of His Majesty's arms. I await your first despatches, Monseigneur, with the greatest impatience; I hope you will have been so good as to
enter into the reasons which I have set out to you alone.

However, you see that, so as to have no reproach to make to myself, I have told the Marquis de Montcalm that I expect him to take command of the army at Carillon.

I am with very deep respect, Monseigneur, your very humble and very obedient servant, Vaudreuil.
