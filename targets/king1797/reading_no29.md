## What it says

King answers points from Pickering's letters, and takes Sweden first. Under the late regency the Duke Regent favoured France,
and Swedish policy followed. The young King, now of age, is more purely Swedish in his outlook, and French influence at
Stockholm has declined. King therefore expects the President's answer to the Swedish overtures to be well received there.
In giving it, he has tried to show the United States' regard for Sweden and the friendly spirit in which the proposal was taken,
and to explain frankly why the President declines it for the present. The despatch then turns, in clear, to the Maryland Bank
Stock, the conduct of Captain Home, and the protection of seamen.

**Context.** Gustav IV Adolf of Sweden took over the government as a king of age on 1 November 1796. Since 29 March 1792 he had
been under the guardianship of his uncle Duke Karl, who soon handed real power to his favourite G. A. Reuterholm (*Svenskt
biografiskt lexikon*, hereafter SBL, "Gustav IV Adolf"). Reuterholm's foreign policy leaned towards France: a defensive alliance
with the Republic in May 1793, never ratified, and a subsidy treaty in September 1795. He was relieved of his offices on 22 October
1796 and left for Germany (SBL, "Gustaf Adolf Reuterholm"). In 1794 the Swedish minister in London, Lars von Engeström, invited
the United States through Thomas Pinckney to accede to the Danish-Swedish neutrality convention of 27 March 1794 (*Papers of
Alexander Hamilton* 16:548-50, notes). Hamilton, Knox and the Attorney General Bradford advised Washington against a concert with
Sweden and Denmark (Randolph to Washington, 9 July 1794, *Papers of George Washington*, Presidential Series 16:321-25). Pickering
later named the overture King answers here. Writing to John Adams on 24 Sept 1798, he said Engeström was mistaken in supposing that
his "propositions" to Pinckney (which Pickering dates to 1793) had gone unanswered, as "my letter to Mr. King, of the 17th of Septr.
1796" would show, with extracts from King's "letters of April 13th 1797, & June 5th following" (Founders Online, Adams Papers
99-02-02-3023). Pickering's letter of 17 Sept 1796 was not read.

## Reading

No. 29. London Apr. 13th 1797.

Dear Sir,

There are several Subjects mentioned in different Letters that I have received from you, concerning which I should make you
some Report: I will take notice of them as they occur to my recollection, beginning with *Sweden*. During the *late regency*
it is well understood that the *Duke Regent* *[?indulged]* *a partiality for France, and the measures of the government* were
consequently in some degree *influenced by this* *[?predilection]*. *The King since his* *[?majority]* is supposed to entertain
sentiments, which if not of an *opposite nature* are more exclusively *national, and French influence is less at*
*[?Stockholm]* *than* during the *regency*. *The answer which the President instructed me* to return to the Overtures which had
been made *to us by* *[?the]* *[?Swedish]* *government* will therefore in my opinion be *well received* *[?and]* *approved at*
*[?Stockholm]*.

It has been my aim on this occasion to manifest *our* *[?esteem]* *for the* *[?Swedish]* *nation*, to shew likewise the
friendly light in which *we received the proposal that had been made to us*, and to state in a candid manner the motives that
have influenced the President for the present *to decline the overtur[e]*.

Maryland Bank Stock

I have omitted saying any thing respecting this Subject, because I could say nothing that would amount to such information as
would authorise a satisfactory opinion concerning the result of the endeavours that I have with much assiduity employed to
bring this Business to the Termination that is desired. –

Though I agree with the State of Maryland in thinking the Claim both Just and equitable, still I do find it is one in the
management of which much prudence and circumspection are requisite: the Business is at present in motion, and I flatter
myself with the belief that it is in a favorable course – I have repeatedly conversed with the Lord Chancellor and other
Members of the Administration on this Subject, and the measures that I have adopted to obtain a satisfactory issue of the
Business have had their approbation. – The Chancellor will dismiss the Bills depending in his Court, on the principle of a
Defect of Jurisdiction – I have sent a Note to the Secretary of Foreign affairs, stating in a concise manner, the Claim and the
principles that support it, as also the allegation that a Counter Claim exists grounded on the supposition of His Majesty's
Right to this Property, and concluding with a request, that his Majesty would order that the Stock in question should be
transferred by the Accomptant General of the Court of Chancery, in whose name it stands, to me for the use of the State of
Maryland – According to present appearances, there is no reason to apprehend a disappointment in this request; though I do
fear that the order to transfer may be upon the Condition that the Claim of Russell, who was one of the original Trustees,
shall be considered and satisfied. –

I will continue to resist the impolicy as well as the injustice of such a condition, and in case it shall be made, I will
wait until I receive your instructions, grounded on those of Maryland, before I accede to the same. –

Captain Home

Before I received your Letter, that reminded me that no answer had been given to our Complaint against the Offensive conduct
of this Officer, I had mentioned the Subject to Lord Grenville, who said that he would enquire and inform me what had been
done respecting it: but as I had not received the promised information when your late Letters came to hand, and as the
Subject has been revived by Mr. Adet, I conceived it to be my duty to renew and enforce the Complaint; which I did in a
Letter to Lord Grenville a copy of which is inclosed – to this Letter I have received an Answer a Copy whereof is also
inclosed. –

Seamen

I have informed you heretofore of the slight probability that this Government would, during the war, consent to any
convention that would give that security to our Seamen which we have an undoubted right to enjoy – It was before my arrival
that Lord Grenville had expressed to Mr. Pinckney a dissatisfaction with the practice of granting Protections to American
Seamen by our Consuls; Mr. Pinckney had not answered the Enquiries that had been made of him concerning the consular
authority in this respect, and probably for the same reason that caused me to waive an answer when the same enquiry was made
of me at my first Conference with Lord Grenville. –

⟦ Before I received your opinion on this Subject Lord Grenville had written me a Letter in which this Branch of the Consular
Power is denied, and notice given to us that the practice must be discontinued; a Copy of that Letter, and of mine
transmitting it to our several Consuls I had the honor to send you with my Letter of the 10th. of December – Previous to the
communication of this Resolution of the British Government, it had been notified to Mr. Pinckney that all applications for the
Discharge of American Seamen impressed into the British Service, must in future come through the American Minister, instead
of coming from the American Consuls as had been customary – One consequence of this Regulation has been, that the Subject in
all its Details has come under my observation, and its importance, I confess, is much greater than I had supposed it –
instead of a few, and those in many instances equivocal cases, I have since the Month of July past, made application for the
Discharge from British Men of war of 271 Seamen, who stating themselves to be Americans, have claimed my interference: Of this
number 86 have been ordered by the Admiralty to be discharged; – 37 more have been detained as British Subjects or as
American Volunteers or for want of Proof that they are Americans; and to my applications for the Discharge of the remaining
148 · I have received no answer; the Ships on board of which these Seamen were detained having in many instances sailed before
an examination was made in consequence of my applications.

⟦ It is certain that some of those who have applied to me are not American Citizens, but the Exceptions are in my opinion
few, and the evidence, exclusive of Certificates, has been such as in most cases to satisfy me, that the Applicants were real
Americans, who have been forced into the British Service, and who with singular constancy have generally persevered in
refusing pay and bounty, though in some instances they have been in service more than two years – As the applications for my
aid, seemed to increase after the suspension of the Consular Power to grant Protections (owing to the exposed situation of
our Seamen in consequence of the denial of this Power) I judged it adviseable, though I saw little prospect of any permanent
Agreement, to attempt to obtain the consent of this Government, that under certain regulations, our Consuls should again be
[so?] authorized to grant Certificates of Citizenship to our Seamen; my Letter to Lord Grenville and his answer you have
inclosed: ⟧ the answer is in several respects one that you should speedily possess; I wish it had been received and sent
before the adjournment of Congress – ⟦ I likewise send you the Copy of another Letter, to which I have received no answer,
that I wrote to Lord Grenville in order to expose the inconsistency with the Laws and Principles of British Allegiance of a
Rule by which acknowledged Americans are detained in the British Service. – ⟧

West Indies

No immediate arrangements on the Subject of our commerce with the British West Indies is likely to take place, – – latterly
I have said nothing respecting it: and a Correspondent silence has been observed on the other side. –

Commercial Regulations

A Bill it is understood is to be brought into Parliament this Session making certain Regulations conformable to the
Provisions of our Treaty of Commerce: I have reason to think that no Countervailing Duties will be laid upon our Trade, and am
in Hopes that I shall be able to satisfy this Government that they cannot in good Policy. nor in my belief of right, impose
any additional Tonnage upon our Ships – Lord Grenville has promised to send me the Draft of the Bill as soon as it is prepared
and before it shall be laid before Parliament in order that its provision[s] should be made to Correspond with a mutual
Interpretation of the Treaty) – I shall on this occasion have an opportunity of paying attention to certain points that are
mentioned in my instructions. – – Your Letter in Cipher of the 15. of February has come to hand and I will on all occasions
keep in mind the Subject that it communicates. –

Though I have never been satisfied with the appointment of Foreigners to the Office of Consuls, and tho' my observations here
have rather strengthened my aversion to such appointments since neither the Courts of Law nor the executive consider a Consul
who is a British Subject exempt from any of the Duties of a Subject or in any degree discharged from his allegiance, still I
continue to send you applications that are made through me to the – President upon this Subject – Mr. Ramsay is undoubtedly a
Merchant of as much reputation as any of the Foreigners who have been appointed to the Consular Office, and the Testimonials
that have been sent to me and which I remit to you are in this respect perfectly satisfactory. –

General Pinckney still remains at Amsterdam; the several copies of your Letter to him were forwarded immediately after they
came to hand; previous to which I had received and sent him some American News Papers in which this important Letter was
published. –

As the General's Letters give you exact Reports of the Capture of our Vessels, as well as of the absurd reasons, assigned by
the French Government, in vindication of their conduct, I think it unnecessary to say any thing on this afflicting Subject; –
the news Papers which I send you will give you an account of the recent Victories of Buona Parte over the Arch Duke – these
Triumphs were quite unexpected by this Government, and apprehensions exist, lest the Emperor should be obliged to make a
separate peace, as the only means of saving his capital – Mr. Hammond departed this morning for Hamburgh on his way to Vienna,
to assist on the part of England in a new, and I believe sincere Endeavour to conclude a General Peace. –

Whatever may have been the sincere and secret wishes of this Government concerning the result of Lord Malmesbury's mission;
the Events that have since happened, as well in Great Britain, as upon the Continent leave no Doubt of the anxious and very
general desire of this Nation to see an end of the War: while on the other side it appears equally plain, that France
steadily pursuing her plan of detaching her Enemies one after another, is at this time employing negotiation as well as arms
to induce the Emperor to form a separate Peace, and thus to leave England single handed in the prosecution of the War with
France: – an Event in the present temper of the Parties, that is repugnant to the Principles of humanity; as well as fatal to
the Hopes that we and others must cherish, that neither France, nor any other Nation should acquire such a decided
superiority, as to be able, in contempt of Justice, to give the Law to the rest of the World. – – The internal situation of
this Country remains as it has been for some weeks past; – the vigorous measures adopted by the Government seem at present to
have restored Quiet to the North of Ireland – the suspension of specie – Payments by the Banks of England, Scotland & Ireland
still continue; tho' it is certain that large remittances in actual specie have lately been made through Hamburgh to this
Country: our Stocks, which experienced a momentary Rise, have again fallen to nearly the same prices at which they stood
before the alarm that prevailed on the first stoppage of the Bank. –

In one of the [fall?] Ships I received under cover from you a Letter from Mr. Elliot, containing an order for a Telescope
which Mr. Elliot informed me he wanted for the public Service: I have accordingly sent the Instrument by the William Penn,
addressed to the care of John Vaughan; it cost Twenty four Guineas, which I have charged in my public account.

With great respect – I have the Honor to remain, Dear Sir, – Yr: ob. & faithful Servt.

Rufus King

Secretary of State &c. &c. &c.
