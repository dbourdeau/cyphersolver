# Gifford to Wilsdon, Paris, 26 October 1587

Did the English ambassador in Paris urge someone to kill Elizabeth I or to serve her? Two calendars of the same letter give those opposing readings. The letter bears the name Francis Hartley and is endorsed to Monsieur Wilsdon. Editors identify them as Gilbert Gifford and Thomas Phelippes. Gifford had acted as an intermediary in the correspondence surrounding the Babington plot the previous year. The letter reports an approach through Lilly. Pollen also entertained the possibility that Lilly fabricated the letter. Its claim about the ambassador therefore needs to be distinguished from the question of what the writer put in cipher. [Pollen, pp. 124–126](https://archive.org/details/maryqueenofscots00polluoft/page/n340/mode/2up) · [Foreign Calendar, January appendix](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/cal-state-papers/foreign/vol21/no1/pp661-671)

## Where to check the manuscript

The National Archives, SP 15/30, old no. 55.VII. Gale’s State Papers Online record starts at stamped folio 112, with a numbered list. The letter itself is on 114, immediately after a worksheet on 113. Select these leaves within the same record. The shelfmark also works for consultation at Kew. [Gale record MC4304880703](https://link.gale.com/apps/doc/MC4304880703/SPOL) · [Kew catalogue](https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C3411826)

1. On f. 114 find the line containing *exhorted greatlie*. Two readers independently examined masked excerpts of two signs in this line before seeing the worksheet or a proposed reading. They described an H-shaped sign with a raised mark, then a curved 2-like sign with a separate raised mark. [Who read, on which model, when, and what of their work is preserved](READERS.md)
2. On f. 113 find the *serve* entry in the word list. Two rows above is an unraised H-like sign with a gloss beginning *part*. Shown these rows separately, both readers read the gloss beside the rounded sign as *serve* (the letterforms also allow the spelling *serue*). Both noted the mark above and to the right. Neither could settle a possible trailing stroke from the cropped view.
3. Compare the second sign on f. 114 with the worksheet entry. The worksheet sign has a more open, U-shaped base. Its form and raised mark support the verb in Lomas’s edition. The match is a visual judgment. The H on the worksheet lacks the mark borne by the first sign in the letter, and the person exhorted remains uncertain. The arrangement of the leaves does not establish who prepared the worksheet or whether Lomas used it.

Satoshi Tomokiyo’s page on ciphers in the reign of Elizabeth I also lists this letter, under “Figure Cipher”, “Use of Figures in England”. Citing the Foreign Calendar, it describes a cipher that “for the most part consists of figures which run on without a break” and so could not be deciphered completely, in a letter from Francis Hartley (Gifford) to M. Wilsdon (Phelippes) enclosed with Stafford’s January 1588 letter to Walsingham. It does not discuss the disputed verb or the worksheet. [Tomokiyo, Ciphers during the Reign of Queen Elizabeth I](https://cryptiana.web.fc2.com/code/elizabeth.htm), read 22 November 2026

Both verb readings were previously published. This comparison supplies a manuscript reference and a reason to prefer *serve*. It contributes zero newly deciphered words or key values. The two signs do not support a statistical key test. No shuffled-key trial was run.
