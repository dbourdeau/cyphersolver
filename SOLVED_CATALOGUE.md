# What has been solved or read, and how

Compiled 17 Sept 2026 from the README result tables, SOLVED_RANKING.md, the per-target NOTES and the session memory.
Committed 17 Sept 2026 and linked from README.md and the site page docs/solved.html; audited the same day against the per-target NOTES (Armstrong count corrected). The "where the AI did the work" column is a judgement by the assistant
about its own contribution and is marked * throughout: the notes record what was done, not who did which step
first, and Daniel should correct it where the memory of the session differs.

The "Solved" and "Read" columns give the day the finding landed in this repository, from the git history;
"Date" is the date of the document itself. The site publishes the same dates on every write-up.

Three kinds of item are counted here: **Solved** (system recovered or table applied, text read end to end or nearly),
**Read** (text recovered by a route other than cryptanalysis of the system, such as a printed sibling or a minute),
and **Partly read** (key recovered in part, long stretches read, remainder open). Items that turned out to be
already solved by others, items explained as non-ciphers, and negatives are listed at the end for completeness
but are not "solved" in the sense of the title.

## 5. Where the model was strongest, across the set*

These are patterns over the 18 items above, not measurements.

- **Reading the catalogue and the leaf as a historian would.** Six of the items were unlocked by noticing something in the archival material rather than by a solver: the Béthune minute described as a "copie", the Mendoza fair copy mistaken for a second cipher, the Du Bellay years hidden by the notice, the Ormonde news sitting in Knowler 1739, the Feuquières sibling in the 1819 Mémoires, the Bazeries table never applied to the printed Catinat despatches. In each case the model knew the printed edition existed or recognised a clerk's working habit.
- **Structural hypothesis before search.** Ségur (mod-5 test), Warsaw (plain-order alphabet), Lucca (polyphony), Huang Xing (kana rows as digits), Urquhart (page index rule). The solvers were written to the hypothesis; blind annealers on the same data generally failed their own controls (see Ottobon, Birago, Bordeaux, Joyeuse).
- **Period language as a language model.** Reading 16th–17th-century French, Italian, Spanish, Scots and Chinese telegraph-code output to judge candidate plaintexts, and adjusting the model to period spelling (Forster: *u* for *v*, *i* for *j*).
- **Purpose-built small solvers plus matched controls.** Every solve and every negative in the repo was run against a control of the same length and design, so a failure says something about the method, not just the target. This discipline is what let 30-odd items be closed honestly and kept the Armstrong pencil-frame and SP 53 same-key claims from standing once retested.
- **Fetching from awkward archives.** JACAR raw-PDF path, Gallica IIIF, the HCPortal API photographs, MDZ hOCR, NARA microfilm frames, archive.org OCR of 1739 and 1688 editions. Much of the reading depended on getting an image or text that the lists' authors never had.

## 6. Where it was weakest*

- **Transcription from images.** Ottobon at 70 % per token, the Mondoucet aligners at baseline, Béthune needing a 4× re-transcription, Tomokiyo's normalised strings hiding glyph variants. Nearly every partial reading stops at a transcription problem, not a cryptanalytic one.
- **Blind homophonic and syllabic solving on short texts.** The annealers fail their controls below roughly 500 tokens at 130+ symbols, and word nomenclators are out of reach without a crib. Colbert, Chaulnes, Joyeuse, Birago and SP 53 all stop there.
- **Over-claiming, caught by controls.** The Armstrong pencil score, the SP 53 same-key inference and the Urquhart distich each looked good until the control was run. The record is honest because the controls were run, not because the first reading was right.

The cipher is that of the sibling record R1874 (Mocenigo, Madrid 1628), which is imaged together with the chancery's own decipherment; transcribing that decipherment (~4,800 letters) and aligning it to its cipher by soft-EM rebuilt the key — a syllabary grid, prefix = vowel (6 a, 51 e, 62 i, 53 o, 64 u), last two digits = consonant — which reads this despatch unchanged. 1,280 groups transcribed here from the images; 99.1% valued, 0.95 of the text reads as sense (0.92 strict window, 0.96 hand audit). **Unknown author → unknown recipient, "L'Estat du Roy de Navarre et de son party en France", after 1580**, BL, Harley MS 1582 ff. 263–264, DECODE R8505, catalogue 121 (read 22 Sept 2026): **cipher broken here, read (95.1%)**. Catalogued as four pages of unknown graphic signs by an unknown sender, language "English?". Identified from its clear text as a French memoir on Henri de Navarre's party, and deciphered from the ciphertext alone: a homophonic alphabet of about fifty graphic signs in which **the letter m is written as a group of two, three or four dots** — read as punctuation by every earlier pass, which is why no key fitted. A control experiment on synthetic text located the fault in the solver rather than the manuscript. The cipher carries the Spanish drift: the prince of Parme "pour lui rendre les places", "un party de malcontens", an "italien qui sert d'ingenieur a Lisbone", "une armee de reistres", and the Languedoc places — Beaucaire sur le Rosne, Béziers, Carcassonne — held "a la devotion du Roy de Navarre". Ten nomenclator numbers stay open, though 72 is a country and almost certainly France, and 81 a person, probably the King. `harley1582r8505/`
